# Maintenance and operating practice for construction plant

**Summary.** Plant fails from neglect far more often than from wear. This file sets out the maintenance regimes that actually keep machines running — daily walkarounds, hour-meter-based service intervals, fluid and filter management, tyre and undercarriage care — plus the records, competence requirements and risk assessment that make the fleet legally defensible, and a maintenance log template ready to use.

## Key facts

| Item | Value | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Typical engine oil interval, modern diesel plant | **250–500 h** | manufacturer-specific; extend only on oil analysis evidence |
| Typical hydraulic oil interval | **2 000–5 000 h**, filters at 500–1 000 h | contamination-driven, not time-driven |
| Typical final drive / swing gear oil | **1 000–2 000 h** | check level at 250 h |
| Coolant (extended-life, OAT) | **6 000 h / 6 years** with an extender at mid-life | conventional coolant: 2 000 h / 1 year |
| Air filter | **by restriction indicator**, not by hours | a filter changed early wastes money; changed late destroys engines |
| Fuel filter | **500 h** typical; halve in dusty/dirty fuel conditions | **[NA]** halve again on bunkered rural fuel |
| Undercarriage cost | **~50% of an excavator's lifetime maintenance cost** | the single largest controllable maintenance item |
| Track tension check | **daily/weekly**, per the manual's sag measurement | over-tight tracks are the commonest cause of premature undercarriage wear |
| Oil analysis interval | every oil change, or **250 h** on critical machines | trending matters more than any single result |
| Availability target, well-run fleet | **85–92% mechanical availability** | below 80% signals a maintenance or fuel-quality problem |
| **[ZA]** Statutory basis | OHS Act 85/1993 — **Construction Regulations 2014**, **Driven Machinery Regulations 2015**, **General Machinery Regulations** | inspection registers and appointed competent persons are legal requirements |

> ⚠️ **[ZA]** The Driven Machinery Regulations require that lifting machinery and lifting tackle are inspected and, where applicable, load-tested by a registered **Lifting Machinery Inspector**, with results entered in a register kept on the premises. Failure to keep the register is itself an offence, independent of whether anything failed.

## The maintenance regime, by interval

### Daily — the pre-start walkaround (10–15 minutes)
Done by the operator, before the machine is started, every shift, and **recorded**.

1. **Walk the machine.** Look underneath for fresh leaks (note the colour: black = engine oil, red/amber = hydraulic or transmission, green/pink = coolant, clear = fuel or water).
2. **Fluid levels** — engine oil, coolant (cold, never open a hot cap), hydraulic tank sight glass, fuel, DEF/AdBlue where fitted, windscreen wash.
3. **Air filter restriction indicator** and pre-cleaner bowl.
4. **Belts and hoses** — cracking, chafing, weeping fittings.
5. **Undercarriage or tyres** — track sag/tension, missing bolts, damaged shoes; tyre pressures, cuts, sidewall damage, wheel-nut torque marks.
6. **Attachment and pins** — quick-hitch locked and secured, pins and retainers present, bucket teeth and adapters.
7. **Structure** — cracks at boom/stick weld toes, cab mounts, ROPS/FOPS integrity, mirrors, glass, steps and handholds.
8. **Safety systems** — seatbelt, horn, lights, beacon, reversing alarm, fire extinguisher, isolator, emergency stop, camera/mirror coverage.
9. **Greasing** per the lube chart — most excavator front-end pins are daily or every 10 h.
10. **Start and check** — warning lights extinguish, no unusual noise, gauges settle, hydraulics respond, brakes and steering function, no smoke beyond a normal start.

Any defect goes on a **defect report**, and a defect affecting safety takes the machine out of service until cleared. This is the mechanism that stops small failures becoming large ones.

### Weekly / 50 hours
Full grease per the chart including slew ring and swing bearing; battery terminals and electrolyte; radiator and cooler core cleaning (blow out from the fan side — **[NA]** in dusty conditions this is often a *daily* task and is the single most common cause of overheating); track tension measurement and adjustment; tyre pressures against the loaded specification; fire extinguisher and first aid kit check; wash-down.

### 250 hours
Engine oil and filter (most machines); fuel filters and water separator drain; check final drive and swing gear oil levels; hydraulic return filter on some machines; belt tension; air-conditioning filter; a torque check on critical fasteners (track shoe bolts, cutting edge bolts, counterweight bolts, wheel nuts); **oil sample for analysis**.

### 500 hours
Hydraulic filters; fuel filters; air filter primary element (if not already done on restriction); transmission/torque converter oil and filter on wheeled machines; battery and charging system test; brake system check; a structural inspection with a torch and a wire brush at known crack-prone areas.

### 1 000 hours
Final drive and swing gear oil change; hydraulic tank breather; valve clearance adjustment (many engines); alternator and starter inspection; cooling system pressure test; a full attachment pin and bush wear measurement; ROPS/FOPS and cab mount inspection.

### 2 000 hours and beyond
Hydraulic oil change (or on analysis); coolant change (conventional) or extender addition (extended-life); injector inspection or replacement; turbocharger inspection; a full undercarriage wear measurement with a gauge set; engine and hydraulic performance test against factory figures. This is the interval at which the **rebuild-or-replace** decision is normally reviewed.

> ⚠️ Every interval above is a **typical** value. The manufacturer's operation and maintenance manual (OMM) is the governing document, and it varies by model, by application severity, and by fuel and oil quality. Get the OMM from the portals in file 09 and follow it.

## Fluids and filters

### Getting oil specification right
Engine oil is specified by **viscosity grade (SAE)** and **performance category (API/ACEA)**. A modern low-emissions engine typically needs API CK-4 / FA-4 or ACEA E6/E9; using an older CI-4 oil in a DPF-equipped engine will ash-load the filter. **[NA] [ZA]** In hot ambients, a 15W-40 is the general workhorse; 10W-30 or a synthetic 5W-40 improves cold starts and marginally improves fuel consumption. Never mix a low-SAPS oil with a conventional one in an aftertreatment-equipped engine.

Hydraulic oil is specified by **ISO VG grade** (VG 46 is the general standard; VG 68 in sustained high ambient temperatures) and by **cleanliness code (ISO 4406)**, typically **18/16/13 or cleaner** for modern piston pumps. Most hydraulic failures are contamination failures.

### Filtration discipline
- **Filter before it enters the machine.** New oil from a drum is typically ISO 20/18/15 — dirtier than the system requires. Use a filter cart.
- Keep drums **sealed, indoors and horizontal with the bungs at 3 and 9 o'clock** if they must be outside; a vertical drum breathes water in through the bung as it heats and cools.
- **Colour-code and label** every fill point and every container. Cross-contamination of hydraulic oil with diesel or coolant is a fleet-wide event.
- Use **genuine or verified-equivalent filters**. Counterfeit filters are widespread in the region and typically have the wrong media, no bypass valve and no anti-drainback valve.
- **Cut open** a used filter occasionally and look at what it caught. It is the cheapest condition monitoring there is.

### Oil analysis
Send a sample at every oil change on major machines, and at 250 h on critical ones. The lab reports **wear metals** (iron, chromium, copper, lead, aluminium, silicon), **contaminants** (water, fuel dilution, coolant/glycol, silicon = dirt), and **oil condition** (viscosity, TBN, oxidation, nitration). **[ZA] [NA]** WearCheck is the established regional laboratory.

What matters is the **trend**, not the absolute number. A rising iron trend on an excavator's final drive is a bearing telling you months in advance. Silicon rising means dirt is entering — check the air intake before you check anything else.

### Fuel
**[NA] [ZA]** Fuel quality is the dominant reliability variable on a remote site.
- Store in **bunded, sealed tanks**; never in open drums.
- Fit a **water separator** and drain it daily.
- Let delivered fuel **settle** before drawing.
- Dose with a **biocide** where warm and humid; diesel bug at the fuel/water interface plugs filters and corrodes tanks.
- **Filter at the dispensing nozzle** — a 5 µm dispensing filter is far cheaper than an injector set.
- Match the machine's emissions tier to the available fuel sulphur (see `00_overview.md`).

## Tyre and track care

### Tyres
- **Pressure** is the whole game: run to the manufacturer's table for the actual load and speed, checked cold. Under-inflation destroys sidewalls and doubles rolling resistance; over-inflation cuts traction and concentrates tread wear.
- **Match pairs** on driven axles — a difference in rolling radius across an axle winds up the differential and destroys it.
- **TKPH/TMPH** (tonne-kilometre per hour) limits govern on ADTs and loaders working long, hot hauls; exceeding them causes heat separation failure, which is sudden and dangerous.
- **Rock and cut damage** — maintain the haul road. Most tyre damage is a road maintenance failure, not a tyre failure.
- Inflate from behind a cage or use a clip-on chuck with an extension; a split-rim failure is lethal.

### Tracks and undercarriage
Undercarriage is roughly **half of an excavator's lifetime maintenance spend**, and most of that spend is avoidable.

- **Track tension** is the single biggest lever. Measure the sag per the OMM (typically 10–40 mm between specified rollers) with the machine on level ground in the specified position. **Too tight** dramatically accelerates pin, bush, sprocket and idler wear; too loose causes de-tracking.
- **Adjust for conditions**: run looser in mud, clay and snow that pack into the chain.
- **Clean the undercarriage daily** in sticky material — packed clay acts as a permanent over-tensioner.
- **Avoid**: travelling in one direction for long periods (wears one side of the bushings); high-speed travel over long distances; spinning tracks; turning always the same way; working on side slopes always in one orientation; travelling with the sprockets forward for long periods (increases bushing wear).
- **Measure wear** with a gauge set at 1 000–2 000 h: link height, bush OD, idler tread, roller tread, sprocket tooth profile. Plan a **turn** of pins and bushes at the correct point rather than running to destruction.
- **Rubber tracks** on mini excavators: keep off kerbs and sharp steel, avoid oil and fuel contamination, and check tension weekly. A rubber track lasts 800–1 500 h in good conditions and 300 h in bad ones.

## Hour-meter-based planning

The hour meter, not the calendar, drives plant maintenance. Build the plan as follows:

1. **Record hours daily** for every machine, from the machine's own meter, on the operator's daily sheet.
2. **Forecast** the date each machine will hit its next service by dividing the hours remaining by the machine's recent average hours per day.
3. **Batch and schedule** services into planned downtime — a weekend, a shutdown, a wet day — rather than reacting when the machine stops.
4. **Kit the service in advance**: filters, oils, seals and gaskets picked and staged before the machine arrives. **[NA]** On a remote site, hold a **service kit per machine per interval** in stock; a two-week wait for a filter is a two-week loss.
5. **Apply a severity factor.** Manufacturers publish severe-duty intervals for dusty, high-load or short-cycle applications. A machine loading trucks in a quarry in Namibia is a severe-duty machine; halve the air and fuel filter intervals as a starting point.
6. **Track cost per hour** by machine: fuel + lubricants + parts + labour + tyres/undercarriage + finance. This is the number that tells you when to replace the machine, and it is the number that most fleets never compute.
7. **Use telematics** where fitted (Cat VisionLink, Komatsu KOMTRAX, Volvo CareTrack, JCB LiveLink, Bell Fleetm@tic, Genie Lift Connect, JLG ClearSky). Machine hours, idle percentage, fuel burn, fault codes and location come automatically. **Idle percentage is the most actionable number in the whole system** — 40%+ idle is common and is pure cost.

## Plant records

Keep, per machine, a **plant file** containing:

- Identification: make, model, serial/PIN, year, registration, fleet number, and a photograph.
- Purchase or hire documentation, and the **CE/compliance and ROPS/FOPS certification**.
- The **operation and maintenance manual** and the parts book (get them from file 09).
- **Service history** — every service with date, hours, work done, parts used, and who did it.
- **Defect reports** and their close-out.
- **Statutory inspections and test certificates** — lifting machinery load tests, LMI reports, pressure vessel certificates on compressors, electrical CoC for gensets. **[ZA]**
- **Oil analysis reports**, trended.
- **Insurance** and, for road-going plant, licensing and roadworthiness.
- **Operator authorisations** for that machine class.
- **Risk assessment** for the machine and its typical task.

Retain records for the life of the machine and beyond — in an incident investigation the plant file is the primary evidence, and its absence is treated as evidence of a failed system.

## Operator competence and licensing

**[ZA]** The Construction Regulations require that the employer ensures every operator of construction vehicles and mobile plant is **competent**, **medically fit**, and **authorised in writing** for that class of machine, and that a **competent person is appointed** to supervise construction vehicles and mobile plant. Practically this means:

1. **Training** at an accredited provider — in South Africa, plant operator training accredited by the **Transport Education and Training Authority (TETA)** or the **Construction SETA (CETA)**, against registered unit standards per machine class.
2. **Assessment** and a certificate of competence naming the specific machine class (a TLB certificate does not authorise a 20 t excavator).
3. **Medical fitness** certificate, including vision and, for some classes, audiometry — repeated at the interval the occupational health practitioner sets.
4. **Written authorisation** by the employer, machine-class specific, kept on site.
5. **Refresher training** — typically every 2 years, or after an incident, a long absence, or a change of machine.
6. **[ZA]** Road-going plant additionally requires a **driving licence of the appropriate code** and, where applicable, a **Professional Driving Permit (PrDP)**.
7. **[NA]** Namibian requirements follow the Labour Act and its regulations, and SA-accredited operator certification is widely accepted in practice — but confirm the client's and the insurer's requirements in writing before mobilising.

**MEWPs** carry their own regime: the IPAF PAL card categories (1a static vertical, 1b static boom, 3a mobile vertical, 3b mobile boom) are the de facto international standard and are widely required on SADC industrial and mining sites, alongside harness and rescue training.

**Lifting** requires, additionally, appointed and competent **riggers/slingers** and **lift supervisors** — see `02_cranes-and-lifting.md`.

## Machine risk assessment

Assess per machine *and per task*, not generically. A working method:

1. **Define the task and the environment** — what the machine will do, where, on what ground, near what.
2. **Identify hazards**, at minimum: overturning and instability; contact between plant and pedestrians (the leading cause of plant fatalities); reversing and blind spots; contact with overhead power lines and buried services; falling and ejected material; unintended movement and inadequate isolation; falls from the machine; entanglement and crushing during maintenance; noise, whole-body vibration, dust and fume; fire; and manual handling during service work.
3. **Evaluate** — likelihood × severity, with honest attention to the fact that plant-pedestrian and overturn events are typically fatal.
4. **Control, in the order of the hierarchy:**
   - **Eliminate** — can the task be done without the machine, or with the machine off-site (prefabrication)?
   - **Substitute** — a smaller machine, a remote-controlled roller, a telehandler instead of a crane.
   - **Engineering** — segregation of plant and people by physical barriers and a **traffic management plan**; edge protection; reversing cameras and 360° camera systems; proximity/exclusion detection; overhead line goalposts; laser or radar people-detection.
   - **Administrative** — permits to dig and permits to lift; banksmen; exclusion zones; one-way haul routes; speed limits; competence and authorisation; pre-start checks; shift patterns that manage fatigue.
   - **PPE** — high-visibility clothing, hard hat, safety footwear, hearing protection, respiratory protection. Last, never first.
5. **Record, communicate and review** — brief the crew, keep it in the plant file, and review after any change or incident.

> ⚠️ Segregation is the control that actually works. Every serious plant injury investigation ends with the same finding: a person was where a machine was moving. Cameras, alarms and hi-vis are supplements to segregation, not substitutes for it.
