# Cranes and lifting equipment — manufacturers, load charts and lift planning

**Summary.** Lifting is the highest-consequence activity on most sites: the failure modes are sudden, and they kill. This file maps the crane, telehandler, MEWP and hoist manufacturers, explains how a load chart actually works (radius versus capacity, and the four interlocked variables), covers ground bearing pressure under outriggers and crawler tracks, sets out rigging fundamentals, and states what a lifting plan must contain.

## Key facts

| Item | Value | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Mobile crane rating margin | typically **75% of tipping load** where stability governs | 85% for crawler cranes in some standards; structural-limited entries are lower |
| Rating standards | **ISO 4305/4310**, **EN 13000** (mobile), **EN 14439** (tower), **ASME B30.5** | the chart states which |
| Radius definition | horizontal distance from **centre of rotation** to the **centre of the hook block** | measured *loaded*, i.e. with boom deflection |
| Typical mobile telescopic range | 25 t to 1 200 t | Liebherr LTM family spans this |
| Typical tower crane | 1–20 t max, 3–80 m jib | max capacity only near the mast; tip capacity is the number that matters |
| Telehandler class | 2–5 t capacity, 6–25 m lift height | rated at a stated forward reach — capacity falls fast with reach |
| MEWP working height vs platform height | working height = platform height **+ 2 m** | manufacturers quote both; do not mix them |
| Allowable soil bearing (planning only) | soft clay 75–100 kPa; firm clay 150–200 kPa; dense sand/gravel 300–500 kPa | **must** be confirmed by the geotechnical report |
| **[ZA]** Legal basis | OHS Act 85 of 1993, **Construction Regulations 2014** and Driven Machinery Regulations | lifting machinery requires competent operator and periodic load test |

> ⚠️ **[ZA]** Under the Driven Machinery Regulations, lifting machinery must be inspected and load-tested by a registered Lifting Machinery Inspector (LMI) at prescribed intervals, and the register kept on site. **[NA]** Namibian practice follows the Labour Act regulations on machinery; SA-registered LMI certification is widely accepted in practice but confirm with the client's requirements.

### Hoists and site elevators
**Alimak**, **GEDA**, **Böcker**, **Maber**, **Scanclimber** for rack-and-pinion mast climbing work platforms and passenger/goods hoists. Capacity typically 500–3 200 kg, speeds 12–90 m/min. Hoists are lifting machinery and fall under the same statutory inspection regime as cranes.

## Load charts — how to read one

A load chart is **not** a capacity. It is a matrix answering: *for this exact configuration, at this radius, what is the maximum gross load?*

### The four interlocked variables (mobile cranes)

1. **Radius** — the dominant one. Capacity falls roughly as 1/radius in the stability-governed region. A crane rated 90 t at 3 m may be rated 12 t at 20 m and 3 t at 40 m.
2. **Boom length / configuration** — a longer boom is heavier and has more deflection, so at the *same* radius a longer boom rates lower. Fly jib, luffing jib and each insert has its own chart page.
3. **Counterweight** — each counterweight combination has its own chart. A crane brought to site "without the full counterweight package" is a different crane.
4. **Outrigger spread / on-tyres** — full spread, intermediate spread, retracted, and "on tyres" (pick-and-carry) each have separate charts. On-tyres capacities are a small fraction of outrigged capacities and are usually 360°-restricted or over-rear only.

Plus **quadrant of operation**: over-rear, over-side, over-front. Over-front on a truck-mounted crane is usually the weakest.

### Deductions — what to subtract from the chart value

The chart gives **gross load at the hook**. From it deduct:
- the **hook block and ball** (0.2–3 t depending on size),
- all **rigging** — slings, shackles, spreader beam, lifting frame,
- the **stowed or erected jib** even when not in use (its weight is on the boom head),
- **auxiliary hoist rope** weight on long booms,
- any **load-handling device** (vacuum lifter, clamp, magnet).

The remainder is the **net load** you may lift. Deductions of 5–15% of chart are routine; on long-boom fine lifts they can exceed 25%.

### Tower crane charts

A tower crane chart is simpler: **jib radius versus capacity**, with a **maximum capacity** near the mast and a **tip capacity** at full jib. The two reeving modes matter: **2-fall** (faster, lower capacity) and **4-fall** (double capacity, half the speed). The number the site actually lives with is **tip capacity in 2-fall** — that is the load the crane can pick at the far corner of the building at working speed.

### Crawler crane charts
Crawler cranes are rated with the tracks at a stated width, on level, firm ground, and may permit travel with load (a distinctive and valuable capability). Duty charts differentiate **freely suspended** loads from loads restrained by the machine.

## Ground bearing pressure

The most common cause of mobile crane overturn is not overload — it is outrigger punch-through.

**Outrigger loads** are not the crane's weight ÷ 4. In the worst quadrant, one outrigger can carry **50–70% of the total (crane + counterweight + load)**. The crane manufacturer publishes outrigger load tables or the on-board computer displays them; use those.

**Pad sizing.** Required pad area = outrigger load ÷ allowable bearing pressure, with a safety factor. A 60 t outrigger load on ground with 150 kPa allowable needs 60 000 × 9.81 / 150 000 ≈ **3.9 m²**, i.e. a 2.0 × 2.0 m mat. Timber crane mats, steel spreader plates or engineered composite pads all work; the load must be able to spread through the pad at roughly 45°, so a thin plate on soft ground does very little.

**Crawler cranes** distribute over the track contact area, typically **80–250 kPa** depending on machine and load — often more than outriggers per unit area but over a much larger footprint. Travelling with load shifts pressure to the leading rollers and can double local pressure.

> ⚠️ Never set outriggers over a service trench, a manhole, a basement slab, a retaining wall zone of influence, or backfill that has not been proven. Ask for the as-built services drawing, then prove it with a trial pit or a CAT scan.

## Rigging basics

- **Sling angle destroys capacity.** A two-leg sling at 60° included angle carries ~1.16× the vertical load per leg; at 120° it carries **2× per leg**. Below 30° included angle is prohibited in most codes.
- **Sling types:** chain (grade 80/100, heat resistant, damage-visible), wire rope (durable, poor around sharp edges), round slings and webbing (kind to finished surfaces, easily cut). Every sling needs a legible tag with WLL, and a colour code by tonnage on round slings.
- **Shackles:** bow (anchor) shackles for multi-leg, dee shackles for in-line. Never side-load a dee shackle; never replace a shackle pin with a bolt.
- **Edge protection** is mandatory anywhere a soft sling passes a corner.
- **Centre of gravity.** The load's CoG must lie under the hook, or the load will swing violently on pick-up. Pre-calculate for anything asymmetric.
- **Tag lines** for anything with a face area exceeding roughly 1 m², and always in wind.
- **Wind.** Most cranes stop at **9–14 m/s (roughly 20–30 mph)** for standard loads; large-area loads (cladding panels, formwork tables, tank sections) may have limits below 7 m/s. The manufacturer's chart states the maximum permissible wind speed and the reference area — exceeding the area invalidates the chart even below the wind limit.

## Lifting plan requirements

Every lift needs a plan; the depth scales with risk. Classify lifts as **routine**, **complicated** or **critical** (tandem lifts, blind lifts, lifts over live areas, lifts >75–90% of chart, personnel lifting, or anything unusual).

A lift plan should contain:

1. **The load** — description, verified mass (from drawing/weighbridge, not estimate), dimensions, CoG, lifting points and their capacity.
2. **The crane** — make, model, configuration (boom length, jib, counterweight, outrigger spread), the specific chart page used, chart capacity at the required radius, and the utilisation percentage after deductions.
3. **Geometry** — pick radius, set radius, lift height, slew arc, and clearances to structures and overhead lines.
4. **Ground** — bearing capacity source, outrigger loads, pad sizes and material, and confirmation of no voids/services below.
5. **Rigging** — sling type, length, WLL, angle, shackles, spreader beam, edge protection, total rigging mass.
6. **Personnel** — appointed lift supervisor, appointed rigger/slinger, banksman/signaller, crane operator with competence evidence.
7. **Communication** — radio channel, hand signals, one signaller only.
8. **Environment** — wind limits, visibility, exclusion zone, public interface.
9. **Contingency** — what happens if the lift must be aborted mid-cycle; where the load can be safely set down.

**[ZA]** Under the Construction Regulations, the principal contractor must appoint a competent person for lifting operations, and lifting machinery must carry a valid load test certificate. Keep the plan, the certificate and the operator's competence record together in the site lifting file.
