# HR in construction — the lifecycle and why it differs

**Summary.** Construction HR is the ordinary employment lifecycle — attract, contract, induct, pay, develop, manage, discipline, exit — compressed and repeated at project tempo, on a workforce that is mostly manual, frequently mobile, often semi-literate, and always exposed to physical risk. The two operative legal frameworks in this knowledge base are Namibia's **Labour Act 11 of 2007** and South Africa's **Basic Conditions of Employment Act 75 of 1997** with the **Labour Relations Act 66 of 1995**. Both are protective statutes: a fixed-term or "project" label does not remove an employee's rights, and neither country recognises "no work, no pay, no rights" casual labour as a category outside the Act. This folder covers the whole cycle; files 01 and 02 hold the statutory detail, and files 03–10 are the operating practice.

## Key facts

| Dimension | Ordinary employer | Construction employer |
|---|---|---|
| Workplace | Fixed, employer-controlled | Moves; often the **client's** property; multiple employers on one site |
| Contract horizon | Indefinite | Tied to a contract, phase, or "completion of the works" |
| Turnover | Managed attrition | Structural — the job legitimately ends |
| Workforce mix | Broadly uniform skill band | Labourer to professional on one payroll |
| Supervisory chain | Line management | Statutory **appointments** in writing with competence requirements **[ZA]** reg 8, Construction Regulations 2014 |
| Pay structure | Salary | Hourly, daily, task/piece, plus site allowances |
| Third-party labour | Rare | Subcontractors, labour-only teams, labour brokers as the norm |
| Sectoral bargaining | Uncommon | **[ZA]** BCCEI and building-industry councils can bind non-parties |
| Injury exposure | Low | High — statutory compensation cover is a precondition to being on site |

> ⚠️ Nothing in this folder is legal advice. Wage rates, levy percentages and thresholds change by gazette or collective agreement; verify every figure against the current Government Gazette (Namibia), the Department of Employment and Labour (South Africa), or the relevant bargaining council before relying on it.

## 1. The construction HR lifecycle

The cycle below is the spine of this folder. Each stage maps to a file.

1. **Workforce planning** — read the programme, not the org chart. Labour demand in construction is a curve, not a headcount: groundworks needs plant operators and labourers, superstructure needs bricklayers and steel fixers, finishes needs plasterers, tilers, painters and glaziers. Plan recruitment against the critical path (`02_building_construction/12_programme-and-site-management.md`).
2. **Recruitment and selection** — file `03`. Trade testing matters more than CVs; credentials must be verified against the issuing authority.
3. **Contracting and onboarding** — file `04`. Written particulars are a statutory duty in both countries. Induction is simultaneously an HR and an OHS obligation.
4. **Pay and benefits** — file `05`. Hourly and task rates, allowances, statutory deductions, payslips, and the logistics of paying people who have no bank branch within 100 km.
5. **Skills development** — file `06`. Apprenticeships, learnerships, the VET levy **[NA]** and the Skills Development Levy **[ZA]**, toolbox talks, competency matrices.
6. **Performance, discipline and dismissal** — file `07`. The defensible process: substantive and procedural fairness.
7. **Health, safety and wellbeing** — file `08`. Statutory appointments, medical fitness, injury-on-duty reporting, substance abuse, HIV, heat, fatigue, mental health.
8. **Third-party labour** — file `09`. Subcontractors, labour-only teams, labour brokers, joint liability.
9. **Exit** — certificate of service **[ZA]** s.42 BCEA; final payment including accrued leave **[NA]** s.37 Labour Act; severance where it is triggered.

## 2. Why construction is different — seven structural facts

### 2.1 The work is project-based, but the employment often is not

A builder's instinct is to hire "for the job". Both statutes push back. **[ZA]** the LRA regulates fixed-term contracts for employees earning below the earnings threshold (s.198B): a fixed term longer than three months is only justifiable on defined grounds, one of which is work of a **limited and definite duration** — which genuine project work usually is — but the employer must be able to prove it, and the employee is otherwise deemed indefinitely employed. **[NA]** the Labour Act does not cap fixed-term contracts in the same way, but a contract that is renewed repeatedly on the same terms invites an argument that the employee had a reasonable expectation of continuation, and the s.33 fairness test still applies to the ending of it.

The practical rule: **tie the contract to an identifiable, documented project or phase**, describe the completing event in the contract itself, and do not roll the same person from project to project on fresh "first" fixed terms.

### 2.2 Turnover is structural, so the administrative cost of each hire is high

A firm running six-month contracts will hire, induct, pay, and exit the same number of people two to four times a year that a factory hires once. Every one of those cycles must generate: a signed contract, an induction record, a medical certificate of fitness **[ZA]**, a compensation-fund registration, a leave record, a payroll record retained for the statutory period, and an exit document. The only way a small firm survives this is **template discipline** — file `10`.

### 2.3 Skill levels are mixed and the pay structure has to carry it

On a modest house build you will have a contracts manager on a monthly salary, a foreman on a monthly or weekly wage, artisans on an hourly rate, and general labourers possibly on a task rate — with the national minimum wage as the floor under all of them. See file `05`. Mixed structures are lawful; **mixed record-keeping is not** — hours worked, overtime and remuneration must be recorded for every employee regardless of how they are paid.

### 2.4 Subcontracting is the default operating model

Most southern African contractors subcontract at least the specialist trades — electrical, plumbing, roofing, glazing, tiling. This creates three HR exposures at once:
- **Misclassification.** A "subcontractor" who works only for you, under your supervision, with your tools, on your hours, is an employee in substance. Both countries apply a substance-over-form test with statutory presumptions (**[ZA]** s.200A LRA and s.83A BCEA; **[NA]** s.128A Labour Act, inserted by the Labour Amendment Act 2 of 2012 — *verify the section number against the current consolidation*).
- **Derivative liability.** Site safety duties attach to the principal contractor regardless of who employs the injured person **[ZA]** reg 7 Construction Regulations 2014.
- **Bargaining council extension.** **[ZA]** where a collective agreement has been extended to non-parties, a subcontractor operating in the registered scope is bound by it whether or not they are a party.

File `09` is the compliance checklist.

### 2.5 The site is a regulated workplace with named humans attached to it

Construction is the only sector where HR must produce **people with names and written appointments** to satisfy a safety regulator. **[ZA]** the principal contractor must appoint a full-time competent construction manager in writing, with an alternate, and a construction health and safety officer after consultation with the client (reg 8), plus competent persons for risk assessment (reg 9), fall protection (reg 10), excavation (reg 13) and scaffolding (reg 16). **[NA]** GN 156 of 1997 under the Labour Act requires the appointment of supervisors (reg 4) and safety officer(s) (reg 6) and the election of workplace safety representatives. These are recruitment specifications, training obligations and succession risks — not just safety paperwork.

### 2.6 Physical risk converts HR failures into injuries and claims

An untrained new starter placed at a fall-risk position is a wrongful-death claim, not a performance problem. Induction, competence verification, medical fitness and toolbox talks are the HR controls that stand between a hire and a fatality. Both countries operate no-fault compensation funds that also strip the employee of a common-law damages claim against the employer in exchange **[ZA]** s.35 COIDA — but that protection collapses where the employer was not registered and in good standing.

### 2.7 Remote and rural sites change every practicality

Paying, feeding, housing, transporting and disciplining a workforce 400 km from the office is a different problem from doing it in Windhoek or Johannesburg. **[ZA]** reg 30 Construction Regulations requires the contractor to provide reasonable and suitable **living accommodation** for workers far removed from their homes where transport or other accommodation is not available. Accommodation, transport and subsistence become contractual terms, cost lines, and — if handled badly — disputes.

## 3. What "good" looks like for a small builder

A five-to-forty-person contractor in Namibia or South Africa can be fully compliant with a modest system:

- One **contract template** per employment type, reviewed once by a labour lawyer, then reused.
- A **personnel file index** (file `10`) applied identically to every employee.
- A **time record** kept daily by the foreman, signed weekly — this single document defeats most overtime and unfair-dismissal disputes.
- A **competency matrix** on the site office wall showing who is verified for what.
- A **disciplinary code** issued at induction and applied consistently.
- **Registration and good standing** with: the Social Security Commission and the Employees' Compensation Fund **[NA]**; the Compensation Fund, SARS for PAYE/UIF/SDL, and the relevant bargaining council **[ZA]**.
- A **toolbox talk register** — it is training evidence, safety evidence and, when someone ignores an instruction, disciplinary evidence.
