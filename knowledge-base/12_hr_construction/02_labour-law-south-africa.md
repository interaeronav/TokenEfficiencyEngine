# South African labour law for construction employers

**Summary.** South African construction employment sits on four statutes — the **Basic Conditions of Employment Act 75 of 1997 (BCEA)** for minimum terms, the **Labour Relations Act 66 of 1995 (LRA)** for dismissal, bargaining and the CCMA, the **Employment Equity Act 55 of 1998 (EEA)** for equity and reporting, and the **Compensation for Occupational Injuries and Diseases Act 130 of 1993 (COIDA)** for injury on duty — plus the **National Minimum Wage Act 9 of 2018** and the **Skills Development Act 97 of 1998** with its levy Act. On top of that, most civil engineering and much building work is covered by a **bargaining council** whose main agreement can be extended to non-parties, overriding BCEA minima with higher industry rates.

> ⚠️ Legally consequential. Section numbers below are verified against the published section index of each Act. **Rand amounts, levy percentages, earnings thresholds and bargaining council wage schedules change annually and were not verified in this pass** — confirm every figure with the Department of Employment and Labour, SARS, or the relevant council before use.

## 1. BCEA — the floor

The BCEA sets minimum terms that cannot be contracted below, except upward. Several provisions (hours, overtime, Sunday, night work, meal intervals, rest periods) do **not** apply to senior managerial employees, sales staff who travel, and employees working fewer than 24 hours a month — and do not apply above the **earnings threshold** determined by the Minister (an annual rand figure; *not verified here*). Every general worker, artisan and foreman on a construction site is below that threshold.

**Ordinary hours (s.9)** are 45 per week. **Overtime (s.10)** must be by agreement, is capped at 10 hours a week (extendable to 15 by collective agreement for a limited period), and is paid at one and a half times. A **compressed working week (s.11)** permits up to 12 hours a day without overtime pay, provided the weekly and annual ceilings hold — useful on remote sites running four long days. **Averaging (s.12)** requires a collective agreement.

**Sunday work (s.16)** is double time, or 1.5 × for an employee who ordinarily works Sundays. **Public holidays (s.18)** are paid days off; work on one is paid at double, or normal pay plus the hours worked, whichever is greater. There are twelve gazetted public holidays; where one falls on a Sunday, the Monday is a holiday.

**Annual leave (s.20)** is 21 consecutive days on full pay per annual leave cycle — 15 working days for a five-day worker, 18 for a six-day worker. Leave must be granted no later than six months after the end of the cycle and may not be paid out except on termination.

**Sick leave (s.22)** runs on a 36-month cycle: the employee is entitled to the number of days they would normally work in a six-week period. In the first six months, accrual is one day per 26 days worked. Section 24 disapplies paid sick leave where the incapacity is covered by COIDA.

**Maternity (s.25)** is four consecutive months, unpaid by the employer but claimable from UIF; the employee may not start work for six weeks after the birth without a medical certificate. The **Code of Good Practice on the Protection of Employees during Pregnancy and after the Birth of a Child** requires arrangements for breastfeeding employees to have **breaks of 30 minutes twice per day** for the first six months of the child's life, and requires the employer to assess and remove hazards — directly relevant on sites with lead paint, solvents, silica dust, vibration and manual handling. **Parental leave (s.25A)** is ten consecutive days.

**Deductions (s.34)** for loss or damage are permitted only where the loss occurred in the course of employment and was due to the employee's fault, the employee has been given a fair opportunity to show why the deduction should not be made, the total does not exceed the actual loss, and the deduction does not exceed one quarter of remuneration. **s.33A** prohibits an employer from requiring an employee to pay for training, PPE, or the employment itself.

## 2. National Minimum Wage Act 9 of 2018

A single national floor per **ordinary hour worked**, adjusted annually by gazette on the recommendation of the National Minimum Wage Commission, effective from 1 March each year. It applies to all workers including construction, with separate rates historically applied to Expanded Public Works Programme workers and learners on learnerships. An employer may apply for exemption. **The current rand rate could not be verified in this pass** — treat as `needs-verification` and check the Department of Employment and Labour gazette for the rate effective 1 March of the current year.

Where a bargaining council wage schedule sets a higher rate, the higher rate applies.

## 3. LRA — dismissal, the CCMA and Schedule 8

### 3.1 The right and the categories

**s.185** gives every employee the right not to be unfairly dismissed or subjected to an unfair labour practice. **s.186** defines dismissal — including the non-renewal of a fixed-term contract where the employee reasonably expected renewal, and constructive dismissal. **s.187** lists **automatically unfair** dismissals: for union membership or activity, for a protected strike, for pregnancy, for refusing to do the work of a striker, for a transfer under s.197, for a protected disclosure, and on discriminatory grounds. **s.188** requires the employer to prove a fair reason relating to **conduct, capacity, or the operational requirements** of the employer, and a fair procedure.

**s.192** places the onus: the employee proves there was a dismissal; the employer proves it was fair. **s.193** sets remedies — reinstatement, re-employment, or compensation — with reinstatement the primary remedy for a substantively unfair dismissal. **s.194** caps compensation at 12 months' remuneration for ordinary unfair dismissal and 24 months for automatically unfair dismissal.

### 3.2 Schedule 8 — Code of Good Practice: Dismissal

Schedule 8 is the practical standard every chairperson and every commissioner works from. Its core propositions:

- Discipline is **corrective and progressive**, not punitive. Counselling and warnings precede dismissal except for serious misconduct.
- Dismissal for a **first offence** is reserved for gross misconduct — gross dishonesty, wilful damage to property, wilful endangering of the safety of others, physical assault, gross insubordination. **On a construction site, wilful endangering of safety is the operative one**: removing an edge protection rail, disabling a machine guard, working at height without arrest equipment.
- **Fair procedure** requires: an investigation; notification of the allegations in a form and language the employee understands; a reasonable time to prepare; the assistance of a fellow employee or union representative; an opportunity to state a case; a decision communicated with reasons, preferably in writing.
- Formality is proportionate. The Code expressly contemplates that a small employer's process can be simple, but it must be **fair**.
- **Probation** guidelines: the period must be reasonable, the employee must be evaluated, given reasons and an opportunity to improve, and the standard for dismissing a probationer is less onerous than for a confirmed employee — but it is not zero.
- **Incapacity — poor performance**: standards must be communicated, the employee evaluated, given training or guidance and a reasonable opportunity to improve, and alternatives to dismissal considered.
- **Incapacity — ill health**: investigate the extent, the possibility of adapting the work or the workplace, and alternative work. Where the incapacity arises from an injury on duty, greater accommodation is expected.

### 3.3 Operational requirements — ss.189 and 189A

**s.189** requires a consultation process with the union, workplace forum, or the affected employees: written notice disclosing the reasons, the alternatives considered, the number and categories affected, the selection criteria, the timing, severance, assistance offered, and the possibility of future re-employment. The parties must attempt consensus on avoiding, minimising and mitigating the dismissals and on selection criteria.

**s.189A** applies to employers with **50 or more employees** where the number to be dismissed exceeds thresholds tied to workforce size: it introduces a 60-day facilitation period, a CCMA-appointed facilitator on request, and a right to strike over the retrenchment or to refer a procedural dispute to the Labour Court.

Construction demobilisation at the end of a project, where employees are on indefinite contracts, is an operational-requirements dismissal and must run the s.189 process. Employers who instead label it "contract expiry" without a genuine fixed term lose at the CCMA.

### 3.4 The CCMA

The **Commission for Conciliation, Mediation and Arbitration** (ss.112–150) is independent of the state and the parties. Route:

1. Refer the dispute on **LRA Form 7.11** — within **30 days** of dismissal for an unfair dismissal, 90 days for an unfair labour practice.
2. **Conciliation** (ss.133–135) within 30 days; a certificate of outcome issues.
3. **Con-arb** is the default for dismissal disputes — conciliation and arbitration on the same day unless a party objects.
4. **Arbitration** (ss.136–140) produces a final and binding award (s.143), enforceable as an order of the Labour Court. Review lies to the Labour Court under s.145.
5. Dismissals for **operational requirements by an employer with 50+ employees**, automatically unfair dismissals, and dismissals for participation in a strike go to the **Labour Court**, not to CCMA arbitration.

Legal representation at arbitration for misconduct and incapacity dismissals is limited (CCMA Rule 25) — commissioners have discretion.

**Where a bargaining council with jurisdiction exists, the dispute is referred to the council, not the CCMA.**

### 3.5 Non-standard employment — ss.198–198D

- **s.198** regulates **temporary employment services** (labour brokers): the TES is the employer, but the client and the TES are **jointly and severally liable** for contraventions of a collective agreement, a binding arbitration award, the BCEA, or a sectoral determination.
- **s.198A** — for employees earning **below the earnings threshold**, a TES placement exceeding **three months** is not a temporary service, and the employee is **deemed to be the employee of the client**, employed indefinitely, and must be treated no less favourably than comparable employees of the client.
- **s.198B** — fixed-term contracts for below-threshold employees longer than three months are permissible only on listed justifiable grounds, which include work of a **limited or definite duration** and a **specific project of limited duration**. Otherwise the employment is deemed indefinite. An employee on a justified fixed term of longer than 24 months for a project is entitled to severance of one week per completed year on expiry.
- **s.198C** — part-time employees.
- **s.198D** — disputes about ss.198A–C go to the CCMA or bargaining council.

## 4. Employment Equity Act 55 of 1998

The EEA prohibits unfair discrimination (Chapter II, including **equal pay for work of equal value**, s.6(4)) and imposes affirmative action duties on **designated employers** (Chapter III). Designated groups are black people, women, and persons with disabilities.

The **Employment Equity Amendment Act 4 of 2022** materially changed the regime: the turnover-based test for designated employer status was removed, so a designated employer is one with **50 or more employees**; and the Minister may set **sector-specific numerical targets**, with construction among the sectors covered. Employers must report annually (EEA2/EEA4 online reporting, typically opening 1 September and closing mid-January) and must hold a **certificate of compliance** issued under s.53 to do business with the state — which for a contractor is existential.

**These commencement dates, thresholds and target figures were not verified in this pass** — confirm with the Department of Employment and Labour.

## 5. Skills Development Act 97 of 1998 and the Skills Development Levies Act 9 of 1999

The SDA creates the **National Skills Authority**, the **SETAs**, learnerships, and the **QCTO**. The SDLA imposes the levy: **s.3** imposition, **s.4** exemptions, **s.6** payment to the Commissioner (SARS), **ss.8–9** distribution between the SETAs and the National Skills Fund.

Standard practice figures — **1 % of the leviable payroll**, with employers whose total annual payroll does not exceed **R500 000** exempt, paid monthly to SARS on the EMP201 within seven days of month end, distributed **80 % to SETAs / 20 % to the National Skills Fund** — are widely published but were **not verified** from the statutory text in this pass.

The relevant SETA for building and civil work is the **Construction Education and Training Authority (CETA)**. See file `06`.

## 6. COIDA 130 of 1993

- **s.80** — every employer must **register** with the Compensation Commissioner and furnish particulars.
- **s.82** — annual **return of earnings**.
- **s.83** — **assessment** calculated on earnings and the industry risk class; **s.85** tariff variation; **s.86** payment.
- **s.22** — the employee's right to compensation for an accident arising out of and in the course of employment.
- **s.35** — compensation **substitutes** any other legal remedy against the employer; **s.33** makes any cession or relinquishment of benefits void; **s.32** protects compensation from attachment.
- **s.38** — the employee must give notice of the accident to the employer; **s.39** — the employer must report it to the Commissioner; **s.40** — the Commissioner may hold an inquiry.
- **ss.43–45** — lodging and consideration of claims, with prescription periods.
- **ss.65, 68** — occupational diseases and notice.
- **ss.98, 99** — penalties for false statements and general offences.

Employees make no contribution. **A construction employer must be registered and in good standing before starting work** — reg 7 of the Construction Regulations 2014 requires the principal contractor to satisfy itself of this for every contractor before work commences. See file `08` for the reporting workflow (W.Cl.1/2/4 etc.) and file `09` for verification.

## 7. Bargaining councils and sectoral determinations

### 7.1 BCCEI — civil engineering

The **Bargaining Council for the Civil Engineering Industry** is a statutory body created under the LRA to regulate employment relations in civil engineering, providing collective bargaining, industry compliance, dispute resolution and social protection services. Its parties are the employer organisation side (principally SAFCEC) and the trade unions organising in the sector.

The council administers a **Main Collective Agreement** setting minimum wages by task grade, hours, overtime, allowances, leave and provident/leave-pay funds, plus a **Dispute Resolution Agreement** that gives it CCMA-equivalent jurisdiction over disputes in its registered scope. When the Minister **extends** an agreement to non-parties under s.32 of the LRA, every employer whose work falls in the registered scope is bound — including small contractors who have never heard of the council. Employers must register, submit returns, and pay levies and fund contributions.

> ⚠️ Falling within a bargaining council's registered scope is determined by the nature of the work, not by whether you joined. Check your scope before pricing a civil contract; unpaid council contributions are recoverable retrospectively with interest.

### 7.2 Building industry councils

Building work is covered regionally rather than nationally. The principal ones are the **Bargaining Council for the Building Industry, Cape of Good Hope (BIBC)**, and councils covering the Free State/Northern Cape (Kimberley/Bloemfontein) regions. They operate their own main agreements, wage schedules by grade, and holiday, sick-pay and provident funds. Outside a covered region and scope, the BCEA and the national minimum wage are the floor.

### 7.3 Sectoral determinations

Where there is no bargaining council, the Minister may issue a **sectoral determination** under BCEA Chapter Eight setting minimum wages and conditions for a sector. Determinations exist for domestic work, farm work, wholesale and retail, contract cleaning, private security and others. **There is no general construction sectoral determination**; construction is covered by the bargaining councils where they apply, and otherwise by the BCEA plus the national minimum wage.
