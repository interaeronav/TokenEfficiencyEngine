# The HR side of site health, safety and wellbeing

**Summary.** Safety law converts directly into HR obligations: named people appointed in writing with provable competence, medical certificates of fitness before site access, training records, injury reporting within statutory deadlines, and compensation-fund registration that must be in place **before** anyone starts work. **[ZA]** the Construction Regulations 2014 under the OHS Act 85 of 1993 drive the appointment regime; **[NA]** GN 156 of 1997 under the Labour Act does the equivalent. Beyond compliance, the wellbeing agenda in southern African construction is dominated by alcohol and cannabis, HIV, heat, fatigue and — increasingly recognised — mental health and suicide risk in a male-dominated, itinerant workforce.

> ⚠️ Legally consequential and safety-critical. An unregistered employer loses the compensation-fund shield against civil claims and carries the full cost of an injury. An appointment made without verifying competence exposes both the appointee and the appointing employer to personal liability. Do not allow anyone on site without induction, medical fitness (**[ZA]**) and verified competence for their task.

## Key facts

| Item | **[ZA]** Construction Regulations 2014 / COIDA | **[NA]** GN 156 of 1997 / Employees' Compensation Act |
|---|---|---|

| Health and safety officer | Appointed in writing after consulting the client, considering size and hazards (reg 8(5)) | Safety officer(s) appointed (reg 6); may be the CEO |
| Risk assessment | Competent person appointed in writing (reg 9) | Employer health and safety policy and programme (reg 3) |
| Fall protection | Competent person prepares and maintains the fall protection plan, including **medical fitness evaluation** of those working at height (reg 10) | Roof work rules, reg 248 |

## 1. Statutory appointments and competence

### 1.1 What an appointment is

An appointment is a **written instrument**, signed by the appointer and **accepted in writing** by the appointee, that names a person, states the legal provision under which they are appointed, defines the scope of their responsibility (which site, which activity, which period), and confirms that they have the resources and authority to discharge it. It must be kept in the health and safety file and copied to the personnel file.

### 1.2 Competence

**[ZA]** the Construction Regulations define a "competent person" by reference to knowledge, training, experience and, where a qualification is required by law, that qualification, plus familiarity with the Act and the applicable regulations. In practice, evidence of competence means: a relevant qualification or trade certificate; documented specific training for the appointment (e.g. a scaffold inspector's course, a fall protection planner's course, an incident investigation course); demonstrable experience; and, for construction health and safety practitioners, **SACPCMP registration** in the appropriate category.

**HR's job** is to hold the evidence: certificates verified with the issuing body, dated, with expiry tracked on the competency matrix (file `06`).

### 1.3 The appointment register

Maintain a single register listing, for each appointment: the legal provision, the appointee's name, the date of appointment, the date of acceptance, the scope, the competence evidence held, any expiry, and the alternate. Review it whenever a person leaves — **an appointment does not transfer**, and a site running with a departed construction manager is operating unlawfully.

**Common appointments on a building site [ZA]:** construction manager and alternate (reg 8(1)–(3)); assistant construction managers (reg 8(4)); construction health and safety officer (reg 8(5)); risk assessor (reg 9); fall protection planner (reg 10); temporary works designer (reg 12); excavation supervisor (reg 13); demolition supervisor (reg 14); scaffold supervisor and inspector (reg 16); suspended platform supervisor (reg 17); material hoist inspector (reg 19); bulk mixing plant supervisor (reg 20); machinery supervisor (reg 22); vehicle and mobile plant supervisor (reg 23); electrical installation competent person (reg 24); stacking supervisor (reg 28); fire equipment inspector (reg 29). Plus, under the OHS Act and its other regulations: first aiders, incident investigators, and safety representatives.

## 2. Medical fitness certificates

**[ZA]** every employee performing construction work must hold a **valid medical certificate of fitness** in the form of **Annexure 3** to the Construction Regulations, issued by an occupational health practitioner. The principal contractor must ensure this for its own employees, and its reg 7 duty to verify contractors means it will ask subcontractors for theirs too.

The examination should be **risk-based**, matched to the person's tasks: vision and hearing for plant operators; blood pressure, cardiac and neurological screening plus a specific fitness-to-work-at-height assessment for anyone in a fall-risk position (reg 10(2)(b) expressly requires the fall protection plan to include the process for evaluating medical fitness for work at height and the records of it); lung function where there is silica, cement or welding-fume exposure; and general fitness for heavy manual work.

**Confidentiality.** The employer is entitled to the **outcome** — fit, fit with restrictions, unfit — not to the clinical detail. Store medical information separately from the ordinary personnel file, with restricted access. **[ZA]** POPIA treats health information as special personal information.

**Re-examination** on the certificate's expiry, after any significant illness or injury, and on return from extended absence. A person whose certificate has expired must not be allocated to the task.

**[NA]** there is no directly equivalent Annexure 3 regime, but the general duty to provide a safe workplace, plus occupational medical surveillance requirements for specific exposures under GN 156 of 1997, make a comparable programme the sensible standard.

## 3. Injury on duty — reporting and claims

### 3.1 **[ZA]** COIDA

1. **Immediately**: render first aid, get the injured person to medical care, make the area safe, and preserve the scene where the injury is serious.
2. **The employee** must give notice of the accident to the employer as soon as reasonably practicable (s.38).
3. **The employer** must report the accident to the Compensation Commissioner (s.39) within the prescribed period — in practice **within 7 days** of receiving notice for an accident, and within **14 days** for an occupational disease (*verify current periods and forms*). The employer's report is the **W.Cl.2**; the employee completes/accompanies with a **W.Cl.14** notice of accident and claim for compensation; the first medical report is the **W.Cl.4** and the progress/final medical report the **W.Cl.5**; resumption is reported on the **W.Cl.6**.
4. **The Commissioner** may hold an inquiry (s.40) and considers the claim (ss.43–45), subject to prescription periods (s.44).
5. **Benefits**: medical aid; temporary total or partial disablement payments (the employer commonly pays the first three months and recovers from the Fund — *verify*); permanent disablement compensation or a pension; death benefits and funeral costs to dependants.
6. **Also report to the Department of Employment and Labour** under the OHS Act where the incident meets s.24 thresholds — death, unconsciousness, loss of a limb, an injury likely to be permanently disabling, or a dangerous occurrence — and do **not disturb the site** without an inspector's permission.

**s.35 COIDA** substitutes the compensation regime for any other legal remedy against the employer; **s.33** makes any cession or waiver of benefits void; **s.32** protects compensation from attachment. That trade-off only holds for a registered employer in good standing.

### 3.2 **[NA]** Employees' Compensation Act 30 of 1941 (as amended)

Administered by the **Social Security Commission** as the **Employees' Compensation Fund (ECF)**. Every employer must register and pay an annual assessment based on payroll and industry risk class; **employees contribute nothing**. The employee reports the accident to the employer; the employer reports to the Commission on the prescribed form within the prescribed period and assists with the claim. Benefits cover medical treatment, temporary total disablement as a percentage of earnings, permanent disablement as a pension or lump sum by degree, and death benefits to dependants. Serious incidents must also be reported under GN 156 of 1997 to the labour inspectorate.

**The exact Namibian reporting deadlines and forms could not be retrieved in this pass** — confirm with the SSC before relying on any period.

### 3.3 The HR discipline around it

- Keep an **incident register** on site, including near misses. A near miss reported and closed out is free information.
- **Investigate every lost-time injury** with a written report: sequence, immediate causes, underlying causes (training, supervision, equipment, method, fatigue, pressure), corrective actions with owners and dates.
- Never discipline someone **for reporting** an injury. Discipline for the unsafe act that caused it is legitimate — after a proper hearing — but if reporting attracts punishment, reporting stops and the next injury is a fatality.
- Manage the **return to work**: light duties, phased hours, a review with the treating practitioner. Where the incapacity is permanent, run an incapacity process (file `07`) with the greater accommodation Schedule 8 expects for injuries on duty.
- Verify **subcontractor** compensation registration and good standing before site access (file `09`).

## 4. Substance abuse

Alcohol is the dominant substance problem on southern African sites; cannabis is now widespread and, **[ZA]**, private adult use is decriminalised — which does **not** create any right to be impaired at work.

**A defensible policy contains:**
- A clear rule: **no person may report for or remain on duty while under the influence of alcohol or any drug having a narcotic effect**, and no alcohol or illegal drugs may be brought on site. This mirrors the General Safety Regulations **[ZA]**.
- Disclosure of **prescription medication** that may cause drowsiness, to the supervisor or occupational health, so that safety-critical allocation can be adjusted.
- **Testing**: breathalyser as the standard for alcohol, done consistently, by a trained person, on calibrated equipment, with the result recorded and countersigned. Random testing must be genuinely random and applied to all levels including management. Obtain **written consent** in the contract and the policy.
- **A distinction between misconduct and dependency.** Isolated intoxication is misconduct. Established **alcoholism or drug dependency is an incapacity**, and Schedule 8's incapacity guidelines apply — the employer should offer assistance and counselling before dismissal, and dismissal follows only if the employee refuses help or does not improve.
- **Support**: a referral route to a clinic, an EAP where affordable, or a community organisation such as AA. In rural Namibia and South Africa this may be a single named clinic sister; name her anyway.
- **Consistency**: the policy fails the moment a foreman is treated differently from a labourer.

## 5. HIV/AIDS in the southern African workplace

Southern Africa carries among the highest HIV prevalences globally, and construction's mobile, male, migrant workforce is a well-documented higher-risk group.

**Legal position.** **[NA]** s.5 of the Labour Act prohibits discrimination on the ground of **HIV status**, and dismissal for HIV status is unfair. **[ZA]** the Employment Equity Act prohibits unfair discrimination on HIV status, **HIV testing of an employee is prohibited unless the Labour Court has declared it justifiable** (EEA s.7(2)), and the Code of Good Practice on Key Aspects of HIV/AIDS and Employment sets the standard.

**What an employer should do:**
- **No testing** as a condition of employment, promotion or continued employment. **[ZA]** anonymous, unlinked prevalence surveys and voluntary counselling and testing offered on a genuinely voluntary basis are permitted.
- **No disclosure.** Status is confidential medical information; a supervisor who discloses it commits a serious offence under the employer's own code.
- **Universal precautions** in first aid: gloves in every first aid box, resuscitation shields, safe sharps handling, spill kits, and training for first aiders. Provide **post-exposure prophylaxis** access after a needlestick or blood exposure.
- **Access to treatment**: facilitate time off for clinic visits, support ARV adherence, and remember that an employee stable on treatment is fully fit for work.
- **Incapacity, not status, is the ground.** If HIV progresses to genuine incapacity, run the ill-health incapacity process (file `07`) with accommodation, exactly as for any other illness.
- **Awareness** through toolbox talks, condom availability on site, and partnership with the local clinic or a workplace programme.
- **TB screening.** TB is the leading opportunistic infection and is separately relevant on dusty sites; silica exposure materially raises TB risk. Screen and refer.

## 6. Heat stress

Namibian and South African sites regularly exceed 35 °C, with much higher radiant loads on roofs, in trenches and inside partially completed buildings. Heat illness ranges from cramps and exhaustion to heat stroke, which is a medical emergency with a high fatality rate.

**Controls, in order:**
1. **Reschedule.** Start at first light; take the long break in the middle of the day; move heavy work to the morning; do roof and slab work early.
2. **Shade and water.** Shaded rest areas at the workface, not 300 m away. **[NA]** GN 156 of 1997 requires clean drinking water kept **cool** in a hot environment. Plan for roughly 250 ml every 20 minutes of heavy work in heat; provide oral rehydration salts.
3. **Acclimatise.** New and returning workers are the most at risk. Build exposure over 7–14 days.
4. **Clothing and PPE.** Light, breathable, long-sleeved; hard hats with brims or neck flaps; recognise that impermeable PPE dramatically raises heat load and shorten work cycles accordingly.
5. **Buddy system and supervisor training.** Recognise confusion, absence of sweating, staggering, and irritability as emergencies, not attitude problems.
6. **Emergency response.** Move to shade, cool aggressively with water and airflow, and evacuate. Rehearse it — on a remote site, know the transport plan before you need it.
7. **Adjust incentives.** Task rates in extreme heat push people past their limit; suspend or moderate them on the hottest days.

## 7. Fatigue

Fatigue is a systemic risk in construction: long programmes, overtime, early starts, and — on remote projects — long travel each way on top of the shift.

- Respect the statutory ceilings: **45 ordinary hours and 10 overtime hours a week** in both countries. Chronic overtime beyond that is both unlawful and a direct cause of incidents.
- Count **travel time** in the fatigue calculation even though it may not be paid working time. A worker leaving home at 04h30 and returning at 20h00 is not fresh at 15h00.
- Watch the **compressed week** **[ZA]** s.11 BCEA: 12-hour days are lawful but need extra fatigue controls, especially for plant operators and drivers.
- **Drivers** carrying workers are the highest-consequence fatigue risk on any project. Set maximum driving hours, mandate rest breaks, and never let a driver do a full manual shift and then drive the crew home.
- Rotate physically punishing tasks; schedule safety-critical work (crane lifts, hot work, confined space) early in the shift.

## 8. Mental health

Construction has an internationally documented elevated suicide rate among men, and southern African sites add migration, separation from family, debt, alcohol, job insecurity and physical pain to the picture. The industry's culture actively discourages disclosure.

**What is realistic for a small firm:**
- **Train supervisors to notice** — withdrawal, deteriorating work, increased absence, uncharacteristic irritability, heavier drinking, giving things away.
- **Ask directly and privately.** A foreman asking "are you alright, honestly?" and listening is the intervention with the best evidence base available at this scale.
- **Know one referral route** and put the number on the noticeboard: the nearest clinic, a national helpline, a church or community counsellor, or an EAP if the firm has one.
- **Remove the obvious stressors you control**: pay on time and correctly, communicate about project end dates honestly, deal with bullying and harassment as gross misconduct, and manage debt-driven wage attachment sensitively.
- **Handle bereavement properly.** **[NA]** compassionate leave of five working days is a statutory right (s.25); **[ZA]** family responsibility leave is three days (s.27). Grant it without argument and record it.
- **After a fatality or serious injury on site**, arrange debriefing for the crew who witnessed it. Untreated, it produces resignations, absenteeism and a second incident.
