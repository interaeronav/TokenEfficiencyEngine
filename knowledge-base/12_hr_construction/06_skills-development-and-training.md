# Skills development and training in construction

**Summary.** Both countries fund construction training through a compulsory payroll levy that employers can claim back: the **VET levy** at 1 % of annual payroll for Namibian employers with payroll of N$1 000 000 or more, administered by the **Namibia Training Authority** through the **National Training Fund**; and the **Skills Development Levy** in South Africa, paid to SARS and distributed to SETAs — for construction, the **CETA** — and the National Skills Fund. Formal qualifications run through NTA-accredited providers and the National Trade Testing and Certification Centre **[NA]**, and through the **QCTO** occupational qualifications system **[ZA]**. On site, the training that actually changes behaviour is structured mentoring, the toolbox talk, and a visible competency matrix.

> ⚠️ Training is a legal control, not a nice-to-have. **[ZA]** reg 9 of the Construction Regulations 2014 requires every employee to be informed, instructed and trained by a competent person about hazards and control measures **before work commences**. Putting an untrained person on a task is a regulatory breach and, after an incident, the first thing an inspector will ask for is the training record.

## 1. **[NA]** The Namibian system

### 1.1 The Namibia Training Authority

The **Vocational Education and Training Act 1 of 2008** establishes the NTA as a juristic person (ss.4–5), governed by an 11-member Board (s.9) comprising five employer representatives, two trade union representatives, three government members and a Namibia Qualifications Authority nominee. The NTA advises the Minister on VET policy (s.5(1)(a)), develops strategy to increase access, equity and quality, and may **accredit education and training providers and programmes** and **issue awards and certificates** subject to NQA policy (s.5(3)). Quality systems auditors designated by the Board (s.45) may enter premises, interview staff and learners, and require records; providers must cooperate (s.45(4)).

### 1.2 The National Training Fund and the VET levy

The **National Training Fund** (s.24) is made up of parliamentary appropriations, VET levies with interest and penalties, donations and investment income; it replaced the former Vocational Training Fund (s.24(3)). The Board controls the Fund (s.25) and it finances assistance to employers and training providers, approved VET programmes and NTA administration (s.26). The Fund is tax-exempt (s.29).

The **levy** is imposed by the Minister by Gazette notice with the concurrence of Finance and after consulting the Board (ss.35–36); different amounts may be set for different categories of employer by employee numbers, payroll, turnover or other criteria (s.35(3)). Employers pay to the NTA or a designated collection agent (s.37). Late payment attracts prescribed interest (s.38) and a **10 % penalty** (s.39); unpaid levies are recoverable as a debt (s.40). Employers must keep employee records and furnish information (s.42), and inspectors may enter workplaces without a warrant, question people, seize documents and require employees to appear (s.43). The Minister may exempt employers or categories by notice (s.41).

**In practice:** the levy is **1 % of actual annual payroll** for employers with an **annual payroll of N$1 000 000 or more**, payable monthly in arrears by the 20th of the following month.

### 1.3 Claiming the levy back — the Employer Training Grant

Levy-paying employers may claim back the cost of approved training. **Eligible costs** include facilitator/trainer fees, training materials, assessment and certification costs, and subsistence and travel allowances for trainees. **Conditions:** claims are submitted within **31 days after the financial year end (31 March)**; the employer must be current on levy payments with no outstanding penalties. The claims contact published by the NTA is `levyclaims@nta.com.na`.

A small contractor paying, say, N$15 000 a month in levy is funding roughly N$180 000 of training a year whether it trains or not. The economically rational move is to build a training plan that consumes it.

### 1.4 Apprenticeships and trade testing

The VET Act preserved the apprenticeship provisions of the repealed National Vocational Training Act until dates determined by the Minister (s.49(12)), and maintained the **National Trade Testing and Certification Centre** under transitional arrangements (s.49(13)). Construction apprenticeships are typically delivered through **NTA-accredited Vocational Training Centres** — including Valombola, Zambezi, Nakayale, Okakarara, Rundu and Windhoek VTCs — on a **dual/COBE model**: institutional blocks alternating with structured workplace practice with a host employer, assessed against **NTA unit standards**.

The unit standard structure is worth understanding because it is how competence is defined. A single standard — for example *Work safely on scaffolding, platforms and ladders as part of bricklaying operations* — sets out specific outcomes, assessment criteria, range statements and the evidence required. Aligning your on-site assessment rubrics (file `03`) to the unit standards means your internal records become usable evidence towards formal certification.

**Trades relevant to building work** in the Namibian system: bricklaying and plastering, carpentry and joinery, plumbing and pipefitting, electrical general, welding and metal fabrication, boilermaking, painting and decorating, tiling, refrigeration and air conditioning, and plant/earthmoving operation.

## 2. **[ZA]** The South African system

### 2.1 Skills Development Act and the levy

The **Skills Development Act 97 of 1998** creates the National Skills Authority, the SETAs, learnerships and skills programmes, and (via amendment) the **Quality Council for Trades and Occupations (QCTO)**. The **Skills Development Levies Act 9 of 1999** imposes the levy (s.3), provides exemptions (s.4), requires payment to the Commissioner — SARS — (s.6), and distributes the proceeds between the SETAs and the **National Skills Fund** (ss.8–9). The customary figures are **1 % of the leviable amount**, exemption where total annual payroll does not exceed **R500 000**, monthly payment with the EMP201, and an **80/20** split to SETAs and the NSF — all `needs-verification`.

### 2.2 CETA

The **Construction Education and Training Authority** is the SETA for the construction sector. Levy-paying employers recover a **mandatory grant** by appointing a Skills Development Facilitator and submitting a **Workplace Skills Plan (WSP)** and **Annual Training Report (ATR)** by the annual deadline (historically 30 April). **Discretionary grants** fund learnerships, apprenticeships, bursaries, internships, artisan development and workplace experience placements, and are awarded against published windows. CETA also accredits training providers and assessors within its scope.

### 2.3 QCTO and the occupational qualification

The QCTO now owns occupational qualifications. The modern structure of a trade qualification has three components — **knowledge, practical skills, and work experience** — and culminates in an **External Integrated Summative Assessment (EISA)** at an accredited assessment centre. Passing yields an **occupational certificate** and, for a listed trade, artisan status. Legacy **section 28 trade test certificates** remain valid.

**Recognition of Prior Learning (RPL)** is the route for the very large population of southern African construction workers who are competent but uncertified. It is slow and paperwork-heavy but it converts an informal workforce into a certifiable one, and it is fundable from the levy.

### 2.4 Learnerships

A learnership is a **tripartite agreement** — employer, learner, accredited provider — registered with the SETA, combining structured learning and workplace experience and leading to a registered qualification. A learner is treated as an employee for most BCEA/LRA purposes. A separate **learnership determination** under the BCEA governs minimum allowances and conditions for learners who were **not** employed by the employer before the learnership. Employers gain: the mandatory/discretionary grant, a **learnership tax allowance** under section 12H of the Income Tax Act (*current amounts unverified*), and B-BBEE skills development points.

## 3. On-site mentoring — the part that actually works

Formal training produces certificates; mentoring produces tradesmen. A workable structure for a small firm:

- **Pair, don't float.** Assign each learner to one named artisan for a defined period. Both names go on the competency matrix.
- **Pay the mentor.** A modest mentoring allowance (file `05`) buys attention that goodwill will not sustain past week three.
- **Define the task list.** Give the mentor a written list of the tasks the learner must be signed off on — set out a right angle, gauge a course, mix to ratio, place DPC with the correct lap, form a cavity tray, strike formwork safely. This is the workplace-experience component of the formal qualification, so record it in a **logbook** the SETA or NTA will accept.
- **Rotate deliberately.** Move learners through groundworks, superstructure and finishes rather than leaving them where they are most immediately useful.
- **Sign off in front of the work.** Assessment happens at the wall, with a level in hand, not at a desk.

**Retention.** A trained artisan is a liquid asset who will be poached. Counter with: a completion bonus tied to qualification, a pay step on certification written into the contract, a progression path to chargehand, and — where lawful and reasonable — a training cost repayment agreement that reduces to zero over 12–24 months. **[ZA]** s.33A BCEA prohibits requiring an employee to *pay for training*; a genuine, reasonable, reducing repayment agreement for a costly qualification is a different thing but should be drafted by a lawyer.

## 4. Toolbox talks as training

The toolbox talk is the highest-leverage training instrument on a construction site: 5–10 minutes, at the workface, before the shift, on one topic that matters today.

**How to run one that counts as training:**
- One topic. Today's hazard, on today's task.
- Show, don't tell — the actual harness, the actual guard, the actual defective ladder.
- Ask two questions of named people to confirm understanding.
- **Record it**: date, site, topic, presenter, and the signature or thumbprint of every attendee. This register is simultaneously a training record (reg 9 **[ZA]**), a safety record, and — when someone later does exactly what they were told not to — the evidence in a disciplinary hearing (file `07`).
- Deliver in the workers' language.

**A 12-week rolling programme** for a house build: PPE and its limits; manual handling; ladder use; scaffold safety and the tag system; edge protection and fall arrest; excavation collapse and access; hand and power tool safety; electrical safety and temporary supply; hazardous substances and silica dust; heat stress and hydration; housekeeping and slips/trips; emergency procedure and first aid. Then repeat with new examples from your own near misses.

## 5. Competency matrices

A competency matrix is a single sheet: employees down the side, competences across the top, and a cell state in each intersection.

| State | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Blank | Not trained |
| **T** | Trained, not yet assessed |
| **A** | Assessed competent — date and assessor recorded |
| **E** | Expert — can mentor and assess others |
| **X** | Certificate/licence expired — **must not perform the task** |

Columns for a small building contractor: site induction; manual handling; PPE; working at height / harness; scaffold erection; scaffold inspection; excavation supervision; abrasive wheels; power tools; first aid; fire extinguisher use; plant operation by machine class; slinging and signalling; hot work; confined space; and each trade competence with its tolerance standard.

**Rules that make it useful:** it lives on the site office wall where the foreman allocates work; expiry dates are colour-coded 90 days ahead; nobody is allocated to a task where their cell is blank or X; and it is reconciled against the personnel files monthly. It also doubles as your gap analysis for the WSP **[ZA]** or the Employer Training Grant plan **[NA]**.

## 6. Building a pipeline on a small firm's budget

A ten-person contractor can run a credible development pipeline for very little:

1. **Spend the levy you are already paying.** If you are over the threshold, the money is gone whether you train or not. Claim it.
2. **Take one apprentice or learner per year, not five.** One well-mentored learner becomes an artisan; five neglected ones become five resignations.
3. **Partner with the nearest VTC or TVET college** for the institutional blocks and use the firm as the workplace host. The provider carries the accreditation burden.
4. **Use RPL for your existing hands.** Certifying three competent-but-uncertified bricklayers costs less than recruiting one qualified one, and it buys loyalty.
5. **Make the foreman a trainer.** Fund a train-the-trainer or assessor course for the foreman; it multiplies across every hire thereafter.
6. **Bank the records.** Toolbox registers, logbooks, assessment sheets and the competency matrix are the evidence base for grant claims, tender pre-qualification, B-BBEE scoring **[ZA]**, affirmative action reporting **[NA]**, and your defence after an incident.
7. **Train supervisors in the law.** A foreman who understands the disciplinary code, the hours rules and the incident-reporting duty prevents more cost than any technical course.
