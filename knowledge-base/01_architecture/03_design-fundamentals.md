# Design fundamentals — parti, site, order and dimension

**Summary.** Design is the negotiation between an organising idea (parti), a place (site), a set of human requirements (brief) and a means of building (tectonics). This file is written to be used at the drawing board: it states the moves, then gives the dimensions that make them buildable. Where a dimension is legally binding in South Africa it is cited to its SANS 10400 clause and marked **[ZA]**; **[NA]** local-authority regulations differ in detail but the anthropometric logic is identical.

> ⚠️ These are *minima for legality*, not targets for quality. A 750 mm stair and a 6 m² room are lawful and often miserable. Design above the minimum by default; drop to it only deliberately.

## 1. Parti and concept

The **parti** is the diagram that explains the building in one move — the idea that, if removed, collapses the design. It is not a style, a mood board or a metaphor; it is a spatial or organisational proposition that can be drawn in ten seconds.

Recurring parti families:
- **Bar / linear** — single-loaded circulation, thin plan, excellent for daylight and cross-ventilation. Depth 9–15 m.
- **Courtyard** — introverted, climate-buffering, wayfinding-legible. Minimum useful courtyard ≈ 1× to 1,5× the height of enclosing walls for winter sun access.
- **Nine-square / grid** — neutral, extensible, structurally efficient.
- **Poché / served-and-servant** — thick walls absorb services and storage; the free plan reads as clean.
- **Mat / carpet** — low-rise, high-density, roof-lit.
- **Object in field** — a legible figure standing free; demands a resolved all-round elevation.
- **Spine and comb** — a circulation spine with repeated fingers; the standard for schools and clinics.

Test the parti with three questions: *Where does the load go? Where does the water go? Where does the person go?* A parti that cannot answer all three is a picture, not a design.

## 2. Site analysis

Record, at a minimum:
- **Survey** — boundaries, erf/plot number, servitudes, existing levels at ≤ 1 m contour intervals, existing trees with trunk diameter and canopy spread, existing structures.
- **Statutory** — zoning, permitted use, coverage, floor-area ratio (FAR/bulk), height limit, building lines/setbacks, parking ratio, heritage overlay. **[ZA]** from the municipal town-planning scheme; **[NA]** e.g. the Windhoek Town Planning Scheme.
- **Access** — vehicle crossover position and gradient, service and refuse access, pedestrian desire lines, public transport within 400 m / 800 m.
- **Services** — water main, sewer invert level and direction of fall, electrical supply point and capacity, stormwater outfall.
- **Climate** — true north, sun path for the latitude, prevailing wind by season, rainfall intensity, extreme temperatures (see file `05`).
- **Sensory** — noise sources and levels, views to keep and to screen, dust, odour.
- **Geotechnical** — soil classification, expected bearing capacity, water table, heave/collapse potential. In much of Namibia and the SA interior, expansive clays and calcrete both occur; get a geotech report before committing to a foundation type.

Draw the analysis as **overlays on one base plan at a single scale**, not as a slideshow of unrelated diagrams.

## 3. Programme and brief

Convert the client's wishes into **areas, adjacencies and performance**:

| Space | Net area guide | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Bedroom (single) | 9–12 m² | Bed 900 × 2 000; 750 mm clear one side |
| Bedroom (double) | 12–16 m² | Bed 1 500/1 800 × 2 000; 750 mm clear both sides |
| Bathroom (full) | 4,5–6 m² | Min 1 700 bath; 800 clear in front of WC |
| Kitchen (residential) | 8–14 m² | Work triangle sum 3,6–6,6 m; 1 000–1 200 mm between opposing runs |
| Living / dining | 20–35 m² | Dining: 600 mm per seated person at table |
| Open-plan office workstation | 8–12 m² per person incl. circulation | 6 m² is dense; 4,5 m² is unpleasant |
| Cellular office | 10–14 m² | |
| Meeting room | 2,0–2,5 m² per seat | Plus 900 mm behind chairs |
| Classroom (primary) | 1,8–2,2 m² per learner | |
| Retail floor | net:gross ≈ 0,75 | |
| Circulation and services allowance | **+ 20–35%** on net area | Lower for offices, higher for hospitals/labs |

Express the brief as a **net area schedule**, apply the grossing factor, and check the result against the site's permitted bulk before drawing anything.

## 4. Circulation

Circulation is 15–30% of a building's area and 100% of its legibility.

| Route | Clear width |
|---|---|
| Residential passage, single occupancy | 900 mm (1 000–1 100 mm comfortable) |
| Public corridor, two-way pedestrian flow | **1 500–1 800 mm** |
| Corridor with doors opening into it | add 900 mm of recess or use outward-swinging doors into alcoves |
| Corridor serving assembly occupancy | size on occupant load; **[ZA]** see SANS 10400-T 4.21 |
| Hospital/bed-moving corridor | 2 200–2 400 mm |
| Retail mall primary | 4 000–6 000 mm |

Rules: keep primary circulation **daylit and single-loaded where possible**; make junctions visible from both approaches; do not let a corridor exceed **2,5 × its width in dead-end depth** **[ZA]** SANS 10400-T 3.26; place stairs and lifts where they are seen on entry.

**Stair geometry, practical.** A comfortable public stair is riser **165–175 mm**, going **280–300 mm** — check: 300 + 2(170) = 640 mm, inside the 570–650 band. Domestic stairs run steeper: riser 180–190 mm, going 250–260 mm. For a 2 900 mm floor-to-floor with 175 mm risers: 2 900 / 175 = 16,6 → use **17 risers at 170,6 mm**, 16 goings at 280 mm = **4 480 mm** of run, plus landings. That is the single most useful stair calculation in practice — do it before drawing the plan, not after.

## 5. Proportion and order systems

- **Golden section** φ = 1,618. Rectangle 1 : 1,618. Its reciprocal 0,618.
- **Root rectangles** — √2 (1:1,414, the A-series paper ratio), √3, √5. √2 rectangles halve into √2 rectangles, which is why A-series sheets nest.
- **Modulor** (Le Corbusier) — 1 130 / 1 829 / 2 260 mm derived from a 1 830 mm figure; useful as a set of related heights (sill, sitting, standing, reach).
- **Ken / tatami** — 900 × 1 800 mm module; still one of the best planning grids for small residential work.
- **Structural grid** — pick early, and pick something the market builds: 3 000, 3 600, 6 000, 7 200, 8 400 mm. Parking dictates 7 800–8 400 mm bays (three 2 500–2 600 mm bays plus columns).
- **Brick module** **[ZA]/[NA]** — nominal 222 × 106 × 73 mm imperial-derived stock brick with 10 mm joints gives a **75 mm vertical course**; 4 courses = 300 mm. Dimension masonry openings in multiples of 75 mm vertically and ~112,5 mm horizontally to avoid cutting.

Order systems are only useful if applied **consistently across plan, section and elevation**. A golden-section elevation on a chaotic plan is decoration.

## 6. Spatial sequence, scale, threshold

**Sequence.** Design the walk, not the plan. Write the route as a list — arrival, compression, release, orientation, ascent, arrival — and check each transition is registered by something physical: a change of ceiling height, of light, of material underfoot, of width.

**Compression and release** is the cheapest and most reliable spatial effect: a 2 100 mm entry vestibule opening into a 3 600 mm living volume reads as generous at a fraction of the cost of making everything tall.

**Scale.** Three scales operate simultaneously:
- *Intimate* — 2 100–2 400 mm ceiling, 3–4 m dimension, one to four people.
- *Room* — 2 700–3 300 mm ceiling, 4–8 m dimension.
- *Civic* — 4 000 mm+ ceiling, 10 m+ span; requires proportionate detail size or it reads as a warehouse.

Anthropometric anchors: eye height standing **1 500–1 650 mm**; seated eye **1 150–1 250 mm**; comfortable sill for a seated view **450–600 mm**; standing view sill **900–1 000 mm**; handrail 900–1 000 mm; worktop **900 mm**; desk **720–750 mm**; balustrade 1 000 mm minimum **[ZA]**.

**Threshold.** A door is a hole; a threshold is an event. Give it depth (a reveal of 200–450 mm), a change of plane or level, shelter (an overhang of 1 200 mm minimum in a rainfall climate), and something to do — a bench, a bell, a light, a place to put a bag down. In hot-dry Namibia, the threshold should also be a *shade* device: a deep, ventilated porch is a functional cooling element, not an ornament.

## 7. Light

- Rule of thumb: useful daylight penetrates to about **2,0–2,5 × the window head height** from the window wall for a normal room; up to **3,0–3,5 ×** under clear skies (which is the Southern African condition); **1,5 ×** for offices demanding uniformity, **2,5 ×** with a light shelf.
- Window-to-floor area ratio: **10%** gives roughly 100 lux minimum; **20%** gives ~200 lux minimum under overcast sky and considerably more under clear sky. Average daylight factor ≈ **32 × window-to-floor ratio** (%).
- Daylight factor targets: offices 5% average / 2% minimum; kitchens 2% over work surfaces; living rooms 1%; bedrooms 0,5%; general adequacy 2%; "cheerfully lit" 5%.
- Windows should occupy **≥ 50% of the window-wall width** for uniformity, and room depth should not exceed **2 × room width** where the window runs full width.
- Two-sided or top-lit rooms are dramatically better than single-sided ones; a high clerestory on the opposite wall halves the contrast ratio.
- **[NA]/[ZA]** Under a bright clear sky, more glass is usually the wrong answer. Prefer **smaller, well-shaded, well-distributed openings** with light-coloured internal surfaces to large unshaded glazing (see file `05`).

## 8. Materiality and tectonics

**Tectonics** is the expression of how the building is made. Decide, per element, whether you are working in:
- **Stereotomic** (mass, compression, carved — masonry, rammed earth, concrete): openings are holes; corners are solid; weight is expressed at the base.
- **Tectonic** (frame, joint, assembly — timber, steel): members are distinct; joints are visible; lightness is expressed by lifting off the ground.

Mixing the two is normal; mixing them *unconsciously* is what produces incoherent buildings. Name the system before detailing.

**Material honesty checklist**
1. Does the material do structural work, or is it a finish? Say which in the detail.
2. How does water leave it, and where does it go? Drip, throat, flashing, fall.
3. What is the movement joint spacing? (Clay brickwork ≈ 10–12 m; concrete masonry ≈ 6 m; see file `06`.)
4. How is it repaired or replaced in 20 years?
5. Is it locally obtainable? In northern Namibia, transport cost can exceed material cost — see domain `11_logistics_remote_areas`.
6. What does it do thermally? Mass on the inside of insulation; lightweight assemblies need insulation *and* ventilation.

**Standard sizes to design to (avoid cutting) [ZA]/[NA]:**
- Aluminium casement windows: 600×600, 600×900, 900×1200, 900×1500, 1200×1200, 1500×1800 mm.
- Sliding windows: 1200×1200, 1500×1200, 1800×1200, 2100×1500, 2400×1200 mm.
- Awning: 600×600, 900×600, 1200×600, 1500×600, 1800×1200 mm.
- Sheet materials: 1200 × 2400 mm boards; 900/1000 mm cover width profiled steel sheet.
- Brickwork: 75 mm vertical course; plan on 112,5 mm increments.

## Open questions

- Room-area and workstation guides in section 3 are practice norms distilled from the Metric Handbook / Neufert tradition rather than statutory minima; only the SANS-cited figures are legally binding.
- SANS 10400-T escape-route **widths** (clause 4.21) and the occupant-load table could not be read from the archived PDF excerpt; consult the full standard before sizing corridors on fire grounds.
- **[NA]** Equivalent Namibian dimensional minima (Municipality of Windhoek Building Regulations, G.N. 57 of 1969) were not extracted clause-by-clause; do not assume SANS numbers apply automatically in Namibia.
- Brick nominal dimensions vary by manufacturer; verify against the supplier's data before setting out.
