# The reading list — an annotated bibliography

**Summary.** This is what educational psychologists and psychologists working with human behaviour actually read, grouped by stage of training and by topic, with an honest note on each item's standing. Three rules govern the annotations. First, **popularity is not evidence** — several of the best-selling books here rest on findings that have shrunk or failed under replication, and that is said plainly. Second, **primary sources are read for orientation, not authority** — Piaget's empirical claims have been substantially revised and you read him to understand the questions, not the answers. Third, **edition numbers are deliberately omitted** — authors, titles and first-publication years were verified via Open Library, but current editions change and must be checked against the publisher or the department's prescribed list.

## Key facts

| Tier | What it is for | How much of it you actually read |
|---|---|---|
| Undergraduate core texts | Coverage and vocabulary | Cover to cover, once |
| Educational psychology texts | The applied synthesis | One thoroughly, others for reference |
| Assessment texts | Working reference | Kept on the desk for a career |
| Statistics and methods | Working reference | Field cover to cover; the rest by chapter |
| Theory primary sources | Orientation and intellectual honesty | Selected chapters, not whole works |
| Evidence-based teaching | What to tell teachers on Monday | All of it; it is short |
| Diagnostic references | Look-up | Never read through; consulted constantly |
| Popular works | Cultural literacy and caution | Read with the critique alongside |

> ⚠️ Verify every edition before prescribing or purchasing. Only authors, titles and first-publication years were verified here.

## 1. Undergraduate core texts

**Richard Gross, *Psychology: The Science of Mind and Behaviour*** (first published 1987). The dominant British and southern African introductory text for a generation. Encyclopaedic, dense, strong on the history and on conceptual issues, and unusually good at flagging controversies rather than smoothing them. Standing: still excellent as a reference; some readers find it drier and less pedagogically scaffolded than the American competitors.

**David G. Myers and C. Nathan DeWall, *Psychology*** (Myers' text dating from the 1980s; the DeWall co-authorship from 2012 onward). The best-selling introductory psychology text in the world. Superbly written, pedagogically engineered, culturally American. Standing: the default choice; criticised, fairly, for a certain smoothness that can make contested findings look settled.

**Michael W. Passer and Ronald E. Smith, *Psychology: The Science of Mind and Behavior*** (first published 2002). A rigorous alternative to Myers with a more explicit emphasis on levels of analysis (biological, psychological, environmental) and on research methodology. Standing: well regarded, particularly by lecturers who want more method in the first year.

**Henry Gleitman, *Psychology*** (with Gross, Reisberg and later Nolan in subsequent editions; *Basic Psychology* from 1983). The most literate introductory text ever written, organised around big questions rather than around chapters. Standing: a minority taste and no longer the market leader, but the book most likely to make a student think psychology is interesting.

**James W. Kalat, *Biological Psychology*** (long-established; Open Library records editions from the 1990s). The standard biological psychology text: clear, accurate, well-illustrated, and good at explaining why a method can and cannot answer a question. Standing: the default. Carlson's *Physiology of Behavior* and Bear, Connors and Paradiso's *Neuroscience* are the alternatives, the latter being a genuine neuroscience text rather than a psychology text.

**John W. Santrock, *Life-Span Development***. High-coverage, high-production developmental survey. Standing: widely prescribed, criticised for breadth over depth and for very frequent new editions.

**Laura E. Berk, *Development Through the Lifespan*** (first published 1998) and ***Child Development***. Standing: **the better book**. Berk is stronger on theory, on culture and context, and on the research base; she engages seriously with Vygotsky and with cross-cultural evidence rather than treating them as sidebars. If you read one developmental text, read Berk.

**Robert J. Sternberg, *Cognitive Psychology***. Clear, well-structured coverage of attention, memory, language, reasoning and problem solving, written by a major figure who is also a critic of narrow conceptions of intelligence. Standing: solid; Eysenck and Keane's *Cognitive Psychology: A Student's Handbook* is the more thorough alternative and Goldstein's the more accessible.

**Elliot Aronson, *The Social Animal*** (first published 1972). A short, argued, beautifully written essay on social psychology rather than a textbook. Standing: a classic, and the book that made the most social psychologists. **Read it alongside the replication literature**: several of its showcase findings have been downgraded, and the Stanford Prison Experiment material in particular needs the modern critical re-examination read next to it. Aronson, Wilson and Sommers' *Social Psychology* is the full textbook.

**Ronald J. Comer, *Abnormal Psychology*** (first published 1992). Accessible, case-rich, widely prescribed in South Africa. Standing: good coverage, lighter on aetiology and mechanism.

**David H. Barlow and V. Mark Durand, *Abnormal Psychology: An Integrative Approach*** (first published 1995). Standing: **the better book on causation**, organised around an integrative multidimensional model rather than around a diagnostic list. Barlow is a major figure in anxiety-disorder treatment research and it shows.

## 2. Educational psychology texts

**Anita Woolfolk (Woolfolk Hoy), *Educational Psychology*** (first published 1987; later editions with Philip Winne and Nancy Perry for the Canadian adaptation). The single most widely prescribed educational psychology text in the English-speaking world, including in South African education faculties. Comprehensive: development, learning theories, motivation, classroom management, assessment, individual differences. Standing: **the default choice**, and the fairest single introduction to the field. Criticism: it is a survey and therefore gives roughly equal airtime to ideas of very unequal evidential standing — the reader must supply the weighting from files `03` and `06` here.

**Jeanne Ellis Ormrod, *Educational Psychology: Developing Learners*** (Ormrod's educational psychology dating from 1994). Standing: an excellent alternative to Woolfolk, arguably better organised around the learner and clearer on cognitive processes. Ormrod's *Human Learning* is the more focused companion.

**Robert E. Slavin, *Educational Psychology: Theory and Practice*** (first published 1986). Standing: strong, and distinctive for its explicit "what works" orientation — Slavin founded the Best Evidence Encyclopedia and has spent a career on evidence standards in education research. If you want an educational psychology text written by someone who cares about effect sizes and study quality, this is it.

**Dale H. Schunk, *Learning Theories: An Educational Perspective*** (first published 1991). Standing: **the best single treatment of learning theory** for practitioners — behaviourism, social cognitive theory, information processing, constructivism, motivation and self-regulation, each presented on its own terms with its instructional implications. Schunk's own work on self-efficacy and self-regulated learning is a strength of the book.

**Roger H. Bruning, Gregg J. Schraw and colleagues, *Cognitive Psychology and Instruction*** (editions from 1995 and 2003 onward). Standing: narrower and deeper than the general texts — cognitive architecture, knowledge representation, metacognition, and instruction in reading, writing, mathematics and science. The right second book after Woolfolk for anyone serious about instruction.

**Also worth having.** John Hattie and Gregory Yates, *Visible Learning and the Science of How We Learn* (2013) — Hattie's more useful book (see §6). Daniel Muijs and David Reynolds, *Effective Teaching*. Paul Kirschner and Carl Hendrick, *How Learning Happens* — a guided tour of the primary papers with commentary, which is the single most efficient way into the classic literature.

## 3. Assessment texts

**Jerome M. Sattler, *Assessment of Children: Cognitive Foundations and Applications*** and ***Foundations of Behavioral, Social, and Clinical Assessment of Children*** (Sattler's assessment volumes running from 1988; the current pair from 2014 and 2018). Standing: **the field's reference work.** Nothing else comes close for detail on administration, scoring, interpretation, report writing and the professional context. It is enormous, expensive, and organised as a reference rather than a read-through. Every practising child assessor owns it. Its limitation for southern African readers is that it is thoroughly American: the norms, the legal framework (IDEA, FAPE, IEP procedure) and the population assumptions do not transfer, and must be read alongside Foxcroft and Roodt.

**Gary Groth-Marnat and A. Jordan Wright, *Handbook of Psychological Assessment***. Standing: the standard adult-oriented assessment handbook — interviewing, behavioural assessment, the Wechsler scales, MMPI, Rorschach, projective drawings, neuropsychological screening, treatment planning and report writing. Strong on integrating data into a coherent report, which is the skill most under-taught.

**Dawn P. Flanagan, Vincent C. Alfonso and colleagues, *Essentials of WISC-V Assessment*** (2017), in the **Essentials of Psychological Assessment** series edited by Alan S. and Nadeen L. Kaufman. Standing: the practical companions to the major batteries — administration pitfalls, interpretation frameworks, cross-battery assessment (XBA), and CHC-based interpretation. There is an *Essentials* volume for nearly every major instrument and they are the fastest route to competent use of a new test. Note the authorship: the Kaufmans edit the series and wrote several volumes; the WISC-V volume is Flanagan and Alfonso's.

**Alan S. Kaufman and Elizabeth O. Lichtenberger, *Assessing Adolescent and Adult Intelligence***, and Kaufman's *Intelligent Testing with the WISC* tradition. Standing: Kaufman's "intelligent testing" philosophy — that the test is a tool for understanding the individual and that the psychologist, not the score, does the interpreting — is the intellectual foundation of modern child assessment practice, and is worth reading for that argument alone.

**Cheryl Foxcroft and Gert Roodt (eds), *An Introduction to Psychological Assessment in the South African Context*** (Roodt's *Introduction to Assessment in the South African Context* from 1999; the Foxcroft and Roodt volume in later editions). Standing: **the essential South African text and the reason this list is not simply an American one.** It covers the history of assessment in South Africa including its use under apartheid, the statutory control of psychological tests, cross-cultural test adaptation, the local instruments, and the ethical and equity dimensions of testing a multilingual population. There is no Namibian equivalent; Namibian practitioners use it.

**Sumaya Laher and Kate Cockcroft (eds), *Psychological Assessment in South Africa: Research and Applications*** (Wits University Press, 2013). Standing: the research complement to Foxcroft and Roodt — chapters on WAIS-III performance in the South African context (Shuttleworth-Edwards, Gaylard and Radloff), on the JSAIS and school readiness (Theron), and on the technical problems of assessing across the country's languages and educational histories. Verified via Crossref (doi:10.18772/22013015782).

**American Educational Research Association, American Psychological Association and National Council on Measurement in Education, *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing***. Standing: the authoritative statement of what validity, reliability and fairness require. Not a textbook; the document you cite when you need to say what good practice actually is.

**Anne Anastasi and Susana Urbina, *Psychological Testing*.** Standing: the classic psychometrics text; superseded in currency but unmatched in clarity on the fundamentals.

## 4. Statistics and research methods

**Andy Field, *Discovering Statistics Using IBM SPSS Statistics*** (editions from 2013 in Open Library's record; the series considerably older). Standing: **the dominant text in UK and southern African psychology**, and deservedly. It is funny, it explains the conceptual basis before the button-pressing, and it does not condescend. Companion volumes exist for R (*Discovering Statistics Using R*, with Miles and Field) and, more recently, for other packages. Criticism: the jokes wear on some readers, and the sheer length intimidates.

**David C. Howell, *Statistical Methods for Psychology*** (first published 1982). Standing: the sober, precise alternative. Better than Field on the mathematics and on the assumptions; less fun. *Fundamental Statistics for the Behavioral Sciences* is the shorter version.

**Barbara G. Tabachnick and Linda S. Fidell, *Using Multivariate Statistics*** (first published 1983). Standing: **the multivariate reference** — MANOVA, discriminant analysis, multiple regression, logistic regression, factor analysis, SEM, survival analysis — with the distinctive feature of worked examples in multiple software packages and unusually good chapters on data screening and assumption checking. Honours and master's level.

**Jacob Cohen, *Statistical Power Analysis for the Behavioral Sciences*** (first published 1969). Standing: the foundational work on power and effect size, and the source of the .2/.5/.8 conventions that Cohen himself warned against reifying. Read also his short papers "The earth is round (p < .05)" and "A power primer" — between them they anticipated the replication crisis by two decades.

**John W. Creswell and J. David Creswell, *Research Design: Qualitative, Quantitative, and Mixed Methods Approaches*** (first published 1994). Standing: the standard mixed-methods and design reference in education and the social sciences; clear on worldviews, designs and writing a proposal. Criticism: some methodologists find its treatment of qualitative traditions thin, treating them as a family rather than as genuinely incompatible epistemologies. Creswell and Poth's *Qualitative Inquiry and Research Design* is the fuller qualitative treatment.

**Virginia Braun and Victoria Clarke, *Thematic Analysis: A Practical Guide*** (2021). Standing: **essential**, and it exists largely because their enormously cited 2006 paper was so widely misapplied. This book is where they insist on the distinction between reflexive TA, codebook TA and coding-reliability TA, and where they explain why calculating inter-rater reliability within reflexive TA is a conceptual error. Anyone doing qualitative work in psychology should read it before, not after, collecting data.

**Jonathan A. Smith, Paul Flowers and Michael Larkin, *Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis: Theory, Method and Research*** (2009); Smith and Nizza, *Essentials of Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis* (2021). Standing: the definitive statements of IPA by its originators.

**Kathy Charmaz, *Constructing Grounded Theory***. Standing: the version of grounded theory most used in psychology; the constructivist turn away from Glaser's positivism and Strauss and Corbin's proceduralism.

**Jonathan Potter and Margaret Wetherell, *Discourse and Social Psychology***. Standing: the founding text of discursive psychology.

## 5. Theory primary sources

Read these for orientation and intellectual honesty, in extract rather than entire. The annotation that matters for each is *what has survived*.

**Jean Piaget** — *The Origin of Intelligence in the Child* (1952 English translation), *The Child's Conception of the World* (1929), *The Psychology of the Child* with Bärbel Inhelder. Standing: **historically foundational, empirically substantially revised.** The stages underestimate infant and young-child competence because the tasks confounded reasoning with language, memory and pragmatics (file `03`). What survives: the qualitative-difference insight, the clinical interview method, assimilation and accommodation, and the idea that conceptual change requires disequilibrium. Read a chapter of the primary text and a good secondary account of the critiques.

**Lev Vygotsky, *Mind in Society: The Development of Higher Psychological Processes*** (1978). Standing: **the most influential single volume in modern educational psychology**, and short. Note that it is an edited compilation of Vygotsky's writings assembled posthumously by Cole, John-Steiner, Scribner and Souberman, not a book Vygotsky wrote — a fact that matters when people quote "Vygotsky said". *Thought and Language* (*Thinking and Speech*) is the other essential. What survives: the ZPD, the social origins of higher mental function, mediation by tools and signs, and inner speech. What is thin: the empirical base is small and the ZPD has never been operationalised in a way that satisfies psychometricians.

**Jerome Bruner, *The Process of Education*** (1960), *Toward a Theory of Instruction*, *Acts of Meaning*. Standing: enormously influential on curriculum design (the spiral curriculum; enactive-iconic-symbolic representation); the discovery-learning advocacy has been substantially qualified by the cognitive-load literature, and Bruner himself moderated it. His later cultural-psychology turn is under-read and worth reading.

**Albert Bandura, *Social Foundations of Thought and Action: A Social Cognitive Theory*** (1985/1986) and *Self-Efficacy: The Exercise of Control* (1997). Standing: **among the best-supported theoretical positions in psychology.** Self-efficacy is measurable, manipulable, domain-specific and predictive. Bandura is also unusually clear about the difference between self-efficacy and self-esteem, a distinction that most school programmes get wrong. Caveat: the Bobo doll studies' relevance to real-world media violence is more contested than the textbook accounts suggest.

**Urie Bronfenbrenner, *The Ecology of Human Development: Experiments by Nature and Design*** (1979). Standing: **the framework most educational psychologists actually think in.** Micro-, meso-, exo-, macro- and later chronosystem. Note that Bronfenbrenner substantially revised the theory late in his life into the **bioecological model** with its process-person-context-time (PPCT) formulation, and complained that people were citing the 1979 version and ignoring the person and process elements. Cite the later work.

**B. F. Skinner, *Science and Human Behavior*** (1953), *The Technology of Teaching*, *Verbal Behavior*. Standing: the technology of behaviour change works and is the basis of applied behaviour analysis; the philosophical programme (radical behaviourism as a complete account of mind) does not survive Chomsky's critique of *Verbal Behavior* or the cognitive revolution. Read Skinner for the analysis of contingencies, which is genuinely useful in classrooms, not for the philosophy.

**Carl Rogers, *On Becoming a Person*** (1961), *Client-Centered Therapy*, *Freedom to Learn*. Standing: the therapeutic conditions — unconditional positive regard, empathy, congruence — are the ancestor of the modern common-factors literature and are well supported as ingredients of therapeutic alliance. The claim that they are *sufficient* is not supported. *Freedom to Learn* is Rogers on education and is the most romantic and least evidenced part of the corpus.

**John Bowlby, *Attachment and Loss*** (vol. 1, 1969; vols 2 and 3 following). Standing: the theory reoriented developmental psychology and remains central. What has changed: the strong maternal-deprivation claims of the 1950s were overstated and were used politically; multiple attachments are normal; and the predictive power of infant attachment classification for later outcomes is real but modest.

**Mary D. S. Ainsworth, Mary Blehar, Everett Waters and Sally Wall, *Patterns of Attachment*** (1978). Standing: the Strange Situation and the secure/avoidant/ambivalent classification system, with Main and Solomon's disorganised category added later. Essential and genuinely empirical. Caveat: the Strange Situation's cross-cultural validity is contested — distribution of classifications varies substantially across cultures, and whether that reflects real differences in attachment or an ethnocentric procedure remains argued.

**Also.** Reuven Feuerstein on mediated learning experience and the LPAD — important in southern Africa (file `04`). Howard Gardner, *Frames of Mind* (1983) — read it to understand what he actually claimed, then read the psychometric critique. Lawrence Kohlberg on moral development, with Carol Gilligan's *In a Different Voice* as the standard counterweight.

## 6. Modern evidence-based teaching literature

This is the section to give a teacher, a head of department or a school governing body. It is short, it is readable, and it is the highest-yield reading in this file.

**John Hattie, *Visible Learning*** (2009, Routledge) and *Visible Learning for Teachers* (2012). Standing: **enormously influential and legitimately contested.** Hattie synthesised hundreds of meta-analyses into ranked "effect sizes" for influences on achievement, with a "hinge point" of d = 0.40. The critiques are serious and should be known: averaging effect sizes across meta-analyses with incompatible outcome measures, designs, ages and comparison conditions is statistically indefensible; the CLE (common language effect) statistic was mis-derived in early editions and produced impossible values; effect sizes drawn from studies with very different standard deviations are not comparable; and the ranking invites exactly the "just do the top ten" reading Hattie disclaims. Use it as a **map of what has been studied**, not as a league table. His *Visible Learning and the Science of How We Learn* with Gregory Yates (2013) is the better and more careful book.

**Daniel T. Willingham, *Why Don't Students Like School?*** Standing: **the best short book on cognitive science for teachers.** Nine principles, each a chapter, each grounded in well-replicated findings — memory is the residue of thought; factual knowledge precedes skill; we understand new things in the context of what we already know; proficiency requires practice; cognition early in training differs from cognition late in training. Willingham is also the author of one of the definitive debunkings of learning styles (Willingham, Hughes and Dobolyi, "The scientific status of learning styles theories", *Teaching of Psychology*, 2015). His *When Can You Trust the Experts?* and *The Reading Mind* are also worth having.

**Peter C. Brown, Henry L. Roediger III and Mark A. McDaniel, *Make It Stick: The Science of Successful Learning*** (2014). Standing: **the best popular treatment of retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving and desirable difficulties**, written by two of the researchers who did the work. Reliable because the underlying findings are among the most replicated in the field. The one caution is the usual popular-book compression: the classroom-transfer evidence is thinner than the laboratory evidence, and the book does not dwell on that.

**John Sweller, Paul Ayres and Slava Kalyuga, *Cognitive Load Theory*** (2011). Standing: the systematic statement of CLT by its originators. The primary paper is Sweller, "Cognitive load during problem solving: effects on learning", *Cognitive Science* 12:257–285 (1988). Also essential: Kirschner, Sweller and Clark, "Why minimal guidance during instruction does not work", *Educational Psychologist* 41:75–86 (2006) — read it with the Hmelo-Silver, Duncan and Chinn reply, because the honest position is about degree of guidance for whom and when. Oliver Lovell's *Sweller's Cognitive Load Theory in Action* (2020) is the practitioner translation.

**Deans for Impact, *The Science of Learning***. A free six-page summary of cognitive-science principles with classroom implications, organised around questions teachers actually ask. **A second edition was released on 19 May 2026**, adding research from the last decade, a treatment of pitfalls teachers should be aware of, and a new section on supporting students to self-regulate their learning. Standing: the single best free document to hand a teacher.

**Education Endowment Foundation, *Teaching and Learning Toolkit*** and its guidance reports. Standing: **the most useful evidence resource in school education**, precisely because it reports cost, evidence security and months-of-progress alongside effect estimates, and because the EEF also runs and publishes its own randomised trials — including trials that found no effect, such as the *Changing Mindsets* effectiveness trial (2019). The EEF's willingness to publish null results is what makes it trustworthy. Caveat: months-of-progress conversions are a communication device with real statistical looseness behind them.

**Barak Rosenshine, *Principles of Instruction*** (published by the International Academy of Education / IBE-UNESCO and in *American Educator*, 2012). Standing: ten principles derived from process-product research, cognitive science and the study of expert teachers — daily review, small steps, questioning, models, guided practice, checking for understanding, high success rates, scaffolds, independent practice, weekly and monthly review. Short, free, and the most-quoted document in English school CPD.

**Paul A. Kirschner and Carl Hendrick, *How Learning Happens*** and *How Teaching Happens*. Standing: annotated tours of the seminal papers, which is exactly what a busy practitioner needs.

**Dylan Wiliam, *Embedded Formative Assessment***. Standing: the practical statement of formative assessment. Wiliam is also usefully honest about the limits of his own earlier claims about effect sizes for formative assessment.

**Doug Lemov, *Teach Like a Champion***. Standing: not psychology, and it makes no research claims of its own — it is a taxonomy of observed techniques from high-performing US charter schools. Useful as a repertoire; contested on cultural and pedagogical grounds; the techniques' evidence base is largely absent.

## 7. Clinical and diagnostic references

**American Psychiatric Association, *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR)*** (2022). Standing: the operative diagnostic reference in South African, Namibian and US practice. Read it as a **classification for communication and administration**, not as a theory of mental disorder. The standing critiques are substantial and worth knowing: category boundaries are conventions; comorbidity rates suggest the categories carve badly; the removal of the bereavement exclusion and successive broadenings of criteria attract diagnostic-inflation criticism; the field trials produced modest kappa values for several major categories; and the manual's cultural assumptions are those of its authors, only partly addressed by the Cultural Formulation Interview. Alternatives and complements to know about: **RDoC** (the NIMH Research Domain Criteria), **HiTOP** (the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology), the **Psychodynamic Diagnostic Manual**, and the **Power Threat Meaning Framework** published by the BPS Division of Clinical Psychology, which is an explicit non-diagnostic alternative.

**World Health Organization, *ICD-11* (International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision)**. Stable version released 18 June 2018, endorsed by the 72nd World Health Assembly on 28 May 2019, **officially came into effect on 1 January 2022**, updated annually (release 2026-01 published 16 February 2026). Distributed under a Creative Commons licence and freely browsable — which matters enormously in resource-constrained settings where the DSM's price is prohibitive. Standing: the global standard for health statistics and the classification most southern African public health systems formally use. Its mental-health chapter differs from DSM-5-TR in several respects, notably its treatment of complex PTSD (which ICD-11 recognises and DSM-5-TR does not) and its dimensional personality-disorder model. Implementation is uneven: WHO reported 64 countries at various stages of implementation in February 2023.

**Also.** The **DSM-5-TR Handbook of Differential Diagnosis**; **Sattler's** behavioural/clinical volume (§3) for the child-specific version; and, for medication questions that psychologists must understand but not answer, a current paediatric psychopharmacology reference — checked against current prescribing guidance, because this is exactly the material that dates.

## 8. Significant popular works — read with the critique

These matter because clients, teachers, principals and HR managers have read them and will cite them at you. Knowing the book *and* its evidential standing is a professional competence.

**Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*** (2011). Standing: **the most important popular psychology book of the century so far, and partly obsolete.** The heuristics-and-biases research programme (with Amos Tversky) is Nobel-recognised and largely sound; the System 1 / System 2 framing is a useful metaphor that Kahneman was careful to label as such. **But**: the priming chapter has not survived — Kahneman himself wrote an open letter in 2012 warning of a "train wreck looming" in social priming research, and later publicly acknowledged that he had placed too much faith in underpowered studies in that chapter. Ego depletion, also discussed favourably, has failed large-scale replication. Read it, and read the chapter-level replication annotations that circulate alongside it.

**Carol S. Dweck, *Mindset: The New Psychology of Success*** (2006). Standing: **enormously influential, substantially downgraded.** See file `03`. Sisk et al.'s two meta-analyses (*Psychological Science*, 2018, doi:10.1177/0956797617739704) found weak overall mindset-achievement relationships and small intervention effects concentrated in at-risk and low-SES students; Macnamara and Burgoyne (*Psychological Bulletin*, 2023) found little to no effect after accounting for study quality; the EEF's *Changing Mindsets* trial did not find the expected gains. The underlying construct is real; the school-improvement claim is not supported at the strength the book implies. Additional problem: "false growth mindset" — the widespread practice of praising effort regardless of strategy or outcome — is a misreading that Dweck has repeatedly disowned, and it is what most schools actually implement.

**Angela Duckworth, *Grit: The Power of Passion and Perseverance*** (2016). Standing: **downgraded.** Credé, Tynan and Harms, "Much ado about grit" (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 113:492–511, 2017, doi:10.1037/pspp0000102) found grit is largely redundant with conscientiousness, that the perseverance-of-effort facet carries nearly all the predictive weight while consistency-of-interest carries little, and that incremental validity over established predictors is small. Duckworth has engaged with the critique more openly than most. There is also a fair political criticism: grit narratives can shift responsibility for structural educational disadvantage onto the individual child, which is a live issue in South African schooling.

**Susan Cain, *Quiet: The Power of Introverts in a World That Can't Stop Talking*** (2012). Standing: **the most defensible book in this section.** Introversion-extraversion is a well-established trait dimension with a solid measurement base, and Cain's core claims — that group brainstorming underperforms individual ideation, that open-plan offices impose costs, that classroom and workplace norms are built around extraverted defaults — have reasonable empirical support. Caveats: the book at times treats a continuous dimension as a typology, and it draws confident conclusions from some contested findings (the "highly sensitive person" literature, in particular, is weaker than presented). Directly relevant to domains `01` and `19` on the design of learning and work environments.

**Malcolm Gladwell** — *Outliers* (2008), *Blink*, *The Tipping Point*, *Talking to Strangers*. Standing: **read for what your clients believe, and be ready to correct it.** Gladwell is an exceptional storyteller who systematically over-claims from single studies. The "10,000-hour rule" is a misreading of Ericsson's deliberate-practice research — Ericsson publicly objected, the figure was an average rather than a threshold, and subsequent meta-analysis (Macnamara and colleagues) found deliberate practice explains a modest fraction of performance variance in most domains and very little in some. *Blink*'s thin-slicing chapters lean heavily on findings that have since been questioned. *The Tipping Point*'s "law of the few" has little support. This is not a reason to be contemptuous of him — it is a reason to know precisely where the seams are.

**Also in this category.** Richard Nisbett, *Mindware: Tools for Smart Thinking* (2015) — a better and more accurate book than most in this section, by a serious researcher, on the reasoning tools of file `09`. Steven Pinker, *The Blank Slate* and *Rationality* — argued, contested, worth reading with a critical eye. Scott Lilienfeld, Steven Jay Lynn, John Ruscio and Barry Beyerstein, ***50 Great Myths of Popular Psychology*** (2010) — the indispensable corrective, and the book that puts learning styles at Myth #18. Stuart Ritchie, *Science Fictions* — the best popular account of the replication crisis. Chris Chambers, *The Seven Deadly Sins of Psychology* — the same territory from inside methodology reform. Jesse Singal, *The Quick Fix* — a critical history of exactly the intervention fashions covered here (self-esteem, grit, mindset, implicit bias, power posing).

## 9. A suggested reading order

**Before undergraduate study:** Willingham, *Why Don't Students Like School?*; Lilienfeld et al., *50 Great Myths*.

**During undergraduate study:** the survey text your department prescribes, cover to cover; Berk on development; Field on statistics; Aronson, *The Social Animal*; Barlow and Durand on psychopathology; and Ritchie's *Science Fictions* alongside all of it.

**Honours year:** Schunk, *Learning Theories*; Foxcroft and Roodt; Tabachnick and Fidell by chapter; Braun and Clarke; Cohen's short papers.

**Professional master's:** Woolfolk or Ormrod; Sattler (both volumes, as reference); the relevant *Essentials* volume for every instrument you use; DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 as look-up; Vygotsky, *Mind in Society*; Bandura on self-efficacy; Bronfenbrenner's bioecological papers.

**In practice, continuously:** the EEF toolkit and guidance reports; Deans for Impact; Rosenshine; Dunlosky et al. 2013; Hattie and Yates; and the current issues of *Educational Psychology in Practice*, *School Psychology*, *Journal of Educational Psychology*, *South African Journal of Psychology*, *South African Journal of Education* and *Perspectives in Education*.
