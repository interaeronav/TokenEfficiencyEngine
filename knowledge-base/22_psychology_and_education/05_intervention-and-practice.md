# Intervention and practice — what the educational psychologist actually does

**Summary.** The caricature of the educational psychologist is someone who administers an IQ test and writes a report. The reality, in every well-functioning service, is that direct assessment is a minority of the work and **consultation is the core activity** — because the psychologist sees the child for four hours and the teacher sees them for a thousand. The four functions the literature identifies are assessment, consultation and counselling (of learners, parents, teachers and school leadership), community-level intervention (corrective, preventive and optimising), and referral with coordination. This file covers what each of those actually involves, and then what happens to all of it when the school has 55 children in a classroom and no library.

## Key facts

| Activity | Typical share of time in a well-resourced service | Typical share in southern African public-sector practice |
|---|---|---|
| Individual assessment | 25–40% | Often 60%+, driven by placement and concession demand |
| Consultation with teachers/schools | 25–40% | Squeezed |
| Direct therapeutic work with learners | 10–25% | Low; usually referred out |
| Parent work | 10–15% | Low |
| Systems, training, policy, supervision | 10–20% | Low |
| Crisis response | Episodic | Episodic but frequent |

> ⚠️ Figures in the table are practitioner-typical impressions, **not** sourced survey data. Treat as orientation, not evidence.

## 1. Consultation models

Consultation is indirect service delivery: the psychologist works with an adult (teacher, parent, manager) who works with the child. It is the highest-leverage activity available and the one most often done badly, because it requires the psychologist to give up the expert role.

**Mental health consultation (Caplan).** Four types: client-centred case consultation (about this child), consultee-centred case consultation (about the teacher's difficulty with this kind of child — the most interesting and most delicate), programme-centred administrative consultation, and consultee-centred administrative consultation. Caplan's insight — that consultee "lack of objectivity" is often the real obstacle, and must be addressed indirectly without turning the consultation into therapy for the teacher — remains the sharpest thing written on the subject.

**Behavioural consultation (Bergan and Kratochwill).** A four-stage problem-solving sequence: problem identification (define the behaviour operationally and get baseline data), problem analysis (functional assessment — antecedents, consequences, setting events), plan implementation (with integrity checks), and plan evaluation (against the baseline). Its virtue is that it is testable; its weakness is that it can proceed without the consultee ever agreeing that the problem is what the psychologist says it is.

**Collaborative / conjoint behavioural consultation.** Parent and teacher in the room together with the psychologist as facilitator, working the same problem-solving sequence across both settings. Better generalisation, more scheduling difficulty.

**Solution-focused and systemic consultation.** Drawn from brief therapy: exception-finding ("when does this *not* happen?"), scaling questions, the miracle question, amplifying what already works. Fits well with time-poor school contexts and with a consultation stance that assumes competence in the consultee.

**Non-negotiables of good consultation.** Coordinate rather than command; be clear whether the consultation is voluntary; establish who owns the problem and who owns the plan; specify observable goals; check **implementation integrity** before concluding that an intervention failed; and follow up, because unfollowed-up advice is not consultation, it is opinion.

## 2. Response to intervention and multi-tiered systems of support

**RTI** is a tiered service-delivery framework that emerged in the US as an alternative to the discrepancy model for identifying specific learning disability. The logic: provide high-quality core instruction to everyone, monitor progress, escalate support for non-responders, and use the response itself as diagnostic information.

- **Tier 1 — universal.** Evidence-based core instruction for all learners, with universal screening (typically three times a year). Target: roughly 80% of learners adequately served here. If far fewer are, the problem is the core instruction, not the children — the single most valuable question RTI forces a school to ask.
- **Tier 2 — targeted.** Small-group supplementary intervention, standard-protocol or problem-solving, for perhaps 15% of learners, with more frequent progress monitoring.
- **Tier 3 — intensive.** Individualised, high-frequency, often individual intervention for around 5%, with continuous data collection; comprehensive evaluation where indicated.

**MTSS** is the broader successor framework, integrating academic RTI with behavioural supports (SWPBIS) and social-emotional learning into one tiered structure.

**Honest appraisal.** RTI's logic is sound and its emphasis on data and on interrogating core instruction is valuable. The empirical record is more mixed than the enthusiasm: a large-scale US federal evaluation found that in some sites students assigned to Tier 2 reading intervention did **worse** than similar students who were not, which is a serious finding about implementation quality and about who gets assigned. Fidelity of implementation is the recurring problem; "we do RTI" frequently means "we have a spreadsheet".

**[ZA]** The nearest local analogue is the **SIAS (Screening, Identification, Assessment and Support)** process, which sits under Education White Paper 6 and routes support through the school-level support team, then the district-based support team, escalating only where school-level support is insufficient. The structural logic is the same as MTSS; the resourcing is not.

## 3. Individual education plans

An IEP (called by various names — ISP, individual support plan, learner support plan) is the written, agreed, reviewable statement of what will be done differently for one learner. A usable one contains:

- **Present levels of performance** — specific and measured, not adjectival.
- **Goals** that are observable and time-bound, with the measurement method named. "Will read 80 words per minute with ≤3 errors on unseen grade-level text by the end of Term 3" is a goal; "will improve reading" is a wish.
- **Accommodations** (change how the learner accesses or demonstrates learning — extra time, scribe, reader, separate venue, enlarged print, oral examination) versus **modifications** (change what is learned or assessed). The distinction is critical because accommodations preserve the standard while modifications do not, and examination authorities treat them very differently.
- **Who does what, when, and with what resources.**
- **Review date and review data.**

**[ZA]** Formal examination concessions in the National Senior Certificate are governed by the examination authorities' own rules and require specified assessment evidence within specified timeframes. **Requirements and deadlines change annually — verify against the current Department of Basic Education and Umalusi circulars before advising a family.** Nothing about the concession process is stated here as settled.

The commonest failures: the plan is written by the psychologist alone; the teacher who must implement it was not in the room; there is no baseline so change cannot be detected; there is no review; and the accommodations are stacked without evidence of need, which erodes the credibility of the whole mechanism for the learners who genuinely require it.

## 4. Counselling approaches with children and adolescents

Educational psychologists in South Africa are trained in and permitted to do therapeutic work; the scope is developmental, educational and adjustment-focused rather than the treatment of severe psychopathology, which is referred.

**Cognitive behavioural therapy.** The strongest evidence base for child and adolescent anxiety and depression. Developmentally adapted: more behavioural and less cognitive with younger children, heavy use of externalising language, drawing, worksheets and games, shorter sessions, and parental involvement as co-therapist. Core components — psychoeducation, emotion identification, cognitive restructuring, graded exposure for anxiety, behavioural activation for depression, problem solving, relapse prevention. Manualised programmes (Coping Cat and its adaptations, FRIENDS) have good trial evidence, with the usual caveat that efficacy in trials exceeds effectiveness in routine practice.

**Play therapy.** For children roughly 3–11 whose verbal capacity does not support talking therapy. Non-directive/child-centred (Axline, following Rogers) versus directive and structured approaches; sand tray; puppets; art. The evidence base is weaker and methodologically poorer than for CBT — meta-analyses report moderate effects but the trials are small, often unblinded, and heterogeneous. It is a defensible modality, especially where the alternative is no engagement at all, and it should not be presented as equivalently evidenced.

**Narrative therapy** (White and Epston). Externalising the problem ("the Anger", "Sneaky Poo"), identifying unique outcomes, re-authoring the preferred story, definitional ceremony and outsider-witness practices, letters and documents. It fits children well and fits multicultural, post-colonial contexts well because it explicitly resists locating pathology inside the individual. Its evidence base is largely qualitative and case-based; strong on acceptability and fit, thin on controlled trials.

**Solution-focused brief therapy** (de Shazer, Berg). Pre-session change, the miracle question, exception-finding, scaling, small next steps, compliments. Very well suited to school settings because it is brief, non-pathologising and consistent with a consultation stance. Evidence is modest but real, particularly for behaviour and school-based problems.

**Family systems approaches.** Structural (Minuchin — boundaries, subsystems, hierarchy, enactment), strategic, Milan systemic, and Bowen's differentiation and multigenerational transmission. The essential insight for school work is that a child's difficulty frequently expresses something in the family or in the school-family relationship, and that intervening only with the child is often intervening with the wrong unit. Genograms and ecomaps are standard practical tools.

**Bio-ecological framing.** Bronfenbrenner's nested systems (micro-, meso-, exo-, macro-, chrono-) is the framework most educational psychologists use to decide *where* to intervene. A child failing at school because the household has no electricity after 6 p.m. does not need a mindset intervention.

**Group work.** Social skills groups, anxiety groups, bereavement groups, study-skills groups. Efficient, and often the only realistic delivery mode at scale. Watch for **deviancy training** — the well-documented iatrogenic effect of aggregating antisocial adolescents in groups, which can make outcomes worse.

## 5. Career guidance

A large and often under-rated part of the role, particularly **[ZA]/[NA]** where it is one of the services schools actually request.

**Theoretical bases.** Holland's RIASEC typology (realistic, investigative, artistic, social, enterprising, conventional) and person–environment fit; Super's life-span, life-space theory and vocational self-concept, with its stages and the life-career rainbow; Krumboltz's happenstance learning theory, which is the corrective to over-planned career advice — much of career development is unplanned and the skill to teach is capitalising on chance; Savickas's career construction and life-design approach, which is narrative in method and fits well with the narrative therapy tradition above.

**Instruments.** Interest inventories, aptitude batteries, values card sorts, personality measures. The same psychometric cautions from file `04` apply with extra force: interest inventories in particular reflect exposure and opportunity, so a rural learner who has never encountered a profession will not express interest in it. Norms matter; so does the difference between measured aptitude and developed skill.

**The southern African realities that must shape the advice.** Subject-choice decisions in Grade 9 are close to irreversible and are often made with poor information; Mathematics versus Mathematical Literacy is the single highest-stakes choice a South African learner makes and closes most STEM and many commerce pathways if chosen wrongly. Admission point scores, NBT requirements, bursary and NSFAS deadlines, TVET and apprenticeship routes, and the actual labour market (rather than the aspirational one) all belong in the conversation. **Verify all admission and funding requirements against the current institutional prospectus — they change every year and none are quoted here.**

Career guidance also has an equity dimension: the quality of career information a learner receives is strongly patterned by school resourcing, and a psychologist working in an under-served school is often the only source of accurate information the learner will get.

## 6. Working with schools and teachers

**Reading the school as a system.** Leadership style and stability; staff turnover and morale; discipline policy on paper versus in practice; how the timetable actually works; the physical environment; the relationship between the school and its community; whether the school-based support team meets and whether anyone reads its minutes.

**Teacher work.** In-service training, but the evidence is clear that one-off workshops change little — sustained coaching with classroom observation and feedback is what changes practice. Modelling in the classroom. Co-planning a differentiated lesson rather than describing differentiation. Communities of practice.

**Teacher wellbeing.** Burnout (emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation, reduced accomplishment), secondary traumatic stress in schools serving high-violence communities, and the simple fact that an exhausted teacher cannot implement any plan. This is a legitimate target of the psychologist's work, and it is frequently the highest-yield one.

**Whole-school projects.** Anti-bullying programmes (the Olweus family and its derivatives have the best evidence, and the evidence is that programmes work modestly and that whole-school, sustained, adult-supervision-heavy approaches beat curriculum-only ones), social-emotional learning curricula, peer support and peer mediation, and transition programmes between phases.

## 7. Parent work

Feedback of assessment results is a therapeutic act, not an administrative one. Parents remember how they were told for decades. Practical rules: give the headline early rather than building to it; use the child's name; describe strengths concretely and not as a softener; explain scores as ranges; check understanding by asking the parent to say back what they will tell their partner; write down the three things that happen next.

**Behavioural parent training** (Triple P, Incredible Years, Parent-Child Interaction Therapy) has among the strongest evidence bases in child mental health for disruptive behaviour, and it is delivered by a wide range of practitioners. Cultural adaptation is necessary and has been done in African contexts, notably the **Parenting for Lifelong Health** programmes developed with WHO/UNICEF involvement and trialled in South Africa — *verify current programme details and evidence directly*.

**Cross-cultural competence in parent work** means, concretely: knowing who in this family makes decisions about the child; understanding fostering and extended-family caregiving arrangements, which are common in southern Africa and are not pathology; recognising that a caregiver may be a grandmother, an aunt, or an older sibling; working through an interpreter properly (brief them, speak to the parent not the interpreter, allow double the time, never use the child as interpreter); and not mistaking a different explanatory model of illness or misfortune for denial.

## 8. Crisis and trauma response

**Critical incident response in schools** — a learner or teacher death, a suicide, a violent incident, an accident, a community disaster. The current evidence-informed approach:

- **Psychological first aid** (contact and engagement, safety and comfort, stabilisation, information gathering, practical assistance, connection with social supports, information on coping, linkage with services) rather than one-off psychological debriefing. **Single-session critical incident stress debriefing is not supported by the evidence and may increase symptoms in some people**; this is one of the clearest "stop doing this" findings in the field.
- **Restore routine quickly.** Structure is the intervention.
- **Identify and monitor the higher-risk** — those closest to the event, those with prior loss or trauma, those with existing difficulties — and follow up over weeks rather than treating everyone immediately.
- **Suicide postvention** — follow safe-messaging guidance, avoid memorialisation practices that glamorise, screen and support the deceased's close contacts, brief staff.
- Most people are resilient. Universal mandatory counselling after an incident is not indicated.

**Trauma-focused work.** TF-CBT has the strongest evidence base for children with PTSD symptoms. **[ZA]/[NA]** the context includes very high rates of exposure to community and domestic violence, sexual abuse, HIV-related bereavement and orphanhood, and chronic rather than single-incident trauma — which is a poor fit for single-incident treatment models and requires the sustained, safety-first, relationship-based approach that complex trauma demands.

> ⚠️ **Mandatory reporting overrides confidentiality.** **[ZA]** Children's Act 38 of 2005 s.110(1) names psychologists among the professionals who **must** report a reasonable-grounds conclusion of physical abuse causing injury, sexual abuse or deliberate neglect. **[NA]** Child Care and Protection Act 3 of 2015 s.132 imposes the duty on any person performing professional duties with respect to children — psychologists and psychological counsellors are named explicitly — and s.132(6) makes failure an offence carrying a fine up to **N$20 000** or up to **five years'** imprisonment, or both. Section 132(4) expressly overrides privilege other than legal professional privilege. See file `07`.

## 9. Practice in under-resourced schools

This is where most southern African educational psychology actually happens, and a file that ignores it is describing someone else's job.

**The conditions.** Class sizes of 45 to 60 and sometimes more. Teachers who are themselves teaching in their second or third language. Learners taught to read in a language they do not speak at home. No school library, no textbooks per learner, sometimes no electricity. Long travel distances and irregular attendance. Hunger — a hungry child does not have working memory to spare. Household instability, child-headed households, caregiver illness. Violence in the community and sometimes in the school. No school psychologist post at all: **[ZA]** the district-based support teams envisaged by White Paper 6 exist unevenly and are chronically understaffed; **[NA]** the position is more constrained still, with a national profession of a few dozen registered psychologists.

**What follows for practice.**

1. **Individual assessment does not scale and is often the wrong tool.** Assessing one child in a school where the whole cohort is two years behind in reading answers a question nobody needed answered. The first question is always whether this is an individual problem or an instructional one.
2. **Prioritise Tier 1.** Improving core reading instruction for 800 learners beats a full battery on eight of them.
3. **Build capacity in people who are already there.** Teachers, school-based support teams, community health workers, NGO staff, **[ZA]** Registered Counsellors. Train and supervise rather than deliver.
4. **Use free and low-burden instruments.** The SDQ is free. Curriculum-based measurement costs a stopwatch and a photocopy. Structured observation costs nothing.
5. **Triage explicitly and defensibly.** Safeguarding first, then risk of harm, then irreversible educational decisions (subject choice, retention, placement), then everything else. Write the triage rule down so it is not made ad hoc under pressure.
6. **Do not pathologise poverty.** The differential diagnosis for a learner who cannot concentrate includes hunger, untreated hearing loss, undiagnosed visual impairment, sleep deprivation from an overcrowded home, chronic illness, and being taught in a language they do not understand — before ADHD. Vision and hearing screening is a serious and neglected intervention.
7. **Know the referral map and its real capacity.** Which clinic has a psychiatric nurse; which hospital has a paediatrician; which NGO takes trauma referrals; how long the waiting list is. A referral to a service with a two-year waiting list is not a plan.
8. **Sustain yourself.** Caseloads are impossible, supervision is scarce, and burnout among practitioners in these settings is the norm rather than the exception. Peer supervision arrangements and firm limits are professional obligations, not indulgences.
