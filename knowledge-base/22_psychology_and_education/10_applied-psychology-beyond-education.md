# Applied psychology beyond education — the adjacent fields worth knowing

**Summary.** Four applied branches of psychology bear directly on other domains in this knowledge base: **industrial/organisational psychology** (selection, performance, motivation, teams and leadership — domain `12_hr_construction`), **human factors and ergonomics** (equipment, interfaces, error and safety — domains `02` and `09`), **environmental psychology** (how built space affects behaviour, cognition and wellbeing — domains `01_architecture`, `16`, `17`, `19_interior_design`), and **behavioural economics** (how people actually decide). **Health psychology** is the fifth, less directly connected but structurally similar. Each has a real evidence base and each has a well-populated layer of fashionable claims sitting on top of it; the distinction is drawn below.

## Key facts

| Finding | Standing |
|---|---|
| Structured selection procedures outperform unstructured interviews | **Robust**, though the size of the advantage has been revised downward |
| Validity estimates for selection methods (Schmidt & Hunter 1998) | **Substantially revised downward** by Sackett, Zhang, Berry & Lievens (2022) — systematic overcorrection for range restriction |
| Assessment centres predict performance but do not measure the dimensions they claim | **Well established** — the "exercise effect" / construct-validity problem |
| Nature views aid recovery and restoration (Ulrich 1984; Kaplan ART) | **Directionally supported**, with heterogeneous effects and small original samples |
| Noise impairs children's reading comprehension | **Well supported**, including South African evidence |
| Biophilic design improves wellbeing | **Directionally supported, weakly quantified**; much of the commercial literature over-claims |
| Nudges produce large behaviour change at scale | **Downgraded** — publication bias and a large gap between academic and real-world effect sizes |

## 1. Industrial/organisational psychology

I-O psychology (called work and organisational psychology in Europe, occupational psychology in the UK) studies behaviour in workplaces. **[ZA]** it is a full HPCSA registration category with a professional master's and internship, which is unusual internationally — in most countries it is an unlicensed applied specialism.

### 1.1 Selection and assessment

**Job analysis** comes first and is skipped constantly. Before you can select, you must know what the job requires: task-oriented analysis (what is done), worker-oriented analysis (what KSAOs — knowledge, skills, abilities and other characteristics — are needed), critical incidents technique, and competency modelling. **Everything downstream — the interview questions, the tests, the rating scales, and the legal defensibility of the whole process — depends on this step.**

**Selection methods and their validity.** For twenty-five years the reference point was **Schmidt and Hunter, "The validity and utility of selection methods in personnel psychology"** (*Psychological Bulletin* 124:262–274, 1998, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.124.2.262), which reported general mental ability as the strongest single predictor with structured interviews and work samples close behind.

> ⚠️ **That table has been substantially revised.** Sackett, Zhang, Berry and Lievens, "Revisiting meta-analytic estimates of validity in personnel selection: addressing systematic overcorrection for restriction of range" (*Journal of Applied Psychology* 107:2040–2068, 2022, doi:10.1037/apl0000994), showed that the older estimates applied range-restriction corrections that were systematically too large. The revised picture puts **structured interviews at the top**, with cognitive ability lower than previously believed and several methods reordered. Anyone citing the 1998 numbers is citing a superseded table. The qualitative conclusions — structure beats no structure, job-relevant sampling beats general impressions, combining a small number of valid predictors beats any single one — survive intact.

**Practical hierarchy for a construction or trades employer** (see `12_hr_construction/03`): **work samples and trade tests** first, because they are direct behaviour samples and are readily defensible; **structured behavioural interviews** with predetermined job-relevant questions and anchored rating scales; **verified credentials** checked with the issuing authority; **integrity and conscientiousness measures** where legally permissible; and **unstructured interviews last**, because they add little and import bias. Note that **[ZA]** the Employment Equity Act's requirements around psychological testing — that a test must be scientifically shown to be valid and reliable, applied fairly, and not biased against any employee or group — makes the instrument-validity questions in file `04` a legal matter, not merely a technical one.

**Assessment centres.** Multiple exercises (in-basket, group discussion, role-play, presentation, case analysis) rated by multiple trained assessors against defined dimensions, with a wash-up integration session. They **predict job performance reasonably well** and are widely used for management selection and development. The well-established problem is **construct validity**: variance in ratings clusters by exercise rather than by dimension (the "exercise effect"), meaning an assessment centre measures something real but not the dimensional profile it reports. Practical implication: use them for overall prediction and for development feedback, and be sceptical of the dimension-level "leadership 3.2, communication 4.1" reports they generate.

### 1.2 Performance management

Rating formats — graphic rating scales, behaviourally anchored rating scales (BARS), behavioural observation scales, forced distribution — and the rating errors that plague all of them: halo, leniency and severity, central tendency, recency, similar-to-me, and contrast. **Rater training** on frame-of-reference produces more improvement than any change of format. 360-degree feedback is useful developmentally and unreliable for administrative decisions.

**Goal setting** (Locke and Latham) is one of the better-supported applied findings: specific, difficult goals with feedback and commitment outperform "do your best". Caveats worth knowing: goals distort effort toward what is measured (Goodhart again), can encourage unethical shortcuts, and are poorly suited to complex learning tasks where a learning goal beats a performance goal.

**Forced-ranking systems** have largely been abandoned by the organisations that popularised them, for reasons that are well documented and predictable from the individual-differences reasoning in file `09`.

### 1.3 Motivation at work

The classic content theories — Maslow's hierarchy and Herzberg's two-factor theory — are **culturally embedded and empirically weak**, and remain in every management textbook. Teach them as history. The better-supported frameworks:

- **Job characteristics model** (Hackman and Oldham) — skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy and feedback produce experienced meaningfulness, responsibility and knowledge of results, moderated by growth-need strength. Reasonable support; the basis of most job-redesign work.
- **Self-determination theory** at work — autonomy, competence and relatedness (file `03`). The best-supported motivational framework, and it explains why control-heavy supervision reliably undermines discretionary effort.
- **Equity theory and organisational justice** — distributive, procedural, interactional and informational justice. **Procedural justice is the strongest single predictor of acceptance of unwelcome decisions**, which is directly relevant to retrenchment, discipline and pay decisions in domain `12`.
- **Expectancy theory** (Vroom) — effort→performance expectancy × performance→outcome instrumentality × valence. The right diagnostic tool when an incentive scheme is not working: one of the three links is broken and the theory tells you which to check.
- **Job demands–resources model** — the standard framework for burnout and engagement: high demands drive strain, resources buffer them and drive engagement. Directly applicable to construction, where demands are high and resources often are not.

### 1.4 Teams

Tuckman's forming-storming-norming-performing is a memorable and empirically weak description. Better: the **input-process-output** and **IMOI** frameworks; **team mental models** and shared situational awareness; **transactive memory** (who knows what); **psychological safety** (Edmondson) — the belief that one can speak up without humiliation, which predicts learning behaviour and error reporting and is the mechanism behind most of what "safety culture" means in practice; **process losses** including social loafing and coordination loss; and the finding that **team composition matters less than team processes**.

**Crew resource management**, developed in aviation after accident investigations found that hierarchical crew dynamics prevented junior officers from challenging captains, is the most successful applied team intervention on record and has been adapted into surgery, maritime operations and — relevantly — high-risk construction. Its core is training people to speak up and training seniors to make that safe.

### 1.5 Leadership

The trajectory: **trait** approaches (revived by the Five Factor Model, with conscientiousness and extraversion predicting emergence better than effectiveness), **behavioural** approaches (initiating structure and consideration), **contingency** approaches (Fiedler, path-goal, situational leadership — the last of which is enormously popular and has almost no supporting evidence), **transformational and transactional** leadership (Bass; well-researched and criticised for construct contamination and for measurement instruments that partly assess outcomes), **LMX** (leader-member exchange, the dyadic relationship view), **servant** and **authentic** leadership (popular, weaker evidence), and **destructive leadership** — abusive supervision, which is under-researched relative to its costs.

**The honest summary.** Leadership research has a serious construct-proliferation problem: new labels are introduced faster than old ones are tested against them, and the measures overlap heavily. What is reasonably solid: supportive, fair, consistent supervision that provides clarity and psychological safety predicts better outcomes; abusive supervision predicts worse ones; and leadership development that consists of a two-day workshop changes nothing.

## 2. Human factors and ergonomics

Human factors designs systems around human capabilities and limitations rather than expecting humans to adapt to systems. It is the applied discipline with the clearest safety payoff and it belongs alongside domains `02_building_construction` and `09_equipment_manufacturers`.

**Physical ergonomics.** Anthropometry (and the individual-differences lesson from file `09` — design for a range, use adjustability, and never for the mean); biomechanics and manual handling; work-related musculoskeletal disorders; reach envelopes, clearances and sightlines; vibration, both hand-arm and whole-body, which matters for plant operators; thermal environment and heat stress, which is a serious and under-managed issue on southern African sites.

**Cognitive ergonomics.** Mental workload; **situation awareness** (Endsley's three levels: perception, comprehension, projection); vigilance decrement on monitoring tasks; **automation** and its characteristic failure modes — automation complacency, skill degradation, mode confusion, and the **ironies of automation** (Bainbridge's observation that automating the easy parts leaves the operator with only the hard parts, and less practice at them); attention and interruption costs; and decision making under time pressure, where Klein's naturalistic decision-making work on recognition-primed decisions describes what experts actually do better than classical decision theory does.

**Human error and safety.** **Reason's** Swiss cheese model of organisational accidents, and his taxonomy — slips and lapses (execution failures), mistakes (planning failures), and violations (deliberate deviations, which are usually rational responses to production pressure and are therefore a management problem, not a discipline problem). The **hierarchy of controls** — eliminate, substitute, engineer, administer, PPE — ranks interventions by reliability, and PPE is last because it depends on human behaviour every time. **Just culture** distinguishes error from at-risk behaviour from reckless behaviour, because a blame culture destroys the reporting on which safety improvement depends. **Safety-II / resilience engineering** (Hollnagel) argues for studying why things usually go right rather than only investigating failures.

**Design principles.** Affordances and signifiers, mapping, constraints, feedback and forcing functions (Norman's *The Design of Everyday Things*). Population stereotypes for controls — which are genuinely culture-dependent, so a switch direction that is "obviously" correct in one country is not in another. Alarm design and alarm fatigue. Checklists, whose effectiveness derives from making the implicit explicit and legitimising challenge across hierarchy.

## 3. Environmental psychology

The study of transactions between people and their physical settings — the branch with the most direct bearing on domains `01_architecture` and `19_interior_design`.

### 3.1 Restoration: Kaplan and Ulrich

**Attention Restoration Theory (Rachel and Stephen Kaplan).** Directed attention is an effortful, depletable resource; natural settings support recovery from **directed attention fatigue** by engaging **soft fascination** — effortless attention on gentle, interesting stimuli (clouds moving, leaves, water) which leaves capacity for reflection — as opposed to **hard fascination** (sport, thrillers, games) which fully captures attention and affords no reflection. A restorative setting has four properties: **being away**, **extent**, **compatibility** and **fascination**. Source: Kaplan and Kaplan, *The Experience of Nature* (1989); Kaplan, "The restorative benefits of nature", *Journal of Environmental Psychology* 15:169–182 (1995).

**Stress Reduction Theory (Roger Ulrich)** is the competing/complementary account: nature's benefits are primarily affective and physiological rather than cognitive, operating through rapid, pre-cognitive stress recovery. Ulrich, Simons, Losito, Fiorito, Miles and Zelson, "Stress recovery during exposure to natural and urban environments" (*Journal of Environmental Psychology* 11:201–230, 1991, doi:10.1016/S0272-4944(05)80184-7). Kaplan later argued the two theories describe distinct but interconnected processes and are best treated as complements.

**The view from the window.** Ulrich, "View through a window may influence recovery from surgery" (*Science* 224:420–421, 1984, doi:10.1126/science.6143402) — gallbladder surgery patients in rooms with a view of trees had shorter postoperative stays, fewer negative nursing notes and less strong analgesia than matched patients facing a brick wall. **Standing: the founding study of the field, and a small, single-site, retrospective study with a modest sample.** It has been enormously influential and is routinely cited as though it were definitive. The subsequent literature broadly supports the direction of the effect; the specific magnitudes should not be quoted from the 1984 paper as though established.

**What the reviews actually show.** Systematic reviews of nature's cognitive benefits find the effects concentrated in **executive attention and working memory**, with alerting and basic attention less consistent — one review found positive results on only three of thirteen cognitive tasks assessed. More recent meta-analytic work indicates benefits are more consistent for exposures of **30 minutes or longer**, with diminishing returns thereafter, and population work suggests around **two hours of nature contact per week** (however distributed) is associated with better self-reported health and wellbeing, levelling off after roughly 200–300 minutes. Proposed mechanisms include default-mode-network engagement during soft fascination, reduced rumination, and autonomic (vagal) pathways — all still under investigation.

### 3.2 Prospect and refuge

**Jay Appleton's** *The Experience of Landscape* (1975) proposed prospect-refuge theory: humans prefer environments offering both **prospect** (the ability to see out, to survey) and **refuge** (the ability to be sheltered and unseen), on the evolutionary reasoning that such settings supported both foraging and safety. It has been enormously influential on landscape architecture and interior design — the appeal of the window seat, the alcove overlooking a room, the raised terrace, the corner table with a view of the door.

**Standing: an appealing and widely applied theory with a thin and mixed empirical base.** The evolutionary reasoning is a just-so story of the kind file `09` teaches scepticism about, preference studies produce inconsistent operationalisations of prospect and refuge, and cultural and individual variation is substantial. Use it as a **design heuristic that matches observed preference**, not as an established psychological mechanism.

### 3.3 Biophilic design

Derived from E. O. Wilson's biophilia hypothesis — an innate human affiliation with living systems — biophilic design incorporates natural light, natural materials, views to nature, planting, water, natural forms and patterns, and variability of the thermal and airflow environment.

**Standing: directionally supported, weakly quantified, and heavily over-claimed commercially.** Gillis and Gatersleben's "A review of psychological literature on the health and wellbeing benefits of biophilic design" (*Buildings* 5:948–963, 2015, doi:10.3390/buildings5030948) found support for benefits from some elements — particularly views of nature and indoor plants — while noting the limited quantity and quality of evidence for many specific design prescriptions. The widely circulated productivity and absenteeism percentages in industry white papers generally trace back to small studies, to unpublished consultancy work, or to nothing citable at all. **Verify any specific percentage before repeating it.**

Daylighting and view are the elements with the strongest independent evidential support, and they overlap with well-established findings on circadian entrainment and on visual comfort.

### 3.4 Noise, crowding and density

**Noise.** Chronic noise exposure impairs children's cognitive performance, with **reading comprehension and long-term memory** the most consistently affected domains. The large European RANCH study established this for aircraft noise around schools. There is direct South African evidence: **Seabi, Cockcroft and Goldschagg, "The impact of aircraft noise exposure on South African children's reading comprehension"** (*Noise and Health* 14:244, 2012, doi:10.4103/1463-1741.102963). Mechanisms include masking of speech, chronic stress response, and learned inattention. Noise also degrades adult performance on complex verbal tasks, and open-plan office noise is one of the best-documented complaints in workplace research — Witterseh, Wyon and Clausen (*Indoor Air* 14:30–40, 2004) documented performance decrements from open-plan noise combined with moderate heat stress. Design responses: acoustic separation, absorption, sound masking, and the observation that **speech intelligibility, not sound level, is what disrupts cognitive work** — a partially intelligible conversation is far more disruptive than louder unintelligible noise.

**Crowding versus density.** Density is a physical fact (people per unit area); **crowding is the psychological experience of insufficient space**, mediated by control, predictability, social relationships and the ability to withdraw. The same density is crowded in one setting and not in another. The mediators — perceived control and the availability of retreat — are the design levers, which is why circulation design, alcoves, and the ability to close a door matter more than square metres per person.

**Personal space and territoriality.** Hall's proxemic zones (intimate, personal, social, public) are **culturally variable** and the specific distances should not be treated as universal. Territoriality — primary, secondary and public territories — and the **defensible space** tradition (Newman) linking layout, visibility and natural surveillance to crime, which fed into CPTED (Crime Prevention Through Environmental Design). Standing: defensible space is influential and empirically mixed; the strongest supported elements are natural surveillance and clear territorial definition, while the deterministic claims about layout and crime rates do not hold.

**Place attachment.** The affective bond between people and places, with dimensions of place identity (place as part of self-concept) and place dependence (place as functionally necessary). Predicts pro-environmental behaviour, resistance to relocation, community participation and maintenance of shared space. Practically relevant to any project involving relocation, resettlement or the redevelopment of an inhabited area — a live issue in Namibian and South African urban development and one where consultation that ignores place attachment reliably produces conflict.

**Wayfinding.** Lynch's five elements of the legible city — paths, edges, districts, nodes, landmarks — and cognitive maps with their systematic distortions. Sightlines, decision points, differentiated landmarks and clear signage hierarchies. In buildings: floor plate legibility, visible vertical circulation, and the finding that people navigate far better with landmarks than with signage alone.

**Environmental stressors** more broadly: thermal discomfort and heat (with direct cognitive performance costs, and a serious occupational issue in Namibia), poor air quality and its documented effect on cognitive performance, glare, and the accumulation of low-grade daily hassles.

## 4. Health psychology

The application of psychology to health, illness and healthcare. Core content: the **biopsychosocial model**; health behaviour change models (Health Belief Model, Theory of Planned Behaviour, the transtheoretical/stages-of-change model — the last of which is widely used and poorly supported, and the **COM-B model and Behaviour Change Wheel** which is the current best-organised framework); stress, appraisal and coping (Lazarus and Folkman); psychoneuroimmunology; adherence to treatment; pain; chronic illness adjustment; and health communication.

**[ZA]/[NA]** the regionally dominant applications are HIV prevention, testing and treatment adherence; TB adherence; maternal and child health; substance use, particularly alcohol; and non-communicable disease prevention. There is a substantial southern African health-psychology literature and it is where a large share of local psychological research funding sits.

Its structural relevance here is that **health psychology and educational psychology face the same problem** — changing behaviour in a system that is not designed to support the change — and the intervention frameworks transfer.

## 5. Behavioural economics

The systematic study of how actual decision-making departs from the rational-agent model, and the most successfully exported branch of psychology.

**Foundations.** Kahneman and Tversky's **prospect theory** — value is assessed as gains and losses relative to a reference point, the value function is concave for gains and convex and steeper for losses (loss aversion), and probabilities are weighted non-linearly with small probabilities over-weighted. **Heuristics and biases** — availability, representativeness, anchoring and adjustment. **Framing effects.** **Mental accounting** (Thaler). **The endowment effect** and status quo bias. **Hyperbolic discounting** and present bias. **The sunk cost fallacy**, which is the most common and most expensive error in project management.

**Nudge.** Thaler and Sunstein's choice architecture: default options, salience, social norms, simplification, timing, commitment devices. Defaults are the most powerful single tool and by a wide margin — organ donation opt-out versus opt-in, automatic pension enrolment, and default settings generally.

> ⚠️ **Nudge's effect sizes have been substantially revised downward.** The academic literature has been shown to be affected by publication bias, and analyses comparing published effect estimates with the results of large-scale government and firm trials find the real-world effects markedly smaller. Defaults remain robust; many other nudges are small, context-dependent, and sometimes null. The **replication problems in the underlying priming literature** (file `08`, on Kahneman) also touch parts of this field. Treat "nudge units" claims with the same scepticism as any other intervention industry.

**The critique worth holding.** Nudge locates the problem in individual cognition and can serve as a cheap substitute for structural change — a nudge to save more is not a substitute for a living wage, and a nudge to attend clinic is not a substitute for a clinic within walking distance. In southern African contexts this critique bites hard: much apparent irrationality is a rational response to scarcity, unreliable institutions and genuine uncertainty. Mullainathan and Shafir's *Scarcity* is the essential corrective reading — the argument that scarcity itself consumes cognitive bandwidth, which reframes "poor decisions under poverty" as a consequence rather than a cause.

**Where it connects to this knowledge base.** Procurement and tender decisions (anchoring, sunk cost, the winner's curse); safety behaviour (present bias against future harm; salience of hazards); client decision-making in design (framing of options, choice overload, the power of the default specification); and HR (default enrolment, framing of benefits, structured decision procedures as a debiasing tool).
