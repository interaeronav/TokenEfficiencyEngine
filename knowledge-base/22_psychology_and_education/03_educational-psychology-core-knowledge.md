# Educational psychology — the core knowledge

**Summary.** This is the subject matter itself: how learning is theorised, how motivation is theorised, how individual differences are conceptualised and measured, what goes wrong developmentally, and what the instructional evidence base actually supports. The field's honest position in 2026 is that a small number of learning-science findings are robust and replicated (retrieval practice, spacing, worked examples, the limits of working memory), a larger number of popular ideas are weak or dead (learning styles, most brain-based education, multiple intelligences as a psychometric claim), and several headline findings from motivation research (growth mindset, grit) have shrunk dramatically under meta-analysis. Teach the distinction, not just the content.

## Key facts

| Claim | Standing |
|---|---|
| Retrieval practice improves long-term retention | **Robust**; Roediger & Karpicke 2006; rated high-utility by Dunlosky et al. 2013 |
| Distributed (spaced) practice beats massed | **Robust**; Cepeda et al. 2008 gave the optimal-gap function |
| Working memory is severely capacity-limited and this constrains instruction | **Robust**; Sweller 1988 onward |
| Worked examples aid novices; the advantage reverses with expertise | **Well supported** (expertise reversal effect) |
| Feedback is powerful *on average* but highly variable, and ~1/3 of feedback interventions **reduce** performance | **Robust**; Kluger & DeNisi 1996 |
| Minimal-guidance/discovery approaches underperform for novices | **Well supported but contested**; Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006 |
| Matching teaching to a student's "learning style" improves learning | **Not supported**; the meshing hypothesis fails the required design (Pashler et al. 2008) |
| Growth-mindset interventions raise achievement broadly | **Substantially downgraded**; small and heterogeneous effects (Sisk et al. 2018; Macnamara & Burgoyne 2023) |
| Grit predicts success beyond conscientiousness | **Substantially downgraded**; Credé, Tynan & Harms 2017 |
| Multiple intelligences is a validated psychometric model | **Not supported**; lacks empirical evidence and a test |

## 1. Theories of learning

### 1.1 Behaviourism
Learning as a change in observable behaviour produced by environmental contingencies. **Classical conditioning** (Pavlov, Watson) explains the acquisition of emotional responses — including school phobia and test anxiety. **Operant conditioning** (Thorndike's law of effect, then Skinner) supplies reinforcement and punishment, schedules of reinforcement, shaping, extinction, and stimulus control. Skinner's *Science and Human Behavior* (1953) and his programmed-instruction work are the direct ancestors of mastery learning and of every adaptive learning platform.

**What survives.** Applied behaviour analysis remains the strongest evidence base for behaviour management, for skill acquisition in intellectual disability, and (contentiously) for early intensive intervention in autism. Functional behaviour assessment — establishing the antecedent, behaviour and consequence, and identifying the function the behaviour serves — is standard practice for educational psychologists and works.

**What does not.** Behaviourism cannot account for language acquisition (Chomsky's critique of *Verbal Behavior*), for insight, or for the observation that people learn a great deal they never perform. Extrinsic reinforcement can undermine intrinsic motivation under specified conditions (the overjustification effect) — a finding that is itself narrower than it is usually reported.

### 1.2 Cognitivism and information processing
Learning as the acquisition, encoding, organisation and retrieval of knowledge structures. The architecture: sensory register → severely capacity-limited **working memory** → effectively unlimited **long-term memory**, with attention controlling entry and schemas controlling organisation. Atkinson and Shiffrin's modal model; Baddeley and Hitch's working memory; levels-of-processing; encoding specificity; the distinction between declarative, procedural and conditional knowledge.

This is the framework doing the most work in contemporary educational psychology, because it generates falsifiable instructional predictions and most of them have held.

### 1.3 Constructivism (Piagetian / cognitive)
Learners actively construct knowledge by assimilating new experience into existing schemes and accommodating schemes when assimilation fails. Equilibration drives development. The classroom implications drawn from it — discovery learning, hands-on materials, readiness-based sequencing — are the contested part.

### 1.4 Social constructivism (Vygotskian)
Knowledge is constructed **in social interaction first and internalised second**. Higher mental functions originate on the interpsychological plane. Language is the master tool. The classroom implications — collaborative learning, dialogic teaching, apprenticeship models, cognitive apprenticeship — are widely adopted and reasonably well supported.

### 1.5 How to hold them together
The productive position is not to pick one. Behaviourist analysis handles behaviour and skill fluency; information processing handles instructional design; social constructivism handles the classroom as a social system and is the natural frame for consultation work. A practitioner who is an ideologue about one of them will do bad work.

## 2. Piaget and the critiques

**The stages.** Sensorimotor (0–2): schemes built from action; object permanence; the A-not-B error. Preoperational (2–7): symbolic function, egocentrism, centration, irreversibility, failure on conservation tasks. Concrete operational (7–11): conservation, classification, seriation, reversibility, but tied to concrete content. Formal operational (11+): hypothetico-deductive reasoning, systematic combination of variables, propositional logic.

**The critiques, in order of force:**

1. **Method artefacts.** Piaget's tasks confounded the target competence with language demands, memory load and social-pragmatic cues. Simplified tasks show competence far earlier: violation-of-expectation methods put object permanence in the first months, not at 8–12 months; Hughes's policeman task shows perspective-taking in three- and four-year-olds; Donaldson's *Children's Minds* showed conservation errors partly reflect children's reading of the experimenter's intention when a question is asked twice.
2. **Stages are not general.** Performance is domain-specific and context-dependent; the same child conserves in one material and not another (horizontal décalage, which Piaget acknowledged but never explained away).
3. **Formal operations are not universal.** A large minority of adults do not reach them on Piagetian tasks, and performance is heavily schooling- and culture-dependent.
4. **Underspecified mechanism.** Equilibration describes rather than explains transition. Neo-Piagetian theorists (Case, Pascual-Leone, Halford) rebuilt the account on working-memory capacity growth, which is the more productive line.
5. **Culture is absent.** Vygotsky's central objection.

**What survives, and it is a lot:** children think qualitatively differently from adults rather than merely knowing less; conceptual change requires disequilibrium; the clinical interview method; and the general lesson that instruction must be pitched at what the learner can currently assimilate.

## 3. Vygotsky, the ZPD, scaffolding and Bruner

**Zone of proximal development** — the distance between what a learner can do unaided and what they can do with guidance from a more capable other. Two consequences: assessment of the unaided level alone underestimates the learner (which is the theoretical basis of dynamic assessment, file `04`); and instruction should lead development rather than wait for it.

**Scaffolding** is *not* Vygotsky's term — it is Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976). Its defining property is **fading**: support that is not withdrawn is not scaffolding, it is dependence. The functions include recruitment, reduction of degrees of freedom, direction maintenance, marking critical features, frustration control and demonstration.

**Bruner** contributed the spiral curriculum (revisit topics at increasing sophistication), the enactive–iconic–symbolic sequence of representation, discovery learning (which he later qualified substantially), and the framing of narrative as a mode of thought. His claim that "any subject can be taught effectively in some intellectually honest form to any child at any stage of development" is a useful provocation, not a finding.

**Mediation and Feuerstein.** Reuven Feuerstein's mediated learning experience and structural cognitive modifiability sit in this tradition and matter disproportionately in southern Africa, where they underpin dynamic assessment approaches used with learners whose test performance is depressed by educational deprivation rather than by ability.

## 4. Bandura: social learning and self-efficacy

**Observational learning** requires attention, retention, reproduction and motivation. The Bobo doll studies showed acquisition without direct reinforcement and vicarious reinforcement effects. Reciprocal determinism: person, behaviour and environment mutually determine one another.

**Self-efficacy** — belief in one's capability to execute the actions required for a given attainment — is Bandura's most durable contribution and the best-supported motivational construct in education. It is **task- and domain-specific** (self-efficacy for solving quadratic equations, not "confidence"), and it has four sources in descending order of power: mastery experience, vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, and physiological/affective states. It predicts effort, persistence, choice of activity and achievement, and it is manipulable — which makes it the correct target for intervention where "self-esteem" is not. Bandura's *Social Foundations of Thought and Action* (1985/1986) is the primary source.

## 5. Motivation theory

**Self-determination theory (Deci and Ryan).** Three basic psychological needs — autonomy, competence, relatedness — whose satisfaction supports intrinsic motivation and wellbeing. Extrinsic motivation is not a single thing but a continuum of internalisation: external regulation → introjected → identified → integrated. The practical implication for schools is that the goal is not to make everything intrinsically interesting but to move regulation along the continuum by supporting autonomy (rationale, choice, acknowledgment of feelings), structure and relatedness. SDT is the motivational theory with the broadest empirical base and the most cross-cultural support, though the universality of the three needs remains debated.

**Attribution theory (Weiner).** Causal attributions for success and failure vary on three dimensions — locus (internal/external), stability (stable/unstable), controllability. Attributing failure to stable, internal, uncontrollable causes ("I'm just not a maths person") produces learned helplessness; attributing it to unstable, controllable causes (effort, strategy) sustains persistence. Attribution retraining works modestly.

**Expectancy–value theory (Eccles, Wigfield).** Achievement behaviour is a function of expectancy of success × subjective task value, where value decomposes into intrinsic interest, attainment value (importance to identity), utility value, and **cost** — the last of which is under-researched and probably the most practically important for adolescents. Utility-value interventions (having students write about why material matters to them) have shown replicable if modest effects.

**Goal orientation.** Mastery goals (develop competence) versus performance goals (demonstrate competence relative to others), each with approach and avoidance forms. Mastery-approach goals are associated with deeper strategies and persistence; performance-avoidance with the worst outcomes. The 2×2 and 3×2 elaborations have become baroque relative to their explanatory payoff.

**Mindset — handle honestly.** Dweck's implicit theories of intelligence (fixed vs growth) generated an enormous school-improvement industry. Two meta-analyses have since constrained the claim severely: Sisk et al., "To what extent and under which circumstances are growth mind-sets important to academic achievement? Two meta-analyses" (*Psychological Science*, 2018), found weak overall relationships between mindset and achievement and small intervention effects, with what benefit there is concentrated in academically at-risk and low-SES students; Macnamara and Burgoyne, "Do growth mindset interventions impact students' academic achievement?" (*Psychological Bulletin*, 2023), found little to no effect once study quality was accounted for. The UK Education Endowment Foundation's *Changing Mindsets* effectiveness trial (2019) likewise did not find the hoped-for gains.

> ⚠️ The correct professional line: mindset is a real and measurable individual difference; the intervention literature does **not** support universal mindset programmes as a school-improvement strategy; targeted, well-implemented, brief interventions with at-risk students may produce small effects. Anyone selling a mindset programme on the strength of the 2006 popular book is selling an out-of-date claim.

**Grit** has followed the same trajectory. Credé, Tynan and Harms, "Much ado about grit: a meta-analytic synthesis of the grit literature" (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 2017), found grit is largely redundant with conscientiousness, that the perseverance facet does nearly all the predictive work while the consistency-of-interest facet does little, and that grit's incremental validity over established predictors is small.

## 6. Memory, cognitive load and instructional design

**Cognitive load theory (Sweller, 1988 onward)** starts from the architecture in §1.2 and derives instructional prescriptions. Load is conventionally divided into **intrinsic** (set by element interactivity in the material relative to the learner's prior knowledge), **extraneous** (imposed by poor presentation — reducible), and **germane** (resources devoted to schema construction; the status of germane load as a separate category is now disputed and many CLT researchers have folded it into intrinsic).

Robust effects derived from CLT:
- **Worked-example effect** — novices learn more from studying worked examples than from solving equivalent problems.
- **Expertise reversal effect** — the same support that helps novices harms experts. This is the most under-appreciated finding in the whole area and it explains why "what works" questions are almost always ill-posed without specifying prior knowledge.
- **Split-attention effect** — physically integrate text with the diagram it refers to.
- **Redundancy effect** — do not present the same information in two forms simultaneously; it costs rather than helps.
- **Modality effect** — dual-channel presentation (spoken narration plus visual) can raise effective capacity; weaker and more condition-dependent than the others.
- **Goal-free effect** — removing the specific goal from a problem reduces means-ends search load.

**Retrieval practice.** Roediger and Karpicke, "Test-enhanced learning" (*Psychological Science*, 2006), established that retrieval is not a neutral readout of memory but a memory-modifying event. Dunlosky et al., "Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques" (*Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 2013), rated **practice testing** and **distributed practice** as the only two techniques of high utility out of ten reviewed, while rating highlighting, rereading, summarisation, keyword mnemonics and imagery as low utility. This paper is the single most useful thing to hand a teacher.

**Spacing and interleaving.** Cepeda et al. (*Psychological Science*, 2008) showed the optimal inter-study gap scales with the retention interval — roughly 10–20% of it. **Interleaving** (mixing problem types rather than blocking them) improves discrimination and transfer, especially in mathematics category learning, at the cost of feeling harder during study — one of Bjork's **desirable difficulties**. Learners systematically prefer the less effective blocked and massed schedules, which is why metacognitive instruction has to accompany the technique.

**Transfer.** The uncomfortable finding: far transfer is rare and hard. Near transfer within a domain occurs with practice across varied surface features; general "thinking skills" and "brain training" programmes have repeatedly failed to produce transfer to untrained tasks. The practical implication is that domain knowledge matters enormously and that generic skills teaching detached from content is largely wasted effort.

**Metacognition and self-regulated learning.** Metacognitive knowledge (about tasks, strategies and oneself) plus metacognitive regulation (planning, monitoring, evaluating). Zimmerman's cyclical model — forethought, performance, self-reflection — is the standard SRL framework, with Winne and Hadwin's information-processing model the more precise alternative. Metacognition interventions are among the better-evidenced school interventions in the EEF toolkit, with the caveat that they must be taught **within** subject content, not as a standalone course.

## 7. Intelligence and its measurement

**Spearman's *g*.** The positive manifold — all cognitive tests correlate positively — is one of the most replicated findings in psychology. *g* is the general factor extracted from it. It predicts educational attainment, occupational level and job performance better than any other single psychological variable, while explaining a minority of the variance in any individual outcome.

**Cattell–Horn–Carroll (CHC) theory** is the working psychometric consensus and the model behind the current major batteries. Three strata: *g* at stratum III; roughly 8–16 broad abilities at stratum II — fluid reasoning (Gf), comprehension-knowledge (Gc), short-term/working memory (Gwm), long-term storage and retrieval (Glr), visual processing (Gv), auditory processing (Ga), processing speed (Gs), reading and writing (Grw), quantitative knowledge (Gq), decision speed and others; and narrow abilities at stratum I. The WJ-IV, KABC-II and WISC-V index structures are all CHC-influenced, which is why cross-battery assessment is possible.

**Gardner's multiple intelligences.** Introduced in *Frames of Mind* (1983): linguistic, logical-mathematical, musical, spatial, bodily-kinaesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal, later naturalistic. Enormously popular with educators; **not accepted in psychometrics**. The core objections: intelligence tests find high correlations among cognitive abilities where MI predicts low ones, supporting *g*; the selection criteria for what counts as an "intelligence" are, in Gardner's own words, "reminiscent more of an artistic judgment than of a scientific assessment"; there is no MI test; and critics (Sternberg, Eysenck, Scarr, White) argue Gardner has redefined "ability" and "aptitude" as "intelligence" rather than expanded the construct. Gardner himself has said he is "uneasy" with how schools have used the theory, has denied that multiple intelligences are learning styles, and **agrees that learning styles is an incoherent idea lacking empirical evidence**.

**The Flynn effect** — secular gains in raw IQ test performance across the 20th century — requires that norms be periodically renormed, which is precisely why an out-of-date normed test inflates scores. This is a live practical problem in southern Africa where old instruments remain in use.

## 8. Learning difficulties and neurodevelopmental conditions

- **Dyslexia** — a specific learning disorder in word reading, characterised by difficulties in accurate/fluent word recognition and poor decoding and spelling. The dominant causal account is a **phonological deficit**; competing and complementary accounts include rapid automatised naming (the double-deficit hypothesis), visual attention span, and cerebellar theories. Evidence for intervention is strong and specific: **systematic, explicit phonics** and phonemic-awareness instruction. Evidence for coloured overlays, tinted lenses and most visual-training programmes is weak to absent. **[ZA]/[NA]** identification is hugely complicated by multilingualism and by the fact that many children are taught to read in a language they do not speak at home — a reading difficulty in the language of instruction is not automatically dyslexia.
- **Dyscalculia** — a specific learning disorder in mathematics, associated with impaired number sense and magnitude representation, and considerably less well researched and less well served by interventions than dyslexia.
- **ADHD** — persistent inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity, present before age 12, in two or more settings, causing impairment. Substantially heritable. Stimulant medication has large short-term effects on symptoms with much weaker evidence for long-term academic gains; behavioural parent training and classroom behavioural interventions have moderate support; the classroom accommodations that matter are structural (predictable routines, chunked tasks, proximity, immediate feedback). Diagnosis rests on convergent evidence across informants and settings, not on a single rating scale — see file `04`.
- **Autism spectrum disorder** — persistent deficits in social communication and interaction plus restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests or activities, with sensory features. The DSM-5 collapse of previous subcategories into one spectrum with severity levels remains contested. Early intensive behavioural intervention has the largest evidence base but the literature has substantial methodological weaknesses, and the **neurodiversity critique** — that intervention goals should be functional and self-determined rather than normalising — has changed practice, particularly with respect to interventions aimed at suppressing stimming or enforcing eye contact.
- **Developmental language disorder (DLD)** — under-identified relative to its prevalence and to its consequences; strongly implicated in reading failure and in behaviour referrals. An educational psychologist who does not screen language will misattribute language difficulty to inattention or defiance.
- **Intellectual disability** — requires deficits in both intellectual functioning *and* adaptive behaviour, with onset in the developmental period. The adaptive-behaviour requirement is not optional and is routinely skipped in practice; see file `04` on adaptive behaviour scales.

## 9. Giftedness

Definitions vary from the purely psychometric (a cut score, commonly 2 SD above the mean) to the multi-criteria: Renzulli's three-ring conception (above-average ability × task commitment × creativity), Gagné's differentiated model distinguishing gifts (aptitude) from talents (developed performance). Practical issues: **twice-exceptional** learners whose giftedness and disability mask each other; **underachievement** among gifted students; identification bias against learners from low-SES, rural and second-language backgrounds — an acute problem in southern Africa; and the acceleration-versus-enrichment debate, where the evidence favours acceleration more strongly than school practice generally admits.

## 10. Classroom and behaviour management

The evidence-based core is unglamorous: clear expectations taught explicitly rather than announced; high rates of specific positive feedback (a praise-to-correction ratio in the region of 4:1 or 5:1 is the commonly cited target — the precise ratio is not well established); routines and transitions rehearsed; high-frequency opportunities to respond; proximity and non-verbal control before verbal; consistent, calm, low-intensity responses to low-level disruption; **functional behaviour assessment** for persistent individual behaviour, because a behaviour that serves escape is made worse by exclusion and a behaviour that serves attention is reinforced by public reprimand.

School-wide positive behaviour support (SWPBIS) is a tiered whole-school application of these principles with reasonable evidence in US settings; transferability to under-resourced southern African schools with class sizes of 45–60 is genuinely uncertain. **[ZA]** corporal punishment has been unlawful in schools since 1996; **[NA]** it is likewise prohibited. Its persistence in practice is a real and continuing problem that an educational psychologist working in schools will encounter.

## 11. Inclusive education

The principle: learners with disabilities and learning difficulties are educated with their peers, with support brought to the learner rather than the learner removed to the support. **[ZA]** the policy instrument is **Education White Paper 6: Special Needs Education — Building an Inclusive Education and Training System** (Department of Education, July 2001), which committed to converting special schools into resource centres integrated with **district-based support teams**, designating approximately **500 of 20,000 primary schools as full-service schools** beginning with 30 districts, and replacing the old identification-and-placement process with one acknowledging the central role of educators and parents. Implementation is operationalised through the **Screening, Identification, Assessment and Support (SIAS)** policy. **[NA]** the parallel instrument is the Ministry of Education's **Sector Policy on Inclusive Education**. Both are covered in file `07`.

The evidence on inclusion is more equivocal than the advocacy: outcomes depend heavily on the support actually provided, and "inclusion" implemented as placement without resources produces worse outcomes than either well-resourced inclusion or well-resourced special provision.

## 12. What actually works in teaching — the defensible list

1. **Retrieval practice** — high utility, robust, cheap.
2. **Distributed practice** — high utility, robust, requires curriculum-level planning.
3. **Interleaving** — good evidence in maths and category learning; feels worse to learners.
4. **Worked examples for novices**, faded to problem solving as expertise develops.
5. **Explicit/direct instruction of new content**, followed by guided then independent practice. Kirschner, Sweller and Clark's "Why minimal guidance during instruction does not work" (*Educational Psychologist*, 2006) is the canonical statement; the counter-literature (Hmelo-Silver and colleagues on scaffolded problem-based learning) is worth reading because the real answer is about **how much guidance for whom, when** rather than a binary.
6. **Feedback** — but carefully. Hattie and Timperley, "The power of feedback" (*Review of Educational Research*, 2007), gives the model (feed up, feed back, feed forward at task, process, self-regulation and self levels). Kluger and DeNisi's meta-analysis (*Psychological Bulletin*, 1996) is the essential corrective: feedback interventions had a positive average effect but **over a third made performance worse**, and feedback directed at the self rather than the task is the main culprit.
7. **Metacognitive and self-regulatory strategy instruction embedded in subject teaching.**
8. **Formative assessment** used to change subsequent teaching, not merely to record.

And the things to stop doing: matching instruction to learning styles; "brain-based" programmes and most commercial cognitive-training packages; teaching generic thinking skills detached from content; re-reading and highlighting as study strategies; universal mindset or grit programmes sold as achievement interventions.

> ⚠️ **The learning-styles myth in detail.** Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer and Bjork (*Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 2008/2009) set out the study design required to test the meshing hypothesis — classify learners by style, randomly assign to matched and mismatched instruction, test everyone on the same outcome — and found such studies almost entirely absent from the literature, with the handful that existed producing negative results. Their conclusion: "at present, there is no adequate evidence base to justify incorporating learning styles assessments into general educational practice." Belief nonetheless persists among a large majority of educators; one UK study found 90% of academics agreed there are basic conceptual flaws in the theory while 58% still agreed students learn better in their preferred style. Teachers also cannot accurately judge their students' styles. Correcting this belief is part of an educational psychologist's consultation work.
