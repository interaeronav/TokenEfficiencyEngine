# The psychology professions — who does what, and who says they may

**Summary.** "Psychologist" is a legally protected title in South Africa, Namibia, the United Kingdom and every US state, but it is not one profession — it is a family of statutorily separate registration categories with different training, different scopes of practice and different limits. The single most consequential distinction is between a **psychologist** (a registered health professional with a postgraduate professional qualification, supervised practicum and a licensing examination), a **psychiatrist** (a medical doctor with a specialist psychiatric qualification, who may prescribe), and a **counsellor or psychotherapist** (in most of these jurisdictions a much more lightly regulated or entirely unregulated activity, with the exception of South Africa's statutory Registered Counsellor category). This file maps the categories; file `01` gives the training routes country by country.

> ⚠️ Title protection is the operative legal fact. In South Africa and Namibia it is an offence to hold yourself out as a psychologist without registration. "Life coach", "wellness counsellor" and "therapist" are, by contrast, not protected in most jurisdictions covered here — which is exactly why the distinction matters to a client choosing a practitioner.

## 1. The organising distinction: who the client is

The cleanest way to separate the categories is not by technique — they share most techniques — but by **who or what is the unit of concern**.

- The **clinical psychologist** treats *psychopathology in an individual*. The reference frame is the diagnostic system (DSM-5-TR, ICD-11), the setting is a clinic, hospital or private practice, and the output is assessment, formulation and psychotherapy. Historically the category grew out of psychiatry's need for assessment and out of the treatment of war neurosis; its centre of gravity is severe and enduring mental illness.
- The **counselling psychologist** treats *distress and adjustment in an otherwise functioning person*. The reference frame is developmental and humanistic rather than pathological; the classic phrase is "working with the worried well", though in practice the caseload overlaps heavily with clinical psychology. The distinction is more institutional than empirical: in most countries the two categories' actual daily work is difficult to tell apart, and the boundary is defended by professional bodies more than by evidence.
- The **educational psychologist** works at the *intersection of a learner, a teacher and a curriculum*. Wikipedia's framing of an "academic triangle" whose vertices are teachers, students and curricula is a fair summary. The educational psychologist's four characteristic functions are assessment (diagnostic and psycho-educational), consultation and counselling to students, parents, teachers and school leadership, community-level intervention (corrective, preventive, optimising), and referral. The setting is a school, a district support team, or private practice taking school referrals.
- The **industrial/organisational psychologist** works on *human behaviour in the workplace*: selection, assessment centres, job analysis, performance management, motivation at work, team dynamics, leadership development, organisational change. See file `10`, which links this directly to construction HR in domain `12`.
- The **research psychologist** designs, executes and interprets *psychological research and measurement* without a therapeutic caseload. **[ZA]** this is a distinct HPCSA registration category — an unusual arrangement internationally, which exists partly so that people building and validating psychometric instruments are covered by the statutory framework.
- The **neuropsychologist** works on *brain–behaviour relationships*: the assessment of cognitive function after injury, stroke, epilepsy, degenerative disease or neurodevelopmental disorder, and cognitive rehabilitation. It is a post-professional specialisation almost everywhere — you become a clinical or educational psychologist first.
- The **psychiatrist** is a *medical specialist*. This is the sharpest boundary in the whole field: psychiatrists complete a medical degree, an internship and community service, then a specialist registrar programme in psychiatry. They diagnose and treat mental illness with the full medical toolkit, prescribe psychotropic medication, admit and certify patients under mental health legislation, and manage the physical health consequences of psychiatric illness and its treatment. Psychologists do none of these things.
- The **psychotherapist or counsellor** is the loosest term. In the UK and the US the words are essentially unprotected, and the training behind them ranges from a four-year accredited psychotherapy training with personal therapy and supervision to a weekend certificate. **[ZA]** South Africa is unusual in having created a statutory floor: the **Registered Counsellor** category, requiring an accredited BPsych or equivalent four-year degree with a practicum and a board examination, with a scope limited to screening, basic supportive counselling and referral rather than psychotherapy or diagnosis.

## 2. Training length, compared

Approximate, full-time-equivalent, from school-leaving to independent registration:

| Route | Years to independent practice |
|---|---|
| **[ZA]** Registered Counsellor | 4 (BPsych or equivalent + practicum + board exam) |
| **[ZA]** Psychometrist (Independent Practice) | 4–5 |
| **[ZA]** Educational / counselling / I-O / research psychologist | 7 minimum: 3 (bachelor) + 1 (honours) + 2 (professional master's incl. internship) + board exam |
| **[ZA]** Clinical psychologist | 8 minimum: as above **plus 12 months' compulsory community service** |
| **[NA]** Psychologist | Comparable to **[ZA]**; most Namibian psychologists qualify in South Africa — see file `01` |
| UK educational psychologist | 7: 3 (BPS-accredited degree) + ≥1 year relevant experience + 3 (doctorate) |
| UK clinical psychologist | 7–9: degree + assistant-psychologist years + 3-year DClinPsy |
| US school psychologist (specialist) | 6–7: 4 (bachelor) + 3 (EdS incl. 1200-hour internship) |
| US school psychologist (doctoral) | 9–11: 4 + 5–7 (PhD/PsyD/EdD incl. 1500+ hour internship), then EPPP and licensure |
| Psychiatrist (ZA/NA) | 12–13: 6 (MBChB) + 2 (internship) + 1 (community service) + 4 (registrar) |

The pattern to notice: **the professional master's is the terminal practice qualification in South Africa, while the doctorate is the terminal practice qualification in the UK and increasingly in the US.** This causes recurring recognition friction when South African–trained psychologists seek registration abroad, and vice versa.

## 3. What only some of them may do

Certain acts are reserved by statute or regulation, and the reservations differ:

- **Prescribing psychotropic medication** — psychiatrists and other medical practitioners only, in all four jurisdictions covered here. A handful of US states (Louisiana, New Mexico, Illinois, Iowa, Idaho, Colorado and Utah among them) have granted prescriptive authority to specifically trained doctoral psychologists; this is contested and does not apply in southern Africa. *Verify the current US state list before relying on it.*
- **Psychological testing** — **[ZA]** the use of psychological tests is restricted. The Health Professions Act framework provides for tests to be *classified* as psychological tests, after which they may be used only by appropriately registered practitioners; psychometrists may administer and score a defined subset under supervision, while interpretation and diagnostic conclusions rest with psychologists. This restriction is a real constraint on schools, HR departments and NGOs, and is discussed at length in file `04`.
- **Diagnosis** — a psychologist may make a psychological diagnosis within their scope; a Registered Counsellor may not.
- **Certification and involuntary admission** under mental health legislation — medical practitioners, not psychologists.
- **Statutory reporting of child abuse** — mandatory for psychologists in both **[ZA]** (Children's Act 38 of 2005, s.110) and **[NA]** (Child Care and Protection Act 3 of 2015, s.132), both of which name psychologists explicitly. See file `07`.

## 4. Educational psychologist vs school psychologist — a real terminological trap

These are not synonyms, and the mismatch trips up qualification recognition constantly.

- In the **US**, "school psychologist" is the practitioner role (assessment, intervention, consultation inside K–12 schools), while "educational psychologist" usually denotes an academic researcher studying learning — APA Division 15 (Educational Psychology) versus Division 16 (School Psychology).
- In the **UK, South Africa and Namibia**, "educational psychologist" *is* the practitioner role, and there is no separate "school psychologist" title.
- Consequence: a South African educational psychologist applying to the US is applying, functionally, to be a school psychologist; a US-trained educational psychologist (Division 15) may have no practice credential at all.
