# The undergraduate psychology curriculum, module by module

**Summary.** An accredited undergraduate psychology degree is remarkably standardised across South Africa, Namibia, the UK, the US and Australasia — a consequence of professional-body accreditation requirements (BPS in the UK, APA guidelines in the US, HPCSA-linked accreditation **[ZA]**) converging on the same nine or ten content areas. The list below is that consensus core. Note what it is *not*: it is not therapy training, and it contains almost no clinical skills. The degree teaches a body of empirical findings, a method for producing them, and — increasingly — a critical stance toward how much of that body actually replicates.

## Key facts

| Module | Typical year | Weight in EPPP Part 1 (as a proxy for professional importance) |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction to psychology | 1 | — (foundational) |
| Research methods and statistics | 1–3, every year | 7% |
| Biological psychology / neuroscience | 2 | 10% |
| Cognitive psychology | 2 | 13% (with affective bases) |
| Developmental psychology | 2 | 12% |
| Social psychology | 2 | 11% (with multicultural bases) |
| Personality and individual differences | 2–3 | — |
| Abnormal psychology / psychopathology | 3 | 16% (with assessment and diagnosis) |
| History and systems / conceptual issues | 3 | — |
| Applied electives | 3 | 15% treatment; 16% ethics |

> ⚠️ Nothing in an undergraduate psychology degree qualifies the holder to counsel, assess or diagnose anyone. **[ZA]** and **[NA]** it is an offence to represent yourself as a psychologist on the strength of it.

## 1. Introduction to psychology

**Core content.** The survey course: what psychology claims to be, the major perspectives (biological, behavioural, cognitive, psychodynamic, humanistic, evolutionary, sociocultural), the nervous system in outline, sensation and perception, consciousness and sleep, learning (classical and operant conditioning), memory, language and thought, intelligence, motivation and emotion, development across the lifespan, personality, stress and health, psychological disorders and their treatment, and social psychology. Every subsequent module is a deeper pass through one of these.

**Standard textbooks.** Richard Gross, *Psychology: The Science of Mind and Behaviour* (first published 1987, and the standard UK/southern-African survey text for decades); David Myers and C. Nathan DeWall, *Psychology*; Michael Passer and Ronald Smith, *Psychology: The Science of Mind and Behavior*; historically Henry Gleitman's *Psychology*, still the most elegantly written of the group. South African editions and adaptations of Gross and of the Weiten/Passer family are widely prescribed.

**By the end, the student must be able to:** define psychology as an empirical discipline and distinguish it from folk psychology and from psychiatry; describe the major theoretical perspectives and say what each explains well and badly; read and summarise a journal abstract; recognise the difference between a demonstration, a correlational finding and an experiment; and resist the two commonest beginner errors — treating a label as an explanation, and treating a group mean as a description of an individual.

## 2. Research methods and statistics

This is the spine. It runs through every year, it is the module students most often fail, and it is the module that most distinguishes a psychology graduate from a graduate in a neighbouring social science. File `06` covers it in full; the undergraduate version is:

**Year 1.** Variables and operationalisation; levels of measurement; descriptive statistics; frequency distributions; the normal curve; sampling and sampling error; the logic of hypothesis testing; correlation. Ethics in research: informed consent, deception and debriefing, the Milgram and Zimbardo cases and why ethics committees exist.

**Year 2.** Experimental design (between- and within-subjects, factorial designs); *t*-tests; one-way and factorial ANOVA; chi-square; non-parametric alternatives; validity threats (history, maturation, regression to the mean, attrition, demand characteristics, experimenter expectancy); quasi-experimental designs; introduction to qualitative methods.

**Year 3.** Multiple regression; repeated-measures and mixed designs; introduction to factor analysis; effect sizes and confidence intervals; power; measurement theory (reliability and validity — see file `04`); qualitative methodology in earnest (thematic analysis, discourse analysis, IPA); and, in any well-taught modern curriculum, **the replication crisis** — the 2015 Open Science Collaboration reproducibility project in *Science* is now standard undergraduate reading, along with p-hacking, HARKing, publication bias and preregistration.

**Standard textbooks.** Andy Field, *Discovering Statistics Using IBM SPSS Statistics* — the dominant text in the UK and southern Africa, notable for actually being readable; David Howell, *Statistical Methods for Psychology* (first published 1982) for a more conventional treatment; Barbara Tabachnick and Linda Fidell, *Using Multivariate Statistics* at honours level.

**By the end:** design a study that could answer a stated question; identify the confound that will ruin it; choose and justify an appropriate statistical test; run it in SPSS, R, jamovi or JASP; report the result in APA format including an effect size and a confidence interval; and explain why a *p*-value below .05 is not the same as a finding that is true, large, or useful.

## 3. Biological psychology and neuroscience

**Core content.** Neurons and glia; the action potential; synaptic transmission and the major neurotransmitter systems; neuroanatomy — hindbrain, midbrain, forebrain, the cortical lobes and their functions, the limbic system, the basal ganglia; the endocrine system and the HPA axis; methods (lesion studies, EEG, ERP, fMRI, PET, TMS, optogenetics); sensory systems, especially vision and audition; motor control; sleep and circadian rhythms; hunger, thirst and reproductive behaviour; emotion and the amygdala; learning and memory at the cellular level (LTP, the hippocampus, HM); psychopharmacology in outline; neuroplasticity and neurodevelopment; behaviour genetics and heritability.

**Standard textbook.** James Kalat, *Biological Psychology* — the long-standing default. Neil Carlson's *Physiology of Behavior* and Bear, Connors and Paradiso's *Neuroscience: Exploring the Brain* are the common alternatives.

**By the end:** describe how a signal crosses a synapse; locate the major structures and say what a lesion to each produces; explain what an fMRI BOLD signal actually measures and, crucially, what it does not; interpret a heritability estimate correctly (a population statistic about variance, not a statement about an individual); and treat neuro-explanations sceptically — the "seductive allure of neuroscience explanations" is a documented effect and educational psychologists meet it constantly in the form of neuromyths (file `03`).

---

## 4. Cognitive psychology

**Core content.** Attention (selective, divided, sustained; Broadbent, Treisman, the attentional blink, inattentional blindness); perception and pattern recognition; the modal model of memory and its successors — sensory register, short-term store, Baddeley and Hitch's working memory model with the phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad, central executive and episodic buffer; long-term memory and its divisions (episodic, semantic, procedural; explicit and implicit); encoding, storage and retrieval; forgetting and interference; **the reconstructive nature of memory** and eyewitness testimony (Loftus); knowledge representation, schemas and categories; language comprehension and production; problem solving; reasoning and decision making, including heuristics and biases (Tversky and Kahneman); expertise and skill acquisition; and **cognitive load theory** where the department has an applied bent.

**Standard textbook.** Robert Sternberg, *Cognitive Psychology*; Michael Eysenck and Mark Keane, *Cognitive Psychology: A Student's Handbook*; E. Bruce Goldstein, *Cognitive Psychology*.

**By the end:** describe working memory's components and their capacity limits, and explain why those limits are the single most practically important fact in instructional design; explain why memory is reconstructive and what follows for interviewing children and for eyewitness evidence; describe the major heuristics and biases and identify them in your own reasoning.

---

## 5. Developmental psychology

**Core content.** Research designs specific to development (cross-sectional, longitudinal, sequential, and the cohort confound); prenatal development and teratogens; infant perception and motor development; **attachment** — Bowlby's theory, Ainsworth's Strange Situation and the classification system, Main's disorganised category, cross-cultural variation and the critique of the Strange Situation's cultural specificity; temperament; **Piaget's stage theory** and its critiques; **Vygotsky's sociocultural theory**; information-processing accounts of cognitive development; theory of mind and false-belief tasks; language acquisition; moral development (Kohlberg and Gilligan's critique); Erikson's psychosocial stages; peer relations and play; adolescence, identity and risk-taking; adult development and ageing; **Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems model** (1979), which is the organising framework most educational psychologists actually use in practice.

**Standard textbooks.** Laura Berk, *Development Through the Lifespan* (first published 1998) and *Child Development*; John Santrock, *Life-Span Development*. Both are large, expensive and heavily revised; both are also the reason developmental content is well taught.

**By the end:** distinguish a stage theory from a continuous-change account and say what evidence would discriminate between them; describe the four Piagetian stages and the main empirical challenges to them (Baillargeon on object permanence, the effect of task framing on conservation and egocentrism); use Bronfenbrenner's nested systems to structure a case; and describe attachment classifications without over-claiming their predictive power for later outcomes, which is real but modest.

---

## 6. Social psychology

**Core content.** Social cognition, attribution theory and the fundamental attribution error; attitudes, attitude change, persuasion and cognitive dissonance (Festinger); the self and self-concept; conformity (Asch), compliance, obedience (Milgram); group processes — social facilitation, social loafing, groupthink, group polarisation; prejudice, stereotyping and discrimination; intergroup relations, realistic conflict theory, social identity theory (Tajfel and Turner); aggression; prosocial behaviour and the bystander effect; interpersonal attraction and close relationships; culture and cross-cultural psychology.

**Standard textbook.** Elliot Aronson, *The Social Animal* (first published 1972) — the classic short treatment; Aronson, Wilson and Sommers, *Social Psychology* as the full text.

> ⚠️ **Social psychology is the sub-discipline most damaged by the replication crisis.** Priming effects, ego depletion, power posing, the facial-feedback hypothesis and several classic demonstrations have failed to replicate or have been substantially downgraded. The Stanford Prison Experiment has been comprehensively re-examined and is now better treated as a demonstration of demand characteristics than as evidence about situational power. A curriculum that still teaches these as settled findings is out of date. Teach the finding, then teach the replication record.

**By the end:** describe the classic studies *and* their current evidential standing; explain the fundamental attribution error and detect it in your own case formulations; distinguish a laboratory effect from a field effect; and understand why "the situation is more powerful than we think" survives as a general lesson even where specific demonstrations have not.

## 7. Personality and individual differences

**Core content.** The trait approach — Allport, Cattell's 16PF, Eysenck's dimensions, and the **Five Factor Model** (openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism), which is the empirical mainstream; the psychodynamic tradition (Freud, then object relations, ego psychology, attachment-informed models); humanistic accounts (Rogers, Maslow); social-cognitive accounts (Bandura, Mischel, and the person–situation debate); behaviour-genetic findings on personality heritability; personality measurement and its validity; **intelligence and its measurement**, usually taught here — Spearman's *g*, the Cattell–Horn–Carroll model, the Flynn effect, group differences and the long controversy about them.

> ⚠️ The MBTI is widely used commercially and is not a scientifically supported instrument: it dichotomises continuous dimensions, has poor test–retest classification stability, and does not predict job performance. It should be taught as a case study in why face validity is not validity.

**By the end:** describe the FFM and the evidence for it; explain the person–situation debate and its resolution (traits predict aggregated behaviour, not single acts); critique a personality instrument on psychometric grounds rather than on whether it "feels accurate" — the Barnum/Forer effect being the standard demonstration.

---

## 8. Abnormal psychology / psychopathology

**Core content.** Definitions of abnormality and their problems (statistical rarity, distress, dysfunction, deviance from norms); the medical model and its critics; classification — **DSM-5-TR** and **ICD-11**, their structure, their differences, and the recurring critiques (reification of categories, comorbidity, cultural bias, diagnostic inflation); the major diagnostic groups — anxiety disorders, obsessive-compulsive and related, trauma- and stressor-related, mood disorders, schizophrenia spectrum, feeding and eating, substance-related, personality disorders, and **neurodevelopmental disorders** (intellectual disability, communication disorders, ASD, ADHD, specific learning disorder, motor disorders) which is the group educational psychologists live in; aetiological models including the diathesis–stress framework; treatment approaches — pharmacological, CBT and its variants, psychodynamic, humanistic, family and systemic; evidence for efficacy and the common-factors debate.

**Standard textbooks.** Ronald Comer, *Abnormal Psychology* (first published 1992) — heavily used **[ZA]**; David Barlow and V. Mark Durand, *Abnormal Psychology: An Integrative Approach* (first published 1995) — the better book on aetiology.

**By the end:** apply diagnostic criteria without believing that a diagnosis is an explanation; describe the transdiagnostic alternatives (RDoC, the HiTOP hierarchical model, network approaches) and why they exist; and be able to state what is culturally contingent in a diagnostic category — a live question in South Africa and Namibia where the DSM's cultural formulation interview is only a partial answer.

---

## 9. History and systems / conceptual issues

Often the least popular module and the one that pays off most in professional practice. **Core content:** philosophical antecedents (empiricism, rationalism, the mind–body problem); Wundt and structuralism; James and functionalism; Gestalt psychology; behaviourism (Watson, Skinner) and its rise and fall; psychoanalysis and its scientific status; the cognitive revolution; humanistic psychology; the emergence of neuroscience; **critical and indigenous psychologies** — in South Africa this includes psychology's own role under apartheid, the complicity of the profession and its bodies, and the post-1994 project of building a psychology adequate to the country; philosophy of science (Popper's falsificationism, Kuhn's paradigms, Lakatos), operationalism, reductionism, determinism and free will.

**By the end:** situate any theory in its historical and political context; recognise that "scientific" and "true" are not synonyms; and articulate why psychology's replication crisis is a methodological and sociological problem rather than a discovery that psychology is not a science.

---

## 10. Applied electives

Departments differ; the common third-year electives are **educational psychology**, **health psychology**, **industrial/organisational psychology**, **community psychology** (particularly strong in South Africa, for good historical reasons), **forensic and criminological psychology**, **sport psychology**, **cross-cultural psychology**, **psychological assessment**, and **neuropsychology**. For a student heading toward educational psychology, the highest-value electives are psychological assessment, developmental psychopathology and community psychology.

---

## 11. What the degree does not teach

Worth stating explicitly, because the gap is where most disappointment comes from:

- **No therapy training.** Counselling and psychotherapy skills are postgraduate.
- **Very little assessment practice.** Undergraduates learn psychometric theory; they do not administer a WISC.
- **Little law or professional ethics beyond research ethics.**
- **Almost no supervision experience**, which is the actual medium of professional formation.
- **Limited exposure to the local instrument problem** — the fact that most tests in southern African use were normed elsewhere (file `04`).

The degree's real product is a person who can read an empirical literature critically, design a study, handle data honestly, and think about behaviour without reaching immediately for dispositional explanations. That is worth having whether or not the student ever registers.
