# Ethics and law for psychologists working with children

**Summary.** Professional ethics in psychology is not a set of aspirations; in South Africa and Namibia it is enforceable statutory regulation with disciplinary and criminal consequences. The four load-bearing legal facts for anyone working with children in this region are: **mandatory reporting of abuse is a statutory duty that overrides confidentiality and professional privilege**; **children have independent consent rights from a defined age**; **inclusive education policy defines the support system a psychologist operates within**; and **[ZA]** personal information about children and about health is doubly protected under POPIA. The professional codes (HPCSA, APA, BPS) sit on top of that legal floor.

## Key facts

| Provision | **[ZA]** | **[NA]** |

## 1. Informed consent and assent with minors

**The structure.** For a child, consent is usually a two-part act: **legal consent** from the person with parental responsibilities and rights, and **assent** from the child — the child's own agreed, age-appropriate agreement to participate. Assent is not legally sufficient on its own where consent is required, but proceeding without it against a competent child's objection is ethically indefensible except in narrowly defined circumstances.

**Who may consent.** **[ZA]** the holder of parental responsibilities and rights under the Children's Act — which is not always the biological father (s.20–21 distinguish married and unmarried fathers) and may be a guardian or a court-appointed person. **[NA]** the parent, guardian, or where there is none, the **care-giver** (s.220(3)) — a category that matters enormously in Namibia and South Africa where children are commonly raised by grandmothers, aunts and older siblings. Get this right at intake: assessing a child on the consent of an adult who does not hold parental responsibility is a live risk.

**The child's own consent.** **[ZA]** Children's Act s.129(2): a child may consent to their own **medical treatment** if over 12 **and** of sufficient maturity and mental capacity to understand the benefits, risks, social and other implications. For a **surgical operation** (s.129(3)) the child must additionally be assisted by a parent or guardian. **[NA]** the equivalent threshold is **14** (s.220(1)(a)), with the same maturity requirement and the same parental-assistance requirement for surgery (s.220(1)(b)).

**[NA]** s.220(10) is a distinctive and useful provision worth quoting: *"Any child who is of sufficient maturity to understand the benefits, risks and implications of a medical intervention may access confidential medical counselling and advice without parental consent, irrespective of such child's age, where this is in the best interests of the child."* And s.220(9) requires that where a child is not competent to consent but can express an informed view, that view **must be given due consideration**.

> ⚠️ Whether psychological assessment and psychotherapy constitute "medical treatment" or "medical intervention" within the meaning of these sections is not settled on the face of the statutes and is the exact point on which practitioners most often assume rather than check. **Seek current professional-board guidance.** In practice most services obtain parental consent plus child assent for all work with minors, and treat s.129 / s.220 as the floor rather than the operating rule.

**What informed consent must actually cover** — in plain language, at the child's developmental level, and documented:
- who you are, what registration category you hold, and who is paying you;
- what the assessment or intervention involves, how long, how many sessions;
- **what will happen to the information** — who gets the report, who sees the file, what goes on the school record;
- **the limits of confidentiality, stated before anything is disclosed** (§2);
- the right to decline, to stop, and to decline specific parts;
- foreseeable risks and benefits, and what the alternatives are;
- fees, cancellation terms, and medical-scheme implications.

**Special situations.** Court-ordered assessment (consent is not the operative basis; the child and family must still be told exactly what will happen with the report). School-initiated referral (whose client is the psychologist — the child, the parent, or the school? Say so out loud at the start). Custody and care disputes, where the psychologist should be exceptionally careful about role and about who has instructed them. Research with children, which requires ethics-committee approval, parental consent and child assent, and — under POPIA — a separate lawful basis (§7).

## 2. Confidentiality and its limits

The rule: information obtained in a professional relationship is confidential and may be disclosed only with consent or where law or serious risk requires it.

**The limits, which must be explained at the start, not when they bite:**

1. **Mandatory reporting of child abuse or neglect** (§3). Not discretionary.
2. **Risk of serious harm to self or others.** The duty-to-warn/protect line derives from *Tarasoff* in US law; its status in South African and Namibian law is less clearly settled but the professional obligation to take protective action is not in doubt. Act, document, and consult a colleague or supervisor contemporaneously.
3. **Court order or subpoena.** A subpoena compels production; you may raise objections through the court but you do not simply refuse.
4. **Consultation and supervision.** Legitimate, but should be disclosed and, where possible, de-identified.
5. **Statutory reporting duties** beyond child protection — for example the reporting of sexual offences against children and the reporting duties attaching to certain vulnerable adults.

**Confidentiality with minors specifically.** Parents generally have a right of access to information about their child, but a blanket promise of disclosure to parents destroys therapeutic work with adolescents. The workable practice is to negotiate the boundary explicitly at the outset with both child and parent: *what* will be reported to parents (progress, risk, major decisions) and what will not (session content), and that risk-related information will always be shared. Put it in writing. Where parents are separated, establish who holds parental responsibility and what the court order says before disclosing to either.

**Group and school settings.** In a school you cannot guarantee confidentiality of the fact of contact. Say so. Be careful with staffroom conversation, with information written into school records, and with electronic systems that teachers can browse.

## 3. Mandatory reporting of abuse

**[ZA] Children's Act 38 of 2005, section 110(1)** — verbatim in relevant part: *"Any correctional official, dentist, homeopath, immigration official, labour inspector, legal practitioner, medical practitioner, midwife, minister of religion, nurse, occupational therapist, physiotherapist, **psychologist**, religious leader, social service professional, social worker, speech therapist, teacher, traditional health practitioner, traditional leader or member of staff or volunteer worker at a partial care facility, drop-in centre or child and youth care centre who on reasonable grounds concludes that a child has been abused in a manner causing physical injury, sexually abused or deliberately neglected, must report that conclusion in the prescribed form to a designated child protection organisation, the provincial department of social development or a police official."*

Points to note: the trigger is a **conclusion on reasonable grounds**, not proof; the categories are narrower than "any concern" (physical injury, sexual abuse, deliberate neglect); the report goes to one of three named recipients; and s.110(2) allows **any** person to report a belief that a child is in need of care and protection.

**[NA] Child Care and Protection Act 3 of 2015, section 132** — s.132(1) requires a person who performs professional or official duties with respect to children, and who obtains information giving rise to a **suspicion** that a child is or may be in need of protective services (as defined in s.131), to report it in the prescribed form to a state-employed social worker or a member of the police. s.132(2) names the professions and includes *"school principal, teacher, medical or dental practitioner, pharmacist, school counsellor, dentist, **psychologist, psychological counsellor**, nurse, physiotherapist, speech therapist, occupational therapist, traditional leader, traditional health practitioner, legal practitioner, religious leader, labour inspector, social worker in private practice or employed by a child protection organisation or a member of staff at a place of safety…"*

Three provisions do heavy lifting:
- **s.132(4)** — the duty applies *"irrespective of the fact that the information on which the belief is based is privileged information under any law, but does not apply in the case of legal professional privilege."*
- **s.132(5)** — the reporter's identity is kept confidential where the report is made in good faith, and the reporter incurs **no civil liability** unless the report was known to be false or misleading.
- **s.132(6)** — failure to comply is an **offence**, punishable by a fine not exceeding **N$20 000** or imprisonment not exceeding **five years**, or both.

Note the different threshold: **[NA]** requires reporting on *suspicion* that a child may be in need of protective services, which is a broader and lower trigger than **[ZA]**'s *conclusion on reasonable grounds* of abuse causing physical injury, sexual abuse or deliberate neglect.

**Practically.** Know the prescribed form and where it goes before you need it. Report promptly — delay is itself a failure. Record what you observed, in the child's words where possible, without leading. Do not conduct a forensic interview unless you are trained and mandated to do so; a badly conducted preliminary interview can destroy a prosecution. Tell the child, developmentally appropriately, what you are doing and why — you promised at the outset that this was a limit to confidentiality, and keeping that promise is part of not betraying them. Continue to support the child; the report is not the end of your involvement.

## 4. Dual relationships and boundaries

Dual or multiple relationships arise when the psychologist occupies more than one role with a person — therapist and teacher, assessor and family friend, clinician and business partner. They are not all avoidable and not all unethical, but they impair objectivity and create exploitation risk.

**Absolutely prohibited:** sexual relationships with current clients; in most codes, sexual relationships with former clients for a defined period or indefinitely where the client is vulnerable; sexual relationships with a client's immediate family; and any relationship with a minor client outside the professional one that could be construed as grooming.

**Structurally risky and requiring active management:** small-community practice, which is the reality across most of Namibia and rural South Africa — the psychologist's own children may attend the school, the client may be the only mechanic in town, the referring teacher may be a neighbour. The response is not pretended avoidance but **transparency, documentation, consultation, and explicit boundaries** — plus a genuine willingness to refer where objectivity cannot be maintained, and a clear-eyed recognition that in a district with one psychologist, refusing to see anyone connected to you means seeing no one.

**Also boundary issues:** bartering; gifts (small, culturally appropriate, non-monetary gifts are usually acceptable; anything else is not); social media contact with clients or their families; treating employees, students or supervisees; and accepting referrals from a source in which you have a financial interest.

## 5. Competence and its boundaries

Practise within the boundaries of your training, supervised experience and registration category. This has several sharp edges in the settings covered here:

- **Category boundaries.** An educational psychologist does not treat schizophrenia. A Registered Counsellor does not diagnose or do psychotherapy. A psychometrist does not write the interpretive conclusions.
- **Instrument competence.** Being registered does not qualify you to administer any test. Each instrument requires specific training. Administering a battery you have not been trained on is a competence breach even if you are entitled to use the class of instrument.
- **Cultural and linguistic competence.** Assessing a child through an interpreter in a language you do not speak, on norms derived elsewhere, is a competence question and must be addressed in the report (file `04`).
- **Emerging areas.** Telepsychology, digital assessment and AI-assisted report drafting all raise competence and confidentiality issues that the codes were not written for. Check current board guidance rather than assuming.
- **Supervision.** Ongoing supervision or structured peer consultation is a competence requirement in practice even where it is not mandated. **[ZA]** and **[NA]** continuing professional development is a condition of continued registration.
- **Impairment.** Both councils have processes for impaired practitioners. The obligation to act on one's own impairment, and on a colleague's, is part of the code.

## 6. Record keeping

Records must be accurate, contemporaneous, adequate to support the professional decisions taken, and secure.

- **Content:** referral and its source; consent documentation; history; the raw data and the test protocols; observations; scores with the norms used; formulation; the plan; every session's contemporaneous note; every significant communication including telephone calls; the report issued and to whom.
- **Retention.** **[ZA]** health records are generally required to be retained for a defined minimum period, and records relating to **minors** are typically retained for a longer period running from the age of majority. **The specific retention period for psychological records is set by HPCSA guidance and the National Health Act regulations and could not be verified from this environment — check before destroying anything.**
- **Test data versus test materials.** Raw test protocols and stimulus materials are protected: releasing them compromises test security and is generally restricted to release to another qualified professional rather than directly to a parent or lawyer. Clients are nonetheless entitled to a meaningful account of the findings.
- **Security.** Locked storage for paper; encryption and access control for electronic records; care with email; care with cloud services whose servers are outside the country (§7); a defined process for records when a practice closes or a practitioner dies.

## 7. Data protection

**[ZA] POPIA — Protection of Personal Information Act 4 of 2013.** Section 4(1) sets eight conditions for lawful processing: **accountability** (s.8), **processing limitation** (ss.9–12), **purpose specification** (ss.13–14), **further processing limitation** (s.15), **information quality** (s.16), **openness** (ss.17–18), **security safeguards** (ss.19–22), and **data subject participation** (ss.23–25).

Two layers of extra protection apply directly to educational psychology:

- **Special personal information.** Section 26 prohibits processing information concerning religious or philosophical beliefs, race or ethnic origin, trade union membership, political persuasion, **health or sex life**, or biometric information, and information about criminal behaviour — subject to the authorisations in ss.27–33. Section 32(1)(a) authorises processing by *"medical professionals, healthcare institutions or facilities or social services, if such processing is necessary for the proper treatment and care of the data subject, or for the administration of the institution or professional practice concerned"*; s.32(1)(c) authorises processing by **schools** where necessary *"to provide special support for pupils or making special arrangements in connection with their health or sex life"*; and s.32(1)(d) authorises any public or private body managing the care of a child where necessary for their lawful duties. Section 32(2) restricts this processing to responsible parties **subject to an obligation of confidentiality** by virtue of office, employment, profession or legal provision, or by written agreement.
- **Children's information.** Section 34 prohibits processing personal information concerning a child at all, subject to s.35. Section 35(1) lifts the prohibition where processing is carried out **with the prior consent of a competent person**, or is necessary for the establishment, exercise or defence of a right or obligation in law, or to comply with an obligation of international public law, among other grounds.

Practical POPIA obligations for a practice: a clear privacy notice; a lawful basis recorded for each processing purpose; minimisation (do not collect what you do not need); retention limits; security safeguards including encryption and access control; a breach-notification process; care with cross-border transfers (s.72) which affects any cloud service hosting data outside South Africa; and appointment of an Information Officer.

**[NA]** Namibia has **no comprehensive data protection statute in force** comparable to POPIA at the time of writing; a Data Protection Bill has been under development for some years. Constitutional privacy protections, the confidentiality provisions of the Child Care and Protection Act, professional confidentiality obligations under the HPCNA framework, and common-law privacy remain the operative constraints. **Verify the current status of Namibian data protection legislation** — this is precisely the sort of thing that changes.

## 8. The professional codes

**[ZA] HPCSA.** Practice is governed by the Health Professions Act 56 of 1974 and its regulations, including the ethical rules of conduct for practitioners registered under the Act, together with the Professional Board for Psychology's own rules, policy guidelines and scope-of-practice provisions. Breach is a disciplinary matter with sanctions ranging from a caution to erasure from the register. **The HPCSA website was unreachable from this environment on 2026-08-25 and the specific booklet numbers, rule numbers and current guideline texts are therefore not cited here — obtain them directly.**

**[NA] HPCNA.** The Social Work and Psychology Board of Namibia publishes its governing Act, scope of practice, conditions to practice, guidelines and regulations, CPD requirements and a code of conduct. **These pages were served behind a bot-verification interstitial and could not be read; obtain them directly from HPCNA.**

**APA Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct.** Five general principles — beneficence and nonmaleficence, fidelity and responsibility, integrity, justice, respect for people's rights and dignity — followed by ten enforceable standards covering resolving ethical issues, competence, human relations, privacy and confidentiality, advertising, record keeping and fees, education and training, research and publication, assessment, and therapy. Widely used as a reference code internationally, including in southern African training.

**BPS Code of Ethics and Conduct.** Four ethical principles — respect, competence, responsibility, integrity — supported by practice guidelines and by HCPC's separate, legally binding Standards of Conduct, Performance and Ethics and Standards of Proficiency for practitioner psychologists. Note the UK split: the BPS code is professional and voluntary; **HCPC standards are the regulatory ones**.

## 9. Education legislation relevant to inclusion

**[ZA] Education White Paper 6: Special Needs Education — Building an Inclusive Education and Training System** (Department of Education, July 2001). Its commitments include:
- the qualitative improvement of special schools and their **phased conversion to resource centres** providing professional support to neighbourhood schools, integrated into district-based support teams;
- **overhauling the process of identifying, assessing and enrolling learners in special schools**, replacing it with one that acknowledges the central role played by educators, lecturers and parents;
- designating and phasing in approximately **500 out of 20,000 primary schools as full-service schools**, beginning with the 30 districts in the national district development programme;
- establishing **district-based support teams** to provide coordinated professional support, drawing on expertise in further and higher education and local communities;
- mobilising out-of-school disabled children and youth of school-going age;
- a national advocacy and information programme.

The White Paper's implementation instrument is the **Screening, Identification, Assessment and Support (SIAS)** policy, which establishes a staged process running from teacher-level screening through the school-based support team to the district-based support team, with support provisioning rather than categorisation as its output. *The SIAS policy PDF was not retrievable from this environment — verify clause detail directly with the Department of Basic Education.*

Related **[ZA]** instruments: the South African Schools Act 84 of 1996 (which among other things prohibits corporal punishment), the Constitution's s.28 best-interests provision and s.29 right to basic education, and the Promotion of Access to Information Act.

**[NA]** The Ministry of Education's **Sector Policy on Inclusive Education** is the parallel instrument, supported by the Education Act and by Namibia's obligations under the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities. *The primary PDF was not retrievable from this environment; year of adoption and specific provisions are unverified.*

Both countries are parties to the **UN Convention on the Rights of the Child** and the **African Charter on the Rights and Welfare of the Child**, and both inclusive-education policies descend from the 1994 Salamanca Statement.
