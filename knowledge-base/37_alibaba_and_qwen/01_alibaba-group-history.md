# Alibaba Group — corporate history 1999 to 2026

**Summary.** Alibaba was founded on 28 June 1999 in Hangzhou by Jack Ma and seventeen co-founders as a B2B trade directory, took US$25 million from SoftBank, Goldman Sachs and others in October 1999, added consumer marketplaces (Taobao, 2003) and payments (Alipay, 2004), defeated eBay in China by 2007, and listed on the NYSE in September 2014 in what was then the largest IPO in history — US$25 billion raised at US$68 per share. A decade of expansion ended abruptly in November 2020 when regulators halted the Ant Group IPO; an antitrust fine of RMB 18.2 billion (US$2.8 billion) followed in April 2021, Jack Ma withdrew from public life, and the company spent three years restructuring, shrinking and refocusing. From 2024 the story is an AI pivot: capital expenditure quadrupled from RMB 32.1 billion (FY2024) to RMB 126.1 billion (FY2026), the Qwen model family became the world's most-forked open model line, and by August 2026 cloud and AI were the growth engine while the e-commerce core grew at single digits.

> ⚠️ **Confidence note.** Financial figures in this file are taken from Alibaba's SEC filings and press releases and are high confidence. Narrative history before 2014 is largely taken from a Wikipedia summary and secondary reporting; individual anecdotes (dates of specific investment rounds, the "eighteen founders" framing) are conventional but not independently verified here. Items so affected are flagged `needs-verification`.

## 1999–2003: the B2B directory

Alibaba.com began on 28 June 1999 in Hangzhou as an English-language directory connecting Chinese manufacturers to overseas buyers. The founding group is conventionally described as "the eighteen founders" — Jack Ma and seventeen colleagues, students and friends, meeting in Ma's apartment. Ma, a former English teacher, was not a technologist; the founding team's technical lead was John Wu and the commercial architect was Joseph Tsai, a Taiwanese-Canadian lawyer who left a Swedish private-equity job to join for a nominal salary. The "eighteen founders" framing and individual roles are conventional accounts and are `needs-verification` at the level of individual attribution.

In October 1999 the company raised US$25 million from a syndicate including Investor AB, Goldman Sachs and SoftBank. SoftBank's Masayoshi Son famously committed after a short meeting; the SoftBank stake would become one of the most profitable venture investments ever made, and SoftBank remained Alibaba's largest shareholder for two decades before selling down.

The model in this period was unglamorous: a paid membership listing service ("China Gold Supplier") for exporters, with a large direct sales force. It survived the dot-com collapse precisely because it charged money for something businesses wanted.

## 2003–2007: Taobao and the eBay fight

eBay entered China in 2002–2003 by acquiring EachNet, then the market leader. Alibaba's response, launched in 2003, was Taobao — a consumer-to-consumer marketplace that charged sellers nothing. Free listings, no transaction commission, and a chat tool (Wangwang) that let buyers and sellers haggle directly.

Three things decided the contest:

1. **Price.** eBay charged listing and final-value fees; Taobao did not. In a market of tiny margin traders, this was decisive.
2. **Trust.** Chinese consumers in 2004 would not pay strangers in advance. Alipay, launched in 2004 as an escrow service — the buyer's money is held until the goods arrive — solved the trust problem that was actually blocking the market.
3. **Localisation.** eBay routed EachNet onto its global platform and global product decisions. Taobao iterated locally and weekly.

By 2007 Taobao's share was decisive and eBay had effectively withdrawn from the China consumer market. Alibaba monetised Taobao not through commissions but through advertising and search placement — "customer management revenue" in today's accounts — which remains the group's largest single revenue line (RMB 343,867 million in FY2026).

Tmall (originally Taobao Mall, 2008) split the model: an invitation-only B2C platform for brands and authorised retailers, charging commission and deposits, sitting alongside the C2C long tail of Taobao.

## 2010–2011: the Alipay spin-off controversy

In 2010 Alipay's ownership was transferred out of the Alibaba Group structure into an entity controlled by Jack Ma personally. Alibaba's largest shareholders — Yahoo! (~40%) and SoftBank — subsequently stated they had not approved the transfer in advance. Alibaba's justification was regulatory: new PRC rules on third-party payment licences (the People's Bank of China's non-bank payment regulations) were understood to require domestic ownership, and a foreign-invested VIE structure risked the licence.

The dispute was settled in July 2011 with a compensation framework: Alibaba Group would receive a payment on any future Alipay liquidity event, capped in the original settlement. The episode is the canonical illustration of VIE risk — that a US-listed Chinese company's economic interest in its operating assets rests on contracts rather than equity — and it is still cited in Alibaba's own risk factors, which devote pages to the VIE structure.

The relationship was renegotiated repeatedly (the SAPA, amended in 2018, 2019, 2020 and 2022) until, in September 2019, Alibaba received a **33% equity interest in Ant Group on a fully diluted basis** — the position it still holds, accounted for under the equity method.

## 2014: the NYSE IPO

Alibaba listed on the New York Stock Exchange on 19 September 2014 (pricing on the 18th) under the ticker BABA. Terms:

- **Price:** US$68 per American Depositary Share
- **Raised:** approximately US$25 billion including the greenshoe — the largest IPO in history at that time, exceeding the previous record (Agricultural Bank of China, 2010)
- **Implied market value:** approximately US$231 billion at the offer price
- **Structure:** Cayman Islands holding company; the "Alibaba Partnership" — not a dual-class share structure but a partnership holding the right to nominate a majority of the board — which is why Alibaba listed in New York rather than Hong Kong, whose rules at the time did not permit it

Each ADS represents eight ordinary shares. A secondary listing in Hong Kong followed in November 2019 (codes 9988 HKD counter, 89988 RMB counter), and the Hong Kong listing was converted to a primary dual-primary listing in 2022–2024, which mattered for Stock Connect eligibility and for delisting risk under the US Holding Foreign Companies Accountable Act.

## The Singles' Day phenomenon

11 November — "Singles' Day", 11.11 — was appropriated by Alibaba in 2009 as a shopping promotion and grew into the largest retail event in the world. Disclosed gross merchandise volume:

| Year | GMV |
|---|---|

| 2014 | US$9.3 bn |
| 2015 | US$14.3 bn |
| 2016 | US$17.8 bn |

| 2019 | RMB 268.4 bn (US$38.4 bn) |
| 2021 | RMB 540.3 bn (US$114.4 bn) — orders |

From 2022 Alibaba stopped disclosing a headline GMV figure, saying results were "in line with" the prior year and citing macroeconomic headwinds and zero-COVID disruption. The retreat from disclosure is itself informative: GMV had become a vanity metric under regulatory scrutiny (the SAMR's interest in "choose one of two" exclusivity practices made inflated promotional numbers awkward), and the growth story had moved elsewhere. The FY2026 20-F still names the 11.11 Global Shopping Festival as the driver of fourth-calendar-quarter seasonality but attaches no figure.

## November 2020: the Ant IPO suspension and the regulatory winter

Ant Group's dual Shanghai–Hong Kong IPO was scheduled for early November 2020 and would have been the largest in history, valuing Ant above US$300 billion. On 24 October 2020 Jack Ma gave a speech at the Bund Summit in Shanghai criticising Chinese financial regulation — comparing banks to pawnshops and arguing that Basel-style rules were a brake on innovation. Within days regulators summoned Ant's leadership; on 3 November 2020 the Shanghai Stock Exchange suspended the listing, and Hong Kong followed.

What followed was not a single action but a two-year regulatory campaign across the Chinese platform sector:

- **9 April 2021:** the State Administration for Market Regulation fined Alibaba **RMB 18.2 billion (approximately US$2.8 billion)** for abuse of dominance — specifically the "choose one of two" (`er xuan yi`) practice of requiring merchants to sell exclusively on Alibaba platforms. The fine was set at 4% of Alibaba's 2019 China revenue and represented roughly 12% of 2020 net profit. It was, at the time, the largest antitrust penalty in Chinese history.
- **Ant restructuring:** Ant was compelled to restructure as a financial holding company under PBOC supervision, to break the link between its consumer-credit products (Huabei, Jiebei) and its lending partners' balance sheets, and to fold its credit-scoring operations into a regulated joint venture. Ant's valuation fell by roughly two-thirds on secondary marks.
- **Sector-wide:** data-security law, personal-information protection law, algorithm regulation, restrictions on minors' gaming, the after-school tutoring ban, and delisting pressure from the US side simultaneously. Chinese internet equities lost trillions of dollars of market value between February 2021 and October 2022.

**Jack Ma's withdrawal.** Ma had already stepped back from executive roles — he relinquished the CEO title in 2013 and the chairmanship on 10 September 2019, his 55th birthday, handing to Daniel Zhang. After October 2020 he disappeared from public view almost entirely, resurfacing occasionally abroad (Japan, Spain, Thailand) and later in a low-profile academic role. He gave up control of Ant Group in January 2023 through a restructuring of the voting arrangements at Hangzhou Yunbo, reducing his voting power from over 50% to approximately 6.2%. He has made occasional symbolic appearances since — including an internal letter in 2023 urging Taobao to correct course — but he holds no executive position. This is `needs-verification` at the level of the specific voting percentages, which are drawn from Ant's own disclosures via secondary reporting.

## 2023: the "1+6+N" restructuring and its reversal

On 28 March 2023 Alibaba announced the largest reorganisation in its history: the group would become a holding company ("1") over six independently-governed business groups ("6"), each with its own board and CEO and each free to raise external capital and pursue its own IPO, plus a set of other companies ("N"). The six:

1. Cloud Intelligence Group
2. Taobao and Tmall Group
3. Cainiao Smart Logistics Network
4. Local Services Group (Ele.me, Amap)
5. Alibaba International Digital Commerce Group (AliExpress, Lazada, Trendyol, Daraz)
6. Digital Media and Entertainment Group

The logic was partly regulatory (a federation is a smaller antitrust target than a monolith), partly financial (unlock conglomerate discount via separate listings), and partly operational (restore entrepreneurial urgency).

**It was substantially reversed.** Within a year the plan unwound:

- The Cloud Intelligence Group spin-off was **cancelled in November 2023**, with the company explicitly citing US export controls on advanced computing chips as creating uncertainty that would undermine a separately-listed cloud business. This is the single clearest documented instance of US chip policy reshaping Alibaba's corporate structure.
- The **Cainiao IPO was withdrawn in March 2024**, with Alibaba instead offering to buy in minority shareholders.
- The Freshippo (Hema) listing was shelved.

By FY2026 the reporting structure had already been simplified — Taobao and Tmall Group, Ele.me and Fliggy were combined into **Alibaba China E-commerce Group**, while Cainiao, Amap and the media group were pushed into "All others". And in the quarter ended 30 June 2026 the structure collapsed again into four segments (see `02`). The federated experiment lasted roughly three years in name and rather less in substance.

## Leadership

| Period | Chairman | CEO |
|---|---|---|
| 1999–2013 | Jack Ma (executive chairman from 2013) | Jack Ma |
| 2013–2015 | Jack Ma | Jonathan Lu |
| 2015–2019 | Jack Ma | Daniel Zhang (Zhang Yong) |
| Sep 2019 – Sep 2023 | Daniel Zhang | Daniel Zhang |
| Sep 2023 – present | Joseph Tsai | Eddie Wu (Wu Yongming) |

Daniel Zhang, an accountant by training, is credited with creating the 11.11 festival and building Tmall, and led the company through the IPO era and the regulatory winter. In the 2023 restructuring he was to become CEO of Cloud Intelligence Group while relinquishing the group roles; in September 2023 he left that position too and departed the company entirely — an abrupt exit widely read as a strategic disagreement.

**Eddie Wu** is one of the original eighteen founders and a technologist (he was Alibaba's first technical lead on the Taobao build and later ran Alipay's technology). He took direct control of the Taobao and Tmall business as well as the group CEO role, and later of the cloud business, and his stated strategy has been "user first, AI-driven".

**Joseph Tsai**, co-founder and long-time vice chairman, took the chairmanship. His public communication has been notably direct about the AI opportunity and about the company's earlier failures in consumer experience.

## 2024–2026: the AI pivot

The recovery has three visible components.

**1. Capital expenditure.** The clearest single number in this story:

| Fiscal year (to 31 March) | Capex |
|---|---|
| FY2024 | RMB 32,087 m |
| FY2025 | RMB 85,972 m |
| FY2026 | RMB 126,063 m (US$18,275 m) |
| Quarter ended 30 Jun 2026 alone | RMB 67,678 m (US$9,975 m), +75% YoY |

The June 2026 quarter's capex alone exceeded the whole of FY2024. Alibaba attributes the acceleration to procurement-cycle timing, expanded CPU-compute capacity for AI agents, and "higher pricing of a broad range of chip components". Capital commitments contracted but not provided for stood at RMB 54,136 million (US$7,848 million) at 31 March 2026.

The financing followed: on 23–24 August 2026 Alibaba announced and priced a **HK$80 billion placing of new shares** in Hong Kong.

**2. Models and applications.** Qwen went from a research release in 2023 to the world's most-derived open model family. The FY2026 20-F names Qwen3.7-Max as the then-current flagship and reports "three updates to the Qwen family within the past three months". By August 2026 the open flagship was Qwen3.8-Max (released as Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B with 2.4 trillion parameters on 12 August 2026). On the application side: the **Qwen app** launched November 2025 as a consumer assistant integrated with Taobao, Tmall, Taobao Instant Commerce, Fliggy, Damai, Amap and Alipay; **Wukong**, an enterprise agent platform; and **QwenWork**, a workforce agent platform integrated with DingTalk. Alibaba reports 250 million users have had "their first AI-driven shopping experience" through the Qwen app's agentic features.

**3. The financial cost of the pivot.** The AI investment is visibly expensive. FY2026 operating income fell to RMB 50,150 million from RMB 140,905 million in FY2025 — a 64% decline on 3% revenue growth. In the quarter ended 30 June 2026, income from operations fell 57% year over year to RMB 15,161 million, adjusted EBITA fell 30%, net income fell 75%, and free cash flow was an outflow of RMB 44,670 million against an outflow of RMB 18,815 million a year earlier. The AI Labs and Applications segment alone posted adjusted EBITA of **negative RMB 13,861 million** in that quarter.

Simultaneously the company was fighting an expensive instant-commerce war: China Quick Commerce revenue grew 45% to RMB 53,295 million while customer management revenue — the historic profit engine — *fell* 7%.

**The bet, stated plainly.** Alibaba is spending its e-commerce profits and new equity to buy a position in AI infrastructure and applications, accepting a collapse in reported profitability to do it. The June 2026 quarter is the first in which the cloud/AI segment showed the payoff shape management wants: +45% revenue growth with adjusted EBITA up 133% to RMB 5,628 million and a 12% EBITA margin. Whether that continues is the whole question.

## Geopolitical overlay

Two items from the FY2026 20-F belong in any honest history:

- **February 2026:** the US Department of Defense added Alibaba Group to the Chinese Military Companies (CMC) list, then withdrew the list. Alibaba's filing notes there is "no assurance that Alibaba Group will not be included in the list when it is republished." CMC designation does not by itself trigger blocking sanctions but prohibits DoD dealings and carries reputational and supply-chain consequences.
- **January 2026:** the US House of Representatives passed the Remote Access Security Act, which if enacted would restrict China-based companies' access to compute resources of providers powered by advanced US chips — i.e. it would close the remaining route by which a Chinese firm can rent, rather than buy, restricted hardware.

Together with the 2022-onward export controls and the September 2025 Chinese instruction to domestic firms to halt certain chip purchases, these define the operating environment in which Alibaba's silicon programme (`03`) and its open-weight strategy (`08`) were chosen.
