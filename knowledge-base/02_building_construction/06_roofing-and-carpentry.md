# Roofing and carpentry

**Summary.** A roof in northern Namibia has three jobs: shed the rain that arrives in a few violent months, survive the wind, and keep the sun off the ceiling. Structurally the roof is a set of nail-plate trusses or rafters at 760–1 100 mm centres, anchored to the walls with galvanised straps or wire embedded 300–600 mm into the masonry. The covering is almost always IBR or corrugated steel sheeting (minimum pitch 5° and 10° respectively for short slopes) or concrete tiles (minimum 17°). This file gives the timber grades and treatment classes, truss and purlin spacings, sheeting fixing patterns, tile battening gauges, flashing and gutter practice, and the uplift detail that keeps the roof on.

## 1. Timber

### Grading

South African structural pine is graded to **SANS 1783-2** (and SANS 1460 / SANS 10149 for related products; SANS 1783-4 covers roof battens) into grades **S5, S7 and S10**. Design is to **SANS 10163** (*The structural use of timber*) and timber building construction to **SANS 10082**. Roof truss requirements sit in **SANS 10400 Part L**.

Characteristic grade stresses for SA pine (SANS 10163-1), MPa:

| Grade | Bending f_b | Tension ∥ grain | Tension ⊥ grain | Compression ∥ grain | Compression ⊥ grain | Shear ∥ grain | E_min | E_mean |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 11,5 | 6,7 | 0,36 | 18,0 | 4,7 | 1,6 | 4 875 | 7 800 |
| 7 | 15,8 | 10,0 | 0,51 | 22,8 | 6,7 | 2,0 | 6 000 | 9 600 |
| 10 | 23,3 | 13,3 | 0,73 | 26,2 | 9,1 | 2,9 | 7 500 | 12 000 |
| EH | 20,8 | 14,1 | 0,30 | 19,3 | 3,9 | 2,2 | 7 041 | 9 826 |

Practical reading: S5 is the general-purpose grade for battens, brandering and light framing; S7 is the standard grade for trusses and rafters; S10 gives a third more bending capacity but is scarce and priced accordingly.

**Specify kiln-dried timber** unless you know how to design with green timber. Wet off-saw timber has not been graded and must not be used structurally without proof loading.

### Preservative treatment (SANS 457, SANS 10005, hazard classes)

| Class | Exposure | Typical uses |
|---|---|---|
| **H2** | Internal, above ground | Roof trusses, structural timber, ceiling boards, flooring, panelling, doors, window frames, plywood |
| **H3** | Exterior, above ground | Fascias, barge boards, decking, balustrades, cladding, weatherboards, gates, stairs, garden furniture |
| **H4** | Exterior, in ground | Fence posts, carport poles, pergolas, playground structures, agricultural posts |
| **H5** | Freshwater / heavy wet soils | Retaining walls, jetties, walkways, foundation poles and pilings, poles under irrigation |
| **H6** | Marine, in seawater | Harbour walls, jetties, marine walkways — CCA with creosote dual treatment |

Preservatives available in South Africa: **CCA** (copper-chrome-arsenate, waterborne, light green), **creosote** (brown to black, oily, high water repellency, poor paintability, "afterglow" fire risk in veld fires), **borate** (colourless, waterborne, but leaches — not for outdoor or in-ground use), and **CuAz / ACQ** (copper-based, limited distribution), plus LOSP for some interior products.

Buying rules:
- Buy only from **NRCS-approved producers with SANAS-accredited third-party certification**. "Fly-by-night" treaters produce timber that looks treated and fails in a few years.
- The mark must show the producer's identity/trademark, the **year of treatment**, the quality mark of a SANAS accredited body, and **SANS 457 plus the H class**.
- Treated timber must have both the correct **retention** (quantity of preservative) and the correct **penetration** into the sapwood; poles must be seasoned and graded before treatment.
- **Leave CCA-treated timber at least seven days after treatment before fixing metal fasteners or fittings.**
- Allow freshly treated timber to dry and equalise on site before use.

> ⚠️ Every cut end and every drilled hole in treated timber exposes untreated heartwood. Brush-apply an approved end-grain preservative to all site cuts, especially on H3 fascias and H4 poles set in the ground.

### Storage on a hot site

Stack timber flat, level, off the ground on bearers at ≤ 1,5 m centres, with stickers between layers for air movement, and cover it. SA pine left flat in 38 °C sun for a week bows and case-hardens; left on uneven ground it takes a permanent set. Trusses are especially vulnerable — store them **vertical and braced**, never flat.

## 2. Roof structure

### Trusses

For anything above a small span, use **nail-plate trusses designed and fabricated by an ITC-SA registered fabricator**. What the fabricator needs from you:
- Overall span (wall-plate to wall-plate)
- Eaves overhang and cantilever distance
- Roof pitch
- Truss spacing (the truss engineer can optimise this) — **normally 760 mm for concrete tiles and 1 100 mm for sheeting**
- Roofing material and its mass
- Ceiling type and load, and whether any load is suspended (geyser, PV, air conditioner)
- Hatch positions, gable/hip configuration, girder truss locations
- Wind region and terrain category

What you must check on delivery: that the truss profile, span and pitch match the drawing; that no nail plate has been dislodged in transit; that no chord is split at a plate; that the correct number of girder trusses arrived. A damaged truss is not a site repair — it goes back.

**Erection**: set trusses to the marked positions on the wall plate, plumb the first truss and brace it back to a fixed point, then run the temporary bracing along the top chords as you go. Permanent bracing (rafter bracing, web bracing, longitudinal ties) is part of the design and must be installed exactly as drawn. More truss failures come from missing bracing than from undersized members.

### Rafters and purlins (cut roof)

For simple sheeted roofs, a cut roof of rafters and purlins is often cheaper and easier at a remote site than trucking trusses from Ondangwa.
- Rafters: 38×114 or 38×152 S5/S7 at 760–1 100 mm centres depending on span and load.
- Purlins: 50×76 or 76×50 laid flat over rafters, at the spacing required by the sheeting profile and gauge.
- Wall plate: 76×50 or 114×38 bedded on mortar on the top course, anchored with the strap/wire anchors described below.
- Every rafter must be birdsmouthed onto the wall plate — never simply skew-nailed to the side.

### Battens (tiled roof)

**38 × 38 mm** for a 100 mm headlap, **38 × 50 mm** for a 75 mm headlap. Verge battens are heavier: 38×50 for profiled tiles, 38×76 for flat tiles. Ridge batten is 38×38 mm, carried on ridge batten trees, set so there is a 38 mm gap under the ridge tile.

**Calculating batten gauge:** for a pitch below 26° with ~100 mm tile overlap the maximum batten gauge is **320 mm**. Divide the rafter length (apex to outside edge of fascia) by 320 mm, round **up** to a whole number of courses, then divide the rafter length by that number of courses to get the actual gauge. Example: rafter length 4,500 m → 4 500 / 320 = 14,06 → 15 courses → gauge = 4 500 / 15 = **300 mm**. The Coverland tables tabulate this for every rafter length.

## 3. Roof pitch by covering

| Covering | Minimum pitch | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| IBR 686 profile | **5°** (slopes ≤ 30 m); **7,5°** (slopes > 30 m) | Seal end and side laps on low pitches |
| Corrugated (8,5 / 10,5) | **10°** (slopes < 15,8 m); **12,5°** (slopes > 15,8 m) | Deeper laps than IBR |
| Concrete interlocking tiles | **17°** | 17–25° = low pitch, needs 100 mm headlap and mandatory undertile membrane |
| Concrete tiles, normal | 26–35° | 75 mm headlap; membrane recommended |
| Concrete tiles, high | ≥ 36° | Membrane mandatory above 45°, every tile nailed and clipped |
| Thatch | typically ≥ 45° | Heavy roof for anchor selection; outside the scope of this file |

Pitch also drives the anchor selection (see §6) and the truss geometry. On a hot site there is a case for a *steeper* roof than the minimum: it creates a deeper roof void, which buffers the ceiling from radiant heat.

## 4. Sheeting

### IBR 686

- Cover width 686 mm, overall width 740 mm, rib height 37 mm, five trapezoidal ribs at 171,5 mm centres.
- Thickness range 0,3–0,8 mm; 0,5 mm and 0,58 mm are the common domestic gauges. Lengths to 15 m with a length tolerance of ±5 mm.
- Can be cranked to a minimum radius of 409 mm at 0,5/0,53/0,58 mm; curved to any radius above 800 mm.
- **Laps:** end lap ≥ 150 mm at pitch > 15°, ≥ 250 mm otherwise; side lap ≥ 100 mm (one rib). Seal end and side laps on low-pitch roofs.
- **Fasteners:** 65 mm Top Speed / Tek screws into steel purlins; **90 mm** Tek or Top Speed screws into timber. All fasteners with a **26 mm bonded (EPDM) washer**.
- **Fixing pattern:** fixed **through the crests of alternate ribs** to each purlin — i.e. 3 fasteners per sheet per purlin on a 5-rib sheet, plus the side-lap fastener. At eaves, ridge and end purlins fix **every rib**. Stitch side laps between purlins at ~500 mm centres on exposed sites.

### Corrugated

- 8,5-corrugation: 700 mm cover. 10,5-corrugation: 840 mm cover. Gauges 0,50 and 0,58 mm in galvanised or Chromadek.
- Mass: 4,882 kg/m² at 0,50 mm; 5,486 kg/m² at 0,58 mm.
- **Purlin spacing:** single span ≤ 0,60 m; double span ≤ 1,10 m.
- **Laps:** end lap ≥ 150 mm at pitch > 16°, ≥ 250 mm otherwise; side lap **1½ corrugations**; side cladding end laps ≥ 100 mm.
- **Fasteners:** 38 mm Tek screws into steel purlins, 65 mm Tek screws into timber, all with a **19 mm bonded washer**.
- Fix **through the crests**, never the valleys. A screw through a valley is a leak with a warranty exclusion attached.

### Sheeting practice

- Lay sheets starting from the end **away from the prevailing wind**, so the side laps face away from the driving rain.
- Do not over-tighten screws — the washer should be compressed, not extruded. Over-tightening dimples the crest and the dimple ponds water.
- Never walk in the pans between purlins on 0,4–0,5 mm sheeting; walk over the purlin line.
- Cut sheeting with a nibbler or shears, not an angle grinder: grinder sparks embed hot steel particles in the coating and produce rust freckles within months.
- Remove all swarf and offcuts from the roof at the end of each day, for the same reason.

## 5. Tiles

- Minimum headlap **100 mm at 17–25°**, **75 mm at ≥ 26°**. Tiles laid straight bond (except some flat profiles).
- **Undertile membrane** (breather membrane or radiant barrier): mandatory below 26° and above 45°, and at all pitches in exposed and coastal areas; recommended between 26° and 45°.
- **Mechanical fixing.** Wind uplift on a tiled roof depends on basic design wind speed, building type, roof pitch, building height and roof length. At lower pitches **clips** resist uplift better than nails, because a nail sits close to the pivot line where the nib touches the batten and cannot resist the lifting force at the tail; at steeper pitches nailing prevents the tile being dislodged and sliding. In practice: perimeter courses (eaves, verge, ridge, hips, valleys, and around any opening) are always nailed **and** clipped, plus a proportion of the general field per the wind calculation.
- Nail lengths by profile (Marley): Cupola 100 mm; Clay Tile 50 mm; Double Roman 63 mm; Elite 50 mm; Taunus 75 mm; rake verge 75 mm; tile clips 50 mm (Elite 25 mm). Nails 2,8 mm gauge serrated shank. **Coastal (within 30 km):** aluminium alloy or non-corrodible serrated clout-head nails.
- Cut hip and valley tiles must be secured (e.g. with a purpose-made clamp clip wired to the hip batten) to prevent slip-out.
- Bed and point the ridge in mortar, or use a dry-ridge system: ridge roll centred on the ridge batten, secured at 1 m intervals, ridge tiles clipped, every 4th ridge tile additionally secured.

## 6. Wind uplift and roof anchoring

This is the safety-critical detail. From SANS 10400-K clause 4.2.11:

Anchor types: **A** = two strands of 2,4 mm galvanized steel wire; **B** = 30 × 1,2 mm galvanized steel strap; **C** = 30 × 1,6 mm galvanized steel strap.

| Roof slope | Max truss/rafter spacing | Anchor for a light roof | Heavy roof |
|---|---|---|---|
| < 15° | 760 mm | A, B or C | — |
| < 15° | 1 050 mm | B or C | — |
| < 15° | 1 350 mm | C | — |
| 15–30° | 760 mm | A, B or C | A for all applications |
| 15–30° | 1 050 mm | B or C | |
| 15–30° | 1 350 mm | C | |
| > 30° | any | A, B or C | |

Wire is **not** permitted for lightweight roof coverings unless truss/rafter spacing is 760 mm maximum.

Minimum embedment (Table 30, solid units): **light roof 600 mm**, **heavy roof 300 mm**. Straps embedded in mortar in bed joints must have **≥ 70 mm** of embedment in the joint.

*Light roof* = metal profile sheeting, metal roof tiles, fibre-cement sheeting or fibre-cement slates. *Heavy roof* = concrete tiles, clay tiles, slates or thatch.

Practical anchoring sequence: build the strap into the wall as the courses go up (never chase it in afterwards), leave the tail long, bend it over the wall plate and the truss heel, and nail with the full complement of clout nails through every hole in the strap. On a low-pitch IBR roof at 1 100 mm truss centres you need **Type B or C strap**, embedded **600 mm** — that is roughly seven brick courses. Plan it at wall-plate-minus-seven-courses, not at wall plate level.

## 7. Flashings, gutters and rainwater

- **Flashings** at every abutment: apron flashing where the roof meets a wall below, side/step flashing up a rake, back gutter above a chimney or parapet, valley gutter between planes. Minimum upstand into a chase or joint 25 mm, chase raked out and repointed or sealed. Never rely on sealant alone.
- **Ridge and hip caps** to suit the sheeting profile, with closure pieces (foam or steel) in the profile to keep out driven rain, dust, birds and bats.
- **Barge and fascia:** H3-treated timber, or a fibre-cement or steel fascia. Timber fascia in this climate must be primed on **all six faces** before fixing.
- **Gutters:** in a semi-arid climate the whole point of the gutter is rainwater harvesting, so size and detail them for capture, not just disposal. Fall of about **1:200 to 1:350** to the outlet; support brackets at ≤ 1,0 m centres, closer on long runs; expansion joints on runs over ~10 m. Use a first-flush diverter and a leaf/dust screen before the tank — dust load in Ohangwena is heavy.
- **Downpipes:** discharge onto a concrete apron or into a stormwater pipe that carries the water **at least 1,5–2 m away from the wall**. Discharging a downpipe next to a footing on collapsible sand is the classic route to settlement cracks.

## 8. Ceilings

- **Gypsum plasterboard on brandering**: 38×38 mm SA pine brandering at 400 mm centres, cross-brandering as required, boards fixed with drywall screws, joints taped and skimmed. Good finish, poor thermal performance on its own.
- **Fibre-cement ceiling board**: 3,2 or 4,5 mm on brandering with cover strips or flush-jointed. More robust in humid or wet areas.
- **PVC / isoboard**: quick, washable, common in Namibian low-cost work.
- **Exposed truss with sheeting only**: cheapest, thermally unacceptable in this climate.

**Insulation matters more here than the ceiling material.** Under an uninsulated steel roof, ceiling void temperatures well above 50 °C are routine. A minimum of 100–135 mm of glass-wool or polyester insulation over the ceiling, plus a foil radiant barrier (double-sided foil or a foil-faced membrane) under the sheeting, transforms internal comfort and cuts cooling load. Ventilate the roof void: eaves vents plus ridge or gable vents so hot air escapes rather than radiating down.

> ⚠️ Keep insulation clear of recessed downlights and of any chimney or flue by the manufacturer's stated distance, and never bury an electrical junction box under insulation.

## 9. Roof work safety

**[NA]** GN 156/1997 reg 248: an employer must provide protection against falling from a roof; and must provide and cause to be used **suitable roof ladders, duck-boards or crawling-boards** for work on any roof with a pitch steeper than **1 : 4** (about 14°), or with a slippery surface, or covered with material through which a person could fall — unless suitable safety belts attached to the structure prevent a person falling through the roof. **[ZA]** Construction Regulation 10 requires a documented **fall protection plan**, implemented and adhered to, with the construction manager holding the current version; no employee may work on roofs during inclement weather or if the wind makes it unsafe.

Fragile-roof rule: fibre-cement and translucent sheeting will not carry a person's weight. Always work off crawling boards on those.

## Open questions

- Numerical CCA **retention values (kg/m³) per hazard class** under SANS 10005 / SANS 457 were not published in the SAWPA material consulted and are marked `needs-verification`.
- IBR **load–span tables by gauge** are described by manufacturers as guidance only and were not obtainable; purlin spacing for IBR must be taken from the specific manufacturer's current load–span table or from an engineer.
- Rafter and purlin size/span tables for cut roofs in SA pine were not sourced from SANS 10082 and the sizes given are indicative trade practice.
- Wind loading for the Ohangwena region (SANS 10160-3 basic wind speed) was not verified; truss and anchor design must use the correct regional wind speed.
