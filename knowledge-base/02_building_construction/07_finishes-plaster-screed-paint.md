# Finishes — plaster, screed, tiling and paint

**Summary.** Finishes are where a building is judged, and where hot dry weather does the most damage. Plaster to **SANS 2001-EM1** is a 1 : 5 or 1 : 6 cement : sand mix applied 10–16 mm thick in a single coat, and it must be accurate to **6 mm under a 2 m straight edge**. Screeds are 50 kg cement to 130 ℓ coarse sand, laid 25–40 mm thick and finished to a surface-regularity class. Tiling requires the substrate to have cured — **6 weeks for concrete, 4 weeks for screed** — with movement joints at **3 m centres** and around every perimeter. Paint goes on last, over a substrate that is dry, clean and alkali-primed. The sequence of trades matters more than any individual skill: every chase, conduit and pipe must be in the wall before the plasterer starts.

## 1. Plaster

### Materials

**Cement.** CEM I and CEM II/A give good results. **CEM II/B-V or B-W** (fly-ash extended) are recommended for plaster that will be damp in service — plinths below DPC, free-standing walls — because they reduce efflorescence. Cements with slow early strength development should be used only where the plaster can be genuinely protected from sun and wind. Masonry cement (SANS 50413) may be used per SANS 2001-EM1.

**Sand.** Sand is the majority of the plaster and decides its behaviour. Requirements:
- No organic matter — roots, seeds, twigs, humus. Absolutely essential for white or pigmented plaster.
- Continuous grading from dust to the largest particles; SANS 1090 grading for plaster sand: 100 % passing 4,75 mm; 90–100 % at 2,36 mm; 70–100 % at 1,18 mm; 40–90 % at 600 µm; 5–65 % at 300 µm; 5–20 % at 150 µm.
- Maximum particle size: all sand through a **2,36 mm** sieve for smooth plaster; 4,75 mm for coarse textured work.
- Rounded, smooth particles. **Crusher sands are not generally suitable** for plaster (angular shape) except in rich mixes for special applications.
- **Karoo sands with a high proportion of disc-shaped dark particles must not be used** — they swell and shrink excessively and cause shrinkage cracking.
- Ash is not recommended as aggregate.

**Field test for sand quality (plaster):** mix 2,5 kg cement with 12,5 kg sand to uniform colour on a non-absorbent surface, then add water slowly to plastering consistence. 2,5 ℓ = **good**; 3 ℓ = **average**; 3,75 ℓ = **poor**; more = **very poor**. Only *good* sand for all plaster work; *average* may be used for interior plaster only.

**Lime.** SANS 523 type A2P. Up to one 25 kg bag (40 ℓ loose) per bag of common cement. Improves workability, plasticity and water retention; slightly reduces compressive strength but improves bond and reduces permeability. Not with masonry cement.

**Admixtures.** To SANS 50934. The common "mortar plasticisers" are air-entraining agents — **never use them with masonry cement**. Bonding liquids are effective but are not a substitute for surface preparation.

> ⚠️ **Never mix a gypsum-based plaster with a portland-cement plaster.** Sulphate from the gypsum attacks the cement paste in damp conditions, causing swelling, softening and disintegration. The only remedy is to remove and replace the plaster.

### Surface preparation

Substrate requirements: accurately positioned, not deviating excessively from plane, **rough**, **limitedly absorbent**, strong, and clean.

- Where zones deviate from plane by more than about **10 mm**, hack off the high areas first. If that is impractical, build up the low areas with undercoats so the final coat is uniform in thickness.
- Where the overall thickness must exceed the recommended range, mechanically anchor the plaster (e.g. stainless steel studs). Same for dense non-absorbent substrates.
- Roughness options: rough-sawn formwork; early stripping and wire brushing; hacking; abrasive blasting; **raking out mortar joints ~10 mm deep**; spatterdash.
- **Spatterdash** = 1 part cement (preferably CEM I or CEM II/A) : 1½ parts coarse sand, water to a sluggishly pourable consistence, with a polymer emulsion substituted for ¼–⅓ of the mixing water. Flick it on to cover completely, forming nodules about 5 mm high. **Do not let it dry out for at least three days.** Test its adhesion by probing with a screwdriver before plastering over it.
- Cleaning: water jetting, oil-free compressed air, vacuum, brushing. **Curing-compound films must be removed mechanically, never with solvents.**
- **Absorption test:** throw about a cupful of water at the wall.
  - Category I — no water absorbed (hard-burnt face brick, glazed brick, dense high-strength concrete): apply a spatterdash coat including polymer emulsion. **Do not pre-wet.**
  - Category II — some absorbed, most runs off: no suction control needed.
  - Category III — most absorbed (burnt clay stock brick, very absorbent units): wet thoroughly for at least one hour, then let it reach saturated-surface-dry before plastering.

Before plastering starts: **all chases completed, all electrical and plumbing conduits, boxes and pipes fixed in position, and tested.** Pigmented plaster cannot be patched invisibly, so with pigmented work this is absolute.

### Application

- **Clad the roof before plastering starts.** Never work in direct sun. Protect plaster from sun and drying wind; on an exposed wall in hot windy conditions take special measures or the plaster will crack.
- Apply **screed strips** (narrow plaster guide strips at the perimeter or at intervals) so the striker board runs true.
- Apply with heavy pressure using a rectangular trowel to compact the plaster and get full contact; leave it slightly proud.
- Once it starts to stiffen, strike off to a plane with a light striker board. **Discard the struck-off material** — do not reuse it.
- If more than one coat: score the undercoat with roughly parallel lines about **20 mm apart and 5 mm deep** — this keys the next coat and distributes cracking.
- Finish coat: wood float to remove striker ridges and fill depressions. A steel trowel gives a very smooth surface but very smooth surfaces **craze** and show every imperfection — a wood-float finish is generally better.
- Use up the batch within two hours; **never retemper**.
- **Cut the plaster through to the substrate at the DPC line and where different substrate materials meet** (masonry to concrete). Plaster carried across a DPC bridges it.

Accuracy: SANS 2001-EM1:2007 — *"the permissible deviations in plaster shall not exceed 6 mm under a 2 m straight edge."*

### Coverage

100 m² at 15 mm thickness: external plaster 700 kg cement + 2,25 m³ sand (common cement); internal 600 kg + 2,3 m³. Yield per batch of 50 kg cement: 120 ℓ (external, 150 ℓ sand), 150 ℓ (internal, 200 ℓ sand). Area from a batch = yield ÷ thickness ÷ wastage factor — e.g. 150 ÷ 15 ÷ 1,2 = **8,3 m²** per bag for a 1 : 6 internal mix at 15 mm with 20 % wastage.

## 2. Screeds and toppings

### Definitions

**Screed** — a layer of well-compacted sand-cement applied to a base to receive a floor finish. Light duty only: utility rooms, garages, floors under carpet, vinyl or linoleum with light traffic. **Not** suitable as a wearing surface in commercial or industrial premises.
**Topping** — a layer of high-strength concrete providing an abrasion-resistant surface or adding structural depth.

Three types: **monolithic** (applied while the base concrete is still plastic), **bonded** (applied to hardened concrete), **unbonded** (separated from the base by insulation or an impervious membrane).

### Mix and batching

**Sand (moist, loose): 130 ℓ. Cement: 50 kg. Water: enough for a plastic, workable consistence.** Two barrow loads of sand to one bag of cement is a convenient batch, yielding about **115 ℓ** of compacted screed. **Never exceed the amount that can be used within 45 minutes of mixing.** Batching containers filled flush to the rim.

Quantities: 100 m² of 25 mm screed = **23 bags cement + 3,0 m³ sand**, plus a **10 % wastage** allowance.

### Thickness

| Type | Thickness |
|---|---|
| Monolithic screed | ~25 mm; not < 15 mm, not > 40 mm |
| Monolithic topping | ≥ 20 mm at any point |
| Bonded screed | Typically 25–40 mm |
| Unbonded screed | Thicker; risk of curling increases with thinness |

Above 40 mm the risk of loss of adhesion increases. Coarse aggregate for toppings: nominal 6,7 mm for 25 mm monolithic toppings; ≤ 9,5 mm for 30 mm separate bonded toppings; above 40 mm thickness, aggregate up to one quarter of the thickness, max 19 mm.

### Falls

Wet areas must fall to the outlet. A shower floor, a bathroom floor with a floor gully, and an external balcony all need a positive fall built into the screed, not into the tile bed. SANS 10252-2 requires the area around a floor drain gully to slope at a gradient of **at least 1 in 200** from all sides towards the gully. Practical falls:

| Location | Fall |
|---|---|
| Bathroom floor to gully | 1 : 100 to 1 : 60 |
| Shower floor to outlet | 1 : 60 to 1 : 40 |
| External balcony / stoep | 1 : 80 to 1 : 60, away from the building |
| Flat roof to outlet | 1 : 80 minimum; 1 : 60 preferred |

### Joints and cracking

Lay screeds in areas as large as possible in one operation to minimise joints; random drying cracks in a large panel are more acceptable than the **curling** you get at butt joints in small panels. Where joints exist in the base concrete, continue them exactly through the screed. Round joint edges to a 3 mm radius where the screed will remain exposed.

### Acceptance testing

- **Levels and surface flatness:** deviation from datum by survey; surface regularity with a 3 m straight edge supported on two rigid blocks of identical height (say 15 mm) placed 3 m apart, and feeler gauges to measure the deviation.

| Class | Max deviation under 3 m straight edge | Suitable for |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 mm | Floors requiring minimum irregularity (e.g. TV studios); needs special methods and close supervision |
| 2 | 5 mm | The major proportion of construction work |
| 3 | 10 mm | Floors where regularity is not important |

- **Departure from datum:** ± 15 mm for large areas for normal purposes; tighter in small rooms, along partition lines, at door openings and where equipment is fixed to the floor.
- **Abrupt change in level across joints:** for some floorings 2 mm is acceptable; for thin floorings, none.
- **Adhesion:** tap the surface with a rod or hammer. A hollow sound indicates lack of adhesion. Small areas may be tolerable; areas judged unsatisfactory are isolated by sawing, removed and replaced.
- **Screed strength:** the **BRE screed tester** drops a mass on a standard foot four times and the indentation depth is the strength index. Acceptance limits: **3 mm** = category A (heavy traffic, hospital operating suites and corridors, dust-free environments); **5 mm** = category C (offices, consulting rooms, domestic premises). Test one position per room in small rooms, and corridors at 3–5 m intervals, choosing positions at random but including vulnerable areas near panel joints, shrinkage cracks and doorways. The BRE tester is **not** suitable for high-strength toppings — use cubes.

### Curing

Cure screeds as you would concrete: cover with plastic sheeting over damp hessian, or pond, for at least 7 days. In Okongo conditions an uncured screed will craze, curl at the edges and debond.

## 3. Tiling

### Substrate

- Allow new **concrete to cure at least 6 weeks** and new **screeds at least 4 weeks** before tiling. Both must have a **moisture content of 5 % or less**.
- Concrete substrates must be clean and free of curing agents, laitance and contaminants — preferably scarified.
- Screeds must be firmly attached to the base, integrally sound (no crumbling or cracking), with all damaged, defective, deteriorated or hollow-sounding areas removed and made good.
- Prime before tiling or screeding. Wood-floated (rough, porous) surfaces: neat floor primer. Power-floated or steel-floated (smooth, dense) surfaces: a slurry of 1 part bonding liquid to 2 parts tile adhesive powder or 1,5 parts OPC by volume, applied by block brush.
- **Wet areas — showers, balconies — must be waterproofed before tiling.** See `08_waterproofing-and-damp.md`.

### Adhesives and grouting

- Standard-setting cementitious adhesives: **do not traffic or grout for a minimum of 24 hours**; grouting after **1–3 days**.
- Quick-setting high-strength adhesives: pot life ~4 hours, grout after 6–8 hours, traffic after 12 hours.
- Rapid-setting high-strength adhesives for porcelain: grout after ~4 hours.
- Adhesive left standing beyond its pot life must **never** be reconstituted by adding more liquid.
- Pot life varies strongly with climatic conditions — at 38 °C expect it to be substantially shorter than the label figure. Mix smaller quantities.
- Latex-modified adhesive and grout are specified where greater thermal movement is expected (e.g. under-tile heating, external balconies, sun-exposed floors).

### Joints

- **Never butt-joint tiles.** Joints let tiles move relative to each other and vent the adhesive so it can cure. Minimum joint: **5 mm between ceramic floor tiles, 3 mm between porcelain tiles.**
- **Tile panel movement joints** at maximum **3 m centres in both directions**, at least **5 mm wide**, extending through the adhesive and tile layers.
- Movement joints are also required: around the **perimeter of all floors**; in **all vertical corners**; against obstructions fixed to the structural background; over all discontinuities in building materials (e.g. the interface of concrete and brickwork); and around fixtures protruding through the tiled surface such as columns and stairs.
- **Do not tile over structural, expansion or cold joints** in the background — extend them through to the surface at their full width.
- Fill the bulk of the movement joint with a backing cord and seal with a polyurethane joint sealant, bonded only to the sides of the joint, never to the base.

> ⚠️ *"The lack of movement joints in a tile panel is a major cause of tile failure."* — TAL general specification. In a climate with a 20 °C daily swing and floor surface temperatures well above air temperature, this is the single most common cause of tented, drummy tiling.

Reference: **SANS 10107**, *Code of practice for the design and installation of ceramic tiling*.

## 4. Skimming

A skim coat is a thin (1–3 mm) finishing layer over plaster or plasterboard to give a smooth paint-ready surface.
- On plasterboard, joints are taped and filled first, then the whole surface skimmed.
- On cement plaster, skim only over a plaster that is sound, hard and reasonably true; a skim will not fix a bad plaster, it will crack along the same lines.
- Skim coats are gypsum-based. **Do not apply a gypsum skim to a substrate that will be damp in service** (below DPC, bathrooms without waterproofing, external walls) — see the gypsum/sulphate warning above.

## 5. Paint

The substrate matters more than the paint. New cement plaster is alkaline and contains free lime; painting it too early causes saponification, patchy colour and peeling.

| Step | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Drying time before painting new plaster | Commonly specified as a **minimum of 7 days**, with 28 days preferred for full alkali reduction — `needs-verification` against a manufacturer's data sheet |
| Preparation | Remove all dust, laitance, efflorescence and loose material. Wire brush and dust down |
| Primer | An **alkali-resistant plaster primer** on all new cementitious surfaces; a bonding liquid / stabilising primer on chalky or friable surfaces |
| Topcoat, external | 2 coats of a good-quality exterior acrylic (PVA/acrylic emulsion) |
| Topcoat, internal | 2 coats acrylic or acrylic-enamel |
| Metalwork | Degrease, remove mill scale/rust to a clean surface, prime with an appropriate metal primer, 2 topcoats |
| Timber, external | Prime all six faces before fixing; 2–3 topcoats; re-coat regularly under high UV |

Practical notes for this climate:
- Ultraviolet exposure in northern Namibia is severe. Buy the highest-durability exterior grade you can, and expect to repaint sooner than in a temperate climate.
- Do not paint in direct sun on a hot wall — the film skins before it flows and you get roller marks, poor adhesion and pinholing. Follow the sun around the building: paint the west wall in the morning, the east wall in the afternoon.
- Crazing and craze cracks in plaster can be covered with a reasonable-quality paint, and a glass-fibre tissue can be applied during painting where cracking is more pronounced. Structural cracks cannot be painted out — find the cause first.
- Wind-blown dust settles on wet paint. Screen the work or paint early.

## 6. Sequence of finishing trades

The order below avoids the two classic disasters — chasing a plastered wall, and getting water on a finished floor.

1. Roof clad and watertight (**before plastering starts**)
2. Windows and external door frames built in / fitted
3. First-fix electrical: conduits, boxes, chases cut and made good
4. First-fix plumbing: all pipework in walls and floors, **pressure tested**
5. Waterproofing to wet areas and any roof/balcony
6. Internal plaster, then external plaster
7. Screeds (bedrooms/living last, wet areas first so they can cure while other work continues)
8. Ceilings — brandering, boards, cornices
9. Wall and floor tiling, then grouting
10. Second-fix carpentry: door frames, doors, skirtings, architraves, cupboards
11. Painting — undercoat/primer before second-fix electrical and plumbing fittings
12. Second-fix electrical and plumbing: switches, sockets, light fittings, taps, sanitaryware, geyser connection
13. Glazing (if not earlier), mirrors, shower screens
14. Final coat of paint / touch-up
15. Floor coverings, final clean, snagging

Two rules that save the most money: **nothing gets chased after the plaster is on**, and **no wet trade works above a finished floor**.
