# Groundworks and foundations

**Summary.** A foundation does one job: spread the building's load onto ground that will not move. In the deep aeolian sands of Ohangwena the risk is rarely bearing failure — clean sand at moderate density carries a single-storey house easily — it is *collapse settlement* when loose sand is wetted for the first time, and differential movement where sand overlies calcrete at variable depth. SANS 10400-H gives deemed-to-satisfy rules for simple strip foundations under one- and two-storey buildings on non-problem soils; the moment the ground is a heaving clay, a collapsible-fabric soil, deep fill, or dolomite, the rules stop applying and a rational design by an engineer is required.

## 1. Reading the ground

### Trial holes

Dig at least **one trial hole per building corner region, minimum three per house**, to at least **1,5 m** below the proposed founding level, or to refusal. On a housing-development scale the NHBRC geotechnical guideline requires exploration to a minimum depth of **3,0 m or machine refusal**, with hole frequency set by the area of the study.

Record for each hole:
- Depth and description of each horizon (colour, grain size, consistency, moisture)
- Depth of any calcrete, hardpan, clay lens or water strike
- Whether the sides stand vertically (an indicator of apparent cohesion in slightly moist sand — which disappears on saturation)
- A hand penetration test: how easily a 10–12 mm bar or a screwdriver can be pushed in

Backfill and compact trial holes properly. An uncompacted trial hole under a future footing is a hard spot/soft spot problem you created yourself.

### NHBRC site class designations

A useful vocabulary for classifying the site even outside South Africa:

| Class | Ground | Expected total soil movement | Typical response |
|---|---|---|---|
| R | Rock | Negligible | Normal construction |
| C | Collapsible / compressible sands, silty sands | < 5 mm | Normal construction |
| C1 | Potentially collapsible ground | 5–10 mm | Modified normal construction |
| C2 | Collapsible ground | > 10 mm | Specialised design |
| S | Compressible soils — clayey silts, low-plasticity sands | < 10 mm | Normal construction |
| S1 | | 10–20 mm | Modified construction |
| S2 | | > 20 mm | Specialised foundations |
| H | Expansive (heaving) clays, silty clays, sandy clays | < 7,5 mm | Normal construction |
| H1 | | 7,5–15 mm | Modified normal construction |
| H2 | | 15–30 mm | Stiffened or cellular raft, or piles |
| H3 | | > 30 mm | Specialised construction |
| P | Problem soils — contaminated ground, dolomite, mining subsidence, deep uncontrolled fill | Variable | Specialist investigation, always |

**Northern Namibia note.** Deep Kalahari sands typically classify as **C or C1**. They are non-expansive, but loose aeolian sand held together by weak apparent cohesion can lose 5–10 % of its volume when first saturated — for example when a leaking pipe, a downpipe discharging at the wall, or the first heavy rain after occupation wets the founding zone. This is *collapse settlement* and it is the single most likely foundation failure mode on this ground. The defence is not a bigger footing; it is (a) compacting the founding zone, (b) keeping water away from it, and (c) tying the superstructure together so that any movement is shared.

### Indicative presumed bearing values

The values below are the conventional order of magnitude used for first sizing. They are **not** a substitute for a geotechnical assessment and are marked `needs-verification` against a South African primary standard.

| Material | Indicative presumed allowable bearing pressure |
|---|---|
| Sound rock | > 1 000 kPa |
| Weathered rock / hard calcrete | 300–600 kPa |
| Dense sand and gravel | 300–500 kPa |
| Medium-dense sand | 150–300 kPa |
| Loose sand | 50–150 kPa |
| Stiff clay | 150–300 kPa |
| Soft clay, silt, made ground, organic soil | < 75 kPa — do not found on it |

A single-storey masonry house with a sheeted roof imposes roughly **20–40 kN per metre run** on an external wall. On a 600 mm wide strip that is 33–67 kPa, comfortably within even loose sand — which is precisely why the failures here are settlement and collapse, not bearing capacity.

## 2. Foundation types

### Continuous strip foundation

The default for single- and double-storey masonry. Concrete strip cast in a trench, masonry foundation wall built centrally on it up to DPC level.

- Width per the Key facts table; thickness ≥ 200 mm.
- Depth: deep enough to reach undisturbed, uniform bearing material and below any zone of seasonal moisture change, root activity or erosion. On sand, a practical minimum founding depth is **600 mm below finished ground level**; on ground with roots or made ground, deeper until clean material is reached.
- Alternative widths from SABS 0161 Part 4 (quoted in the Clay Brick Guide):

| Founding material | Tile/sheeted roof, internal wall | Tile/sheeted roof, external wall | RC roof, internal wall | RC roof, external wall |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rock | 400 mm | 400 mm | 400 mm | 400 mm |
| Soil | 400 mm | 500 mm | 600 mm | 750 mm |

*Internal walls upon which reinforced concrete roofs do not bear may use 400 mm.*

### Raft / slab-on-the-ground

A stiffened slab with thickened edge beams and internal beams, cast as one element. Appropriate where:
- The ground is uniformly weak or potentially collapsible (C1/C2),
- Fill exceeds the 400 mm limit for conventional strip footings,
- You want a single pour and a fast programme.

Requirements from the Clay Brick Guide: the site is levelled; all fill complies with the compaction requirements; the base of edge beams slopes not more than 1:10; steps in slabs exceeding 400 mm require approval by a competent person; any fill under edge beams is placed under the supervision of a competent person or the beam is deepened to in-situ material; the controlled fill extends at least **1 000 mm** beyond the edge of the foundation before being battered at a slope not steeper than **1 : 2 (V:H)**.

A raft is the natural answer for a house on deep loose sand at Okongo: it is tolerant of small differential settlement, it eliminates the trench-water problem, and it is quick. It does, however, need engineering design of the beam sizes and reinforcement.

### Pad foundations

Isolated square or rectangular pads under columns — verandah posts, carports, steel-framed sheds. Size = column load ÷ allowable bearing pressure. Always tie pads together with ground beams if differential settlement would damage the structure above.

### Piles / piers

Rarely justified for small residential work. Used where competent material is deep, where collapse settlement cannot be controlled, or on class H2/H3 heaving clays. Always an engineered solution.

## 3. Excavation

> ⚠️ **Trench collapse kills more builders than anything except falls.** **[NA]** No person may work in an excavation more than **1,5 m deep** that has not been adequately shored or braced where there is a danger of collapse; safe access must be provided to an excavation more than 1,5 m deep, at intervals of not more than 50 m in a long excavation; and an excavation more than 1,5 m deep must be inspected by a competent person immediately before every shift and before work resumes after rain (GN 156/1997 reg 249). **[ZA]** Construction Regulation 13 requires a competent person appointed in writing to supervise excavation work, shoring or bracing unless the sides are sloped to at least the angle of repose or the material is stable with written permission from that competent person, safe access no further than **6 m** from any worker in the excavation, and inspection **daily prior to each shift, after every blasting operation, after an unexpected fall of ground, after damage to supports and after rain**, recorded in a register.

Practical rules for sandy ground:
- Dry loose sand has an angle of repose of roughly 30–34°. A vertical face in it is temporary at best and lethal at worst.
- Batter the sides, or bench them, or shore them. Do not "just be quick".
- Never place spoil, materials or plant within 1 m of the trench edge — the surcharge is what triggers the collapse.
- Keep trenches free of surface water. Where the bottom has dried out excessively from exposure, or softened from rain or groundwater, **re-bottom the excavation** before concreting.
- Prod the excavation bottom with a 10–12 mm diameter bar before casting. Penetration should be uniform. Where it is not, treat the soft spot: excavate and replace, or reinforce over it (see Key facts).
- Deepen locally to remove soft spots; remove hard spots (isolated boulders, calcrete pinnacles) wherever practicable, because a hard point under an otherwise uniform footing concentrates load and cracks the wall above.

## 4. Casting the foundation

1. Set level pegs in the trench bottom to the underside-of-concrete level (see file 02).
2. Cast to a minimum 200 mm thickness in one operation per bay; avoid cold joints in a strip footing by keeping the pour continuous.
3. Use **15 MPa** concrete as the practical minimum for an unreinforced single-storey strip footing, i.e. a 2-bag mix with 3½ wheelbarrows sand and 3½ wheelbarrows 19 mm stone per two 50 kg bags of 32,5 cement (Cement & Concrete SA). Where the footing is reinforced, or supports a two-storey wall, go to **25 MPa**. See `04_concrete-technology.md`.
4. Compact by rodding; do not over-vibrate a lean mix or the stone will settle out.
5. Protect and cure. In 35 °C wind, a foundation strip left uncovered loses enough water in six hours to reduce the surface strength materially. Cover with plastic sheeting or wet sacking.

### Concrete cover to reinforcement

Where reinforcement is used, cover is the difference between a 60-year foundation and a 15-year one. Commonly specified values in southern African practice:

| Condition | Nominal cover |
|---|---|
| Concrete cast directly against earth (footings, blinding-less) | 75 mm |
| Concrete cast against blinding, in contact with soil | 50 mm |
| Surface beam / ground beam, exposed to weather | 40 mm |
| Internal slab, dry conditions | 25–30 mm |

These are marked `needs-verification` against SANS 10100-2 Table 7, which sets cover as a function of exposure condition and concrete grade. Use spacers — bar chairs, purpose-made plastic spacers or concrete blocks — never stones or brick fragments, and never "lifting the mesh with a hook as we pour", which never works.

## 5. DPC and DPM

**Damp-proof course (DPC)** is the horizontal barrier in the wall. **Damp-proof membrane (DPM)** is the sheet under the floor slab. Both are cheap; both are unrepairable once built in.

Rules from SANS 10400-K clause 4.5.3:
- The DPC material must comply with SANS 248 (bituminous DPC), SANS 298 (mastic asphalt) or SANS 952-1 (polyolefin film), or hold an Agrément certificate.
- **No horizontal DPC may be installed less than 150 mm above the level of the adjacent finished ground.**
- In a solid masonry wall the DPC extends over the **full thickness** of the wall; in a cavity wall each leaf gets its own DPC over the full thickness of that leaf, and the cavity extends **150 mm below** the DPC.
- Weepholes to prevent water building up in the cavity are provided in the external leaf spaced **not more than 1 m apart**, in the course immediately below the DPC.
- Transverse joints in the DPC are lapped **≥ 150 mm**; at junctions and corners, by the full wall or leaf thickness.
- In a timber-framed wall the DPC goes between the bottom plate and the foundation wall or slab.
- **No horizontal DPC is provided in a free-standing wall** (clause 4.2.4.3) — a free-standing wall must be able to dry in both directions.

**DPM under the slab:** minimum 250 micron polyolefin sheet, laid on a sand blinding over the compacted fill, laps of at least 150 mm taped or well overlapped, turned up at the perimeter and lapped with the wall DPC to make a continuous tray. Repair every tear before the reinforcement goes down. On sandy sites, a 50 mm sand blinding over the fill protects the membrane from puncture.

> ⚠️ Plaster must **not** be carried continuously across the line of a DPC. Cut the plaster through to the substrate at the DPC line, otherwise the plaster bridges the DPC and rising damp appears above it (Cement & Concrete SA, *Successful plastering*, §6.4).

## 6. Backfill and compaction

- Backfill only with approved material: clean sand or selected granular fill. **Never** with topsoil, vegetable matter, building rubble containing gypsum plaster, or soft clay lumps.
- Place in layers not exceeding **100 mm** for hand compaction or **150 mm** for mechanical compaction, moisten each layer so a handful squeezed in the hand is firm but shows no free moisture, and compact each layer fully before the next is added.
- Acceptance test: a dynamic cone penetrometer must require **more than 3 blows** to penetrate 100 mm of the fill, provided the fill contains no more than 10 % gravel smaller than 10 mm and no isolated boulders.
- Maximum fill height beneath floor slabs and slab-on-ground foundations: **400 mm at the lowest point**, unless certified by a competent person.
- Compact both sides of a foundation wall evenly, in matched lifts, so the wall is not pushed over.
- On collapsible sand, **soak-and-compact** (inundating the founding zone and re-compacting) or over-excavate and replace with compacted fill are the standard remedies. Both need to be specified by a competent person for anything more than a small house.

## Open questions

- Presumed allowable bearing pressures are given as indicative engineering practice; they are **not** sourced from a South African primary standard and must be verified against SANS 10160-5 / a geotechnical report before design use.
- Nominal concrete cover values are indicative; SANS 10100-2 Table 7 sets cover by exposure condition and concrete grade and was not obtainable in full.
- Minimum founding depth in deep aeolian sand at Okongo is given as trade practice (600 mm); no Namibian standard specifying founding depth was located.
