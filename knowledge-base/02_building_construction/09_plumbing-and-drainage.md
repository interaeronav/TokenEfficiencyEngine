# Plumbing and drainage

**Summary.** Water supply is governed by **SANS 10252-1**, drainage by **SANS 10252-2**, and the application of the National Building Regulations to drainage by **SANS 10400-P**. For a house the decisions that matter are: pipe sizes that deliver the design flow rate at the design flow pressure at every fitting; a geyser with a temperature-and-pressure safety valve, correct vacuum breakers and a drip tray; drains laid at a gradient the hydraulic load requires (a 110 mm domestic drain at about 1:60), with rodding eyes and inspection eyes where the code demands them; and, off-grid, a septic tank of at least 1,7 m³ discharging to an absorption field sized from a percolation test.

## 1. Cold and hot water layout

### Design principle

Size pipework so that every outlet gets its **design flow rate** at its **design flow pressure**. From SANS 10252-1 Table (design flow rates and pressures):

| Fixture / fitting | Design flow rate ℓ/min | Design flow pressure kPa |
|---|---|---|
| Wash-hand basin, 15 mm taps (plain outlet) | 10 | 10 |
| Wash-hand basin, 15 mm taps (aerated) | 8 | 50 |
| Wash-hand basin, mixer (plain), hot & cold separately | 10 | 15 |
| Wash-hand basin, mixing valve (aerated) | 9 | 50 |
| Bath, 15 mm taps (plain) | 15 | 15 |
| Bath, 20 mm taps (plain) | 25 | 15 |
| Bath, 20 mm taps (aerated) | 12 | 50 |
| Bath, mixer (plain), hot & cold separately | 25 | 15 |
| Bath, mixing valve (aerated) | 20 | 50 |
| Shower, standard head (hot and cold combined) | 15 | 20–50 |
| Shower, water-saving head | 10 | 50–100 |
| WC cistern float valve | 5 | 100 |
| Urinal, wash-down type flush valve | 10 | 50 |
| Bidet mixer | 9 | 50 |
| Sink, 15 mm taps (plain) | 12 | 15 |
| Sink, 20 mm taps (plain) | 20 | 15 |
| Sink, mixer (plain) | 15 | 15 |

**Read the pressure column carefully.** An aerated basin tap needs 50 kPa (5 m head) and a water-saving showerhead needs 50–100 kPa (5–10 m head). On a gravity-fed tank system the tank must be high enough to deliver that, or the fittings must be low-pressure types. This is the single commonest mistake in off-grid Namibian houses: a beautiful aerated mixer fed from a 3 m tank stand delivers a dribble.

### Pipe materials

| Material | Typical use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Copper | Hot and cold, exposed and buried | Durable, expensive, theft target |
| Galvanised steel | Legacy, boreholes, exposed risers | Corrodes internally; avoid for new potable work |
| **PEX / PE-X** (SANS 15875 series) | Hot and cold, pushfit or crimp, concealed | Flexible, fast, no site jointing skill barrier; class 2 (70 °C) for hot water, MOP ≥ 600 kPa at 70 °C |
| **PP-R** (SANS 15874) | Hot and cold, socket-fusion welded | Robust, popular in southern Africa |
| **HDPE** (SANS 4427) | Buried mains, borehole rising mains | Long coils, few joints — ideal for remote sites |
| uPVC | Cold water only, buried | Brittle in sun; must be covered |

| Run | Nominal size |
|---|---|

| Distribution to a group of fittings | 22 mm (copper) / 25 mm (plastic) |
| Branch to a single bath or sink | 15 mm |
| Branch to a basin or WC | 15 mm |
| Geyser cold feed and hot draw-off | 22 mm |

### Pressure, tanks and boreholes

Off-grid supply at Okongo is usually a borehole pumping to an elevated tank or to a pressure vessel:

1. **Elevated tank (gravity).** Static pressure = **9,81 kPa per metre of head**. A tank whose base is 6 m above the highest outlet gives about 59 kPa static, before friction losses — enough for plain-outlet taps but marginal for aerated fittings and water-saving showerheads. To get 100 kPa you need ~10 m of head.
2. **Pressure system.** Borehole or transfer pump into a ground tank, then a pressure pump with a bladder vessel (typically set to cut in around 180–200 kPa and out around 320–350 kPa). Gives reliable pressure for modern fittings and is the practical answer for a solar-powered house.
3. **Borehole basics.** Record the borehole's yield (ℓ/h), static water level, dynamic (pumping) water level and recovery rate. Never size the pump on yield alone — size it on the sustainable yield with the pump set above the dynamic level. Fit a dry-run protection device.
4. **Water quality.** Test for total dissolved solids, chlorides, sulphates, nitrates, fluoride, iron and bacteriological quality. Northern-Namibian groundwater is often saline or brackish; where it is, either treat (reverse osmosis for drinking) or use rainwater harvest for potable and borehole for the rest. High chlorides also attack copper pipework and reinforcement in concrete.
5. **Rainwater harvesting.** Roof catchment yield ≈ roof plan area (m²) × rainfall (mm) × runoff coefficient (0,8–0,9 for steel sheeting) ÷ 1 000 = m³. A 100 m² roof at 450 mm annual rainfall yields roughly **36–40 m³ per year** — meaningful, but seasonal, so tank storage is the constraint, not catchment. Use a first-flush diverter and a screen.

> ⚠️ **Cross-connection.** Never connect a borehole or rainwater supply to a municipal supply without an approved backflow-prevention device. Mark non-potable taps and pipes clearly — **[NA]** GN 156/1997 reg 46 requires taps or pipes carrying water not fit for human consumption to be clearly and conspicuously marked.

### Geysers and hot water

SANS 10252-1 requirements:
- Recommended **storage temperature 60 °C**. Raising the thermostat to compensate for an undersized geyser increases energy loss and shortens the geyser's life.
- Closed-type storage water heaters (SANS 151) must have a **temperature and pressure safety valve** in addition to a thermostat, with the flow of hot water controlled on the outlet side.
- Thermostats to **SANS 181** including a high-temperature cut-out. *"Reliance for the protection of water heaters shall not be placed solely on thermostats."*
- Functional control and safety valves to **SANS 198**, colour-coded by working pressure rating, matched to the system's pressure rating.
- Never restrict the outlet of a safety valve.
- **Drip tray to SANS 1848** under any geyser installed in a roof space or above a ceiling, drained to outside.

**Solar water heating.** Two families:
- **Thermosiphon (close-coupled)**: tank mounted directly above the collector on the roof. No pump, no controller, very reliable. Needs the roof structure to carry the tank (a 150 ℓ tank plus collector is a substantial point load — check with the truss designer, not after the fact).
- **Split (pumped)**: collector on the roof, tank inside or at ground level, small circulation pump and differential controller. Better aesthetically and structurally; needs power for the pump.
- **Direct** systems circulate the potable water through the collector — simple, but fail in freezing or scaling water. **Indirect** systems use a glycol loop and a heat exchanger — better with hard or aggressive borehole water.
- Northern Namibia has excellent solar resource and negligible frost risk, so a well-sized thermosiphon system will meet essentially the whole domestic hot-water demand for most of the year. Provide an electric backup element controlled so it does not run during the day.

## 2. Sanitary fittings and traps

Every sanitary fixture discharges through a trap. Minimum trap diameters and water-seal depths (SANS 10252-2 Table 19):

| Trap | Fixture | System | Min diameter | Min seal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Integral | WC pan, hospital fixture | All | 100 mm | 50 mm |
| Integral | Wall-mounted urinal | All | 50 mm | 50 mm |
| Non-integral | Bidet, drinking fountain, wash-hand basin, wall-mounted urinal | Two-pipe or one-pipe | 32 mm | 40 mm |
| Non-integral | as above | Single-stack | 32 mm | **75 mm** |
| Non-integral | Bath, shower, sink, laundry trough, washing machine, dishwasher, waste disposal unit, sanitary-towel disposer | Two-pipe | 40 mm | 40 mm |
| Non-integral | as above | One-pipe | 40 mm | 65 mm |
| Non-integral | as above | Single-stack | 40 mm | **75 mm** |

Maximum seal depth in any trap is **100 mm**. Traps must have at least 8 mm clearance at all points (≤ 32 mm traps) or 10 mm (> 32 mm), be non-absorbent and corrosion-resistant, have a smooth waterway, and have access for cleaning, preferably at the lowest point.

Where a WC trap has a vent horn, that horn must be at least **40 mm** nominal diameter, at the side of and at least **75 mm** from the crown of the trap on the outlet side.

A **floor drain** is a fixture trap and is distinguished from a gully; a floor drain inside a building is usually connected to a waste branch of at least 75 mm discharging into a gully. Trapped floor drains are preferred.

## 3. Above-ground drainage

Modern domestic work is almost always a **single-stack system**: one 110 mm vertical stack takes all soil and waste, vented at the top, with the branch geometry controlling trap-seal loss.

Limiting gradients of discharge pipes (SANS 10252-2 Table 15):

| Pipe | Min gradient | Max gradient |
|---|---|---|
| Waste branch, single-stack | 1,25° (1/46) | 5° (1/11,5) |
| Waste branch, ventilated one-pipe or two-pipe | 1,25° (1/46) | 5° (1/11,5) |
| Soil branch — WC pan | 1,43° (1/40) | 14° (1/4) |
| Soil branch — other soil fixtures | 1,43° (1/40) | 5° (1/11,5) |
| Horizontal fixture discharge pipes | 2,5° (1/23) | 45° (1/1) |

Note: a waste pipe serving **only** wash-hand basins has a **maximum** gradient of 2,5° (1/23) — too steep and the water runs away from the solids, and the trap self-siphons.

Trap venting is required where the discharge from a soil fixture is conveyed by an unventilated branch drain or soil pipe with a fall exceeding 1,2 m to its connection point and within 2,4 m of the trap outlet, or by a discharge stack that receives higher-level discharges. Ventilation pipes must terminate at least **150 mm above the flood level of the highest sanitary fixture** in the installation.

## 4. Below-ground drainage

### Gradients and loading

SANS 10252-2 Table 13 gives maximum permissible drain loading in fixture units for each nominal diameter and gradient. For a **100 mm (110 mm OD)** drain:

| Gradient | Max load, fixture units |
|---|---|
| 1 in 5 | 12 000 |
| 1 in 10 | 9 000 |
| 1 in 20 | 6 400 |
| 1 in 40 | 4 500 |
| 1 in 60 | 3 650 |
| 1 in 80 | 3 150 |

For a **150 mm (160 mm OD)** drain: 40 000 at 1 in 5; 27 000 at 1 in 10; 19 000 at 1 in 20; 13 500 at 1 in 40; 11 000 at 1 in 60; 9 500 at 1 in 80.

Where the slope of the ground requires a drain to be laid steeper than **1 in 5**, the hydraulic load must not exceed the value given for a gradient of 1 in 5.

For a house, a 110 mm drain at **1 in 60 (approx. 17 mm per metre)** is the practical standard: steep enough to be self-cleansing, flat enough to keep the invert shallow. **1 in 40** is used on short runs and where cover allows.

### Access — rodding eyes and inspection eyes

A **rodding eye** gives full-bore access for cleaning; an **inspection eye** is an access opening; an **inspection chamber** and a **manhole** (depth exceeding 750 mm, big enough for a person) give greater access.

A rodding eye must be installed:
- Where a change of direction in a drain **exceeds 45°** — may be omitted where the change is made with a large-radius bend and not more than two such changes of up to 90° each occur between required rodding eyes;
- **Within 1,5 m** of the connection of the drain to a connecting sewer, common drain, or on-site sewage disposal, with an **inspection eye immediately downstream** of that point;
- At intervals such that the rodding distance between permanent access points does not exceed the values in Table 17 (values range from 12 m to 90 m depending on the type of access at each end);
- On all stub stacks in a closed drainage system, directly above the highest branch connection;
- At the highest point of any drain not connected there to a stub stack or a roddable overflow gully.

A rodding eye must join the drain **in the direction of flow at an angle of not more than 45°**, continue up to ground level, be supported where inclined, and be adequately marked and protected. Rodding eyes in the open subject to vehicle loads must terminate below ground in a protective chamber with its own load-bearing cover.

Where a drain passes under a building, access must be provided **outside** and as near as possible to the building at each point of entry and exit; access from within the building is not permitted unless approved. Where a drain passes through or under a food room, access for cleaning must be outside that room.

### Gullies and floor drains

A **gully** is a pipe fitting incorporating a trap into which waste water discharges. Rules of practice:
- Waste pipes discharge into another waste pipe or into a gully connected to a drain.
- A single bath, shower or sink may discharge independently into a gully if it is within **2 m vertically above and 3 m horizontally** of that gully.
- The gully lip must be above the surrounding paving, and the paving must fall away from the building. A gully set below the paving becomes a stormwater inlet in the first storm.
- Fit a sand trap on any inlet gully that will receive gritty water.

### Bedding, cover and backfill

- **Soil cover of at least 300 mm** over the outside of the drain.
- Bed uPVC drains on a granular bed (fine sand or 6 mm stone), surround to at least 100 mm above the crown, then backfill in compacted layers.
- Do not use the excavated Kalahari sand around the pipe if it contains calcrete nodules or building rubble — the point loads crack the pipe.
- Where the drain passes through a wall, build in a lintel or a pipe sleeve so the wall never bears on the pipe, and provide a **flexible short length** either side to accommodate settlement.
- Where cover is less than 300 mm or the drain crosses a driveway, encase in concrete or use a heavier-duty pipe class.

### Testing

**Air (soundness) test, SANS 10252-2 6.8.3:** fill all traps; plug the open ends of all ventilation pipes; fit a T-piece with cocks and connect a manometer; pump air until **0,35 kPa (35 mm water head)** registers; close the inlet cock; read the pressure after **3 minutes**. The installation passes if the pressure is still at least **0,25 kPa (25 mm water head)**. Do the test **after backfilling**, and preferably in one operation. A performance test may additionally be carried out to check that fixtures drain speedily, quietly and completely and that trap seals survive peak conditions.

Test *before* the concrete slab is cast and *again* after backfilling. Finding a cracked drain under a finished floor is the most expensive avoidable defect in a house.

## 5. Off-grid sewage — septic tanks and soakaways

### Septic tank

**Capacity.** For a single dwelling house or dwelling unit: the greater of **1,7 m³** or the expected daily sewage flow. For any other building: at least **three times** the expected daily sewage flow.

**Construction and siting**
- Impervious to liquid.
- Sited so it will not become a source of discomfort or a danger to health, and so it does not endanger the structure of any building or services.
- **No closer than twice its depth to building foundations or site boundaries.**
- Adequate access for emptying and cleaning; located so it can later be connected to a sewer reticulation if one arrives.
- Depth of receiving chamber below the outlet invert: **not less than 1,0 m and not more than 1,8 m**.
- **Air space of at least 200 mm** between the liquid surface and the underside of the top cover.
- All compartments and fittings readily accessible for inspection and cleaning.
- **Soil cover of at least 150 mm.**
- Industrial effluent must never flow into a septic tank.

**Inlet and outlet**
- Incoming drain: inside diameter **at least 100 mm**; laid at a gradient **not exceeding 1 in 60 (1,67 %)** for the last 10 m before the tank; fitted with a **T-piece** at the discharge into the receiving chamber, the vertical arms at least the diameter of the incoming drain; and **ventilated**.
- The outlet invert must be **50–75 mm below** the inlet invert.

**Watertightness test:** fill the newly built tank with water before use and let it stand for one day to detect leaks.

### Absorption field (French drain)

- Must not be provided where the effluent cannot be disposed of inoffensively or would endanger health.
- Must not pollute any public stream, spring, well or water source used or likely to be used for drinking, domestic or kitchen purposes.
- Sited **at least 3 m** from any building or from the site boundary.
- **Percolation limits:** a French drain must not be provided where the percolation rate is **less than 12 mm/h** or **more than 1 000 mm/h**, unless approved measures prevent groundwater pollution.
- **Distance from a water source:** locate uphill of a borehole or spring where possible. Where that is not possible, at least **15 m** from the water source; and if the distance between the groundwater table and the base of the French drain is less than 5 m, at least **30 m**.

**Percolation test:** at least **four test holes** spaced uniformly through the proposed field area (more where soil conditions are highly variable). Each hole approximately **150 mm diameter** and **400 mm deep**, dug vertically down from the bed of the proposed absorption field. Scarify the sides and bottom thoroughly with a sharp-pointed instrument to break up smeared surfaces before testing.

> ⚠️ **The Okongo case.** Deep clean Kalahari sand often percolates *faster than 1 000 mm/h*, which puts a conventional French drain outside SANS 10252-2's permitted range because effluent reaches the water table without being treated. Where that is the case, the answer is not a bigger soakaway; it is a treatment step — a properly sized septic tank followed by a sand filter or a packaged treatment unit, or a lined evapotranspiration bed. Always do the percolation test and always locate the field downhill of and far from the borehole.

**Conservancy tank** (a sealed holding tank with no discharge) is the fallback where an absorption field is not permissible — subject to the availability of approved clearing (honey-sucker) services, which at Okongo means confirming that a tanker will actually come.

## Open questions

- SANS 10252-2 Table 17 (maximum spacing of access points) is a matrix whose row/column pairing could not be reliably reconstructed from the plain-text scan; the range 12–90 m is correct but the specific pairing must be read from the standard.
- Indicative domestic pipe sizes (25/22/15 mm) are trade practice, not a SANS table; SANS 10252-1 requires sizing by calculation from the design flow rate and design flow pressure.
- The edition of SANS 10252-2 available in full text is the 1993 (SABS 0252-2) edition; check for a current edition before relying on it contractually.
- Namibian local-authority requirements for on-site sanitation approval in Ohangwena were not obtainable.
