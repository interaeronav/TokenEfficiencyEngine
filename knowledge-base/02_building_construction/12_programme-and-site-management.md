# Programme and site management

**Summary.** The programme for a house is not a wish list of durations; it is a chain of **technical constraints** — concrete must cure 7 days, lintels must be propped 7 days, the roof must be clad before plastering starts, a screed must cure 4 weeks before tiling, concrete 6 weeks — connected by **lead times**, which at Okongo can be longer than the activities themselves. A realistic single-storey house of about 120 m² takes **five to seven months** from site clearance to handover in this environment, and the critical path runs through the roof: trusses ordered late delay plastering, which delays everything after it.

**Total working days on the critical path: roughly 110–150**, i.e. **5–7 calendar months** allowing for weekends, public holidays and weather. Add contingency for the rainy season (roughly December to March in Ohangwena), which can stop earthworks and external plastering for days at a time.

## 2. Critical path thinking

The critical path is the longest chain of dependent activities. On a house it almost always runs:

**setting out → foundations → foundation walls → surface bed → superstructure masonry → wall plate → trusses → roof covering → internal plaster → screed → tiling → second fix → snagging → handover**

Three practical consequences:

1. **Anything not on the critical path has float.** External works, boundary walls, paving and landscaping usually have weeks of float. Use them to absorb labour when the critical path is waiting.
2. **The critical path can move.** If tiles arrive six weeks late, tiling becomes critical and the roof no longer is. Re-check after every delay.
3. **Only shorten the critical path.** Adding labour to a non-critical activity buys nothing and costs money.

**Where the critical path is most fragile on a remote site**
- **Trusses.** Design, fabrication and delivery from Ondangwa, Tsumeb or further south. If they are late, the roof is late, plastering cannot start, and the whole tail of the programme slides day-for-day.
- **The tiling cure rule.** A screed laid on day X cannot be tiled until day X+28. If the screed slips, tiling slips, second-fix slips.
- **The electrician.** A single visiting electrician doing first fix late holds up plastering; doing second fix late holds up handover and the CoC.
- **Water.** No water means no concrete, no mortar, no plaster and no curing.

## 3. Lead times

Build the procurement schedule **backwards** from the programme. Indicative lead times for a remote northern-Namibian site (verify with your own suppliers; these are planning figures, not sourced):

| Item | Indicative lead time | Order by |
|---|---|---|
| Cement, sand, stone, common brick | 3–10 days | Rolling |
| Reinforcement, brickforce, ties | 1–2 weeks | Before foundations |
| DPC, DPM, termite chemicals | 1–2 weeks | Before surface bed |
| Windows and door frames (standard) | 2–4 weeks | At superstructure start |
| Windows (custom / aluminium) | 6–10 weeks | At foundation stage |
| **Roof trusses (designed and fabricated)** | **4–8 weeks** | **At foundation stage** |
| Roof sheeting (standard profile/colour) | 2–4 weeks | At wall plate stage |
| Roof sheeting (custom length/colour) | 4–8 weeks | At superstructure start |
| Concrete roof tiles | 2–5 weeks | At superstructure start |
| Geyser, solar water heater | 2–5 weeks | Before second fix |
| Sanitaryware, taps | 2–6 weeks | Before second fix |
| Floor and wall tiles (specific range) | 3–8 weeks | Before screeds |
| Kitchen cupboards, built-in furniture | 4–8 weeks | At plastering |
| Electrical DB, cable, fittings | 2–4 weeks | Before first fix |
| PV panels, inverter, batteries | 4–12 weeks | At design stage |
| Ironmongery, specialist items | 4–8 weeks | At plastering |

**Rules for a long supply line**
1. Order the long-lead items **first**, before the short-lead ones, even though they are needed later.
2. Confirm **stock availability at the time of ordering**, not at the time of quoting. A quotation is not a reservation.
3. Consolidate deliveries. One 14 t load costs far less per tonne than three 5 t loads over 700 km.
4. Build a **buffer stock** of the consumables you cannot run without: cement, sand, timber, fixings, fuel. But respect cement shelf life — 4–6 weeks maximum.
5. Substitute early, not late. If the specified tile has a 10-week lead time, decide in week 1, not in week 9.
6. Keep the client's selections (tiles, sanitaryware, ironmongery, paint colours) on a **decision schedule with dates**, and treat a missed decision date as a delay event with a written notice.

## 4. Site records

Records are what turn a dispute into a fact. Keep all of these:

**Daily site diary** — the single most important document. Every working day, one page:
- Date, weather (including temperature and wind — relevant to concrete and plaster), and any weather stoppage with hours lost
- Labour on site by trade and number
- Plant on site and whether working or standing
- Work carried out, by area
- Deliveries received (with delivery note numbers)
- Instructions received, from whom, verbal or written
- Visitors: client, engineer, inspector, supplier
- Delays, their cause and their duration
- Safety: toolbox talk topic, incidents, near misses
- Photographs taken

**Other registers**
- Delivery notes and materials-received register
- Concrete register: date, element, mix, slump, cube numbers, curing method and duration
- Inspection register: foundation bottoms, reinforcement before pour, DPM before slab, drainage air tests, roof bracing, waterproofing before covering
- Test certificates: cube results, drain air test, electrical test report and CoC, water pressure test
- Scaffold inspection register (weekly, and after turbulent weather — see file 14)
- Excavation inspection register (daily before each shift, and after rain)
- Site instruction book (numbered, in duplicate)
- Variation register with cost and time effect
- Subcontractor payment register
- **[ZA]** The **health and safety file** required by the Construction Regulations 2014
- Photographic record: every trench before concrete, every wall before plaster, every roof before ceiling, every service before it is buried

**Photograph discipline:** date, location and a scale object in the frame. Photograph *everything that is about to be covered up*. It costs nothing and it settles arguments years later.

## 5. Managing subcontractors

On a house, the visiting trades are usually electrician, plumber, roof erector, tiler, ceiling contractor, painter and glazier.

**Before they arrive**
- A written order stating scope, specification, quantity, rate, programme dates, payment terms, retention, and who supplies materials.
- Confirm they have their own tools, transport, PPE and (for the electrician) registration.
- Confirm the preceding trade is genuinely finished. Bringing a tiler onto a screed that is three weeks old wastes a trip that may cost more than the tiling.

**While they are on site**
- Set out and mark for them — heights, levels, setting-out lines. A subcontractor who sets out his own work will set it out wrong.
- Inspect the **first area** of each trade before letting them continue. Correcting one bathroom is cheap; correcting five is not.
- Record their attendance in the diary.
- Feed them: on a remote site, accommodation, water and food are your problem or they will leave.

**Payment**
- Pay on measured work done, not on time on site.
- Never pay 100 % before the snag list is closed.
- Hold the agreed retention and release it on the agreed date, in writing.

**The recurring failure mode on remote sites** is the subcontractor who comes for two days, does 60 % of the work, leaves for a job in town and never comes back. Defences: a scope small enough to complete in one visit, payment only on completion of that scope, and a second contact for every trade.

## 6. Snagging and handover

**Snagging** is the systematic inspection against the specification, not a search for perfection.

Method:
1. Inspect **room by room, surface by surface**, in the same order every time: floor, walls (clockwise from the door), ceiling, doors and windows, fittings, services.
2. Then the externals: walls, roof from ground and from a ladder, gutters, downpipes, aprons, paving, stormwater, fences and gates.
3. Then the systems: run every tap, flush every WC, fill and drain every bath, switch every light and socket, open and close every door and window, test the geyser, check the DB labelling.
4. Record each item with a number, a location, a description and a photograph. Issue as a numbered list.
5. Re-inspect and sign off item by item. Do not close an item on a promise.

**Handover pack** — hand over as a bound file and a digital copy:
- As-built drawings, including the actual routes of drains, water pipes and cables
- **[ZA]** Electrical Certificate of Compliance and test report
- Plumbing certificate / test records where applicable
- Waterproofing guarantee and applicator certificate
- Roof truss certificate from the fabricator / ITC-SA
- Concrete cube test results
- Manufacturers' warranties, operating and maintenance instructions: geyser, solar system, PV, pumps, appliances
- Paint schedule and colour codes for future touch-up
- Tile, sanitaryware and ironmongery schedules with supplier references
- Termite treatment certificate
- **[ZA]** NHBRC enrolment documentation
- Keys, labelled, with a key schedule
- The defects liability period start date and the process for reporting defects

**After handover**
- **[ZA]** The builder carries a **3-month maintenance/snag obligation**; roof leaks must be reported within **1 year** and major structural defects within **5 years** of first occupation.
- Diarise a **visit at 3 months and at 11 months**. Finding and fixing a small defect yourself before the client finds it is the cheapest marketing there is, and it protects the retention release.

## 7. Weather and heat planning at Okongo

- **Rainy season roughly December–March.** Plan earthworks, foundations and external plaster outside it where possible. Programme the roof to be on before the rains.
- **Heat.** Working in sustained 35–40 °C reduces output, increases error rates and creates a real heat-illness risk. Practical response: start at first light, take the middle of the day off, work again late afternoon; provide shade, cool drinking water and rest breaks; do concrete pours and plastering early morning or late afternoon.
- **Wind.** Steady wind drives plastic shrinkage in concrete and plaster, blows dust into wet paint, and makes roof sheeting handling dangerous. Programme roof-sheeting work for calm conditions and stop when it is not safe — **[ZA]** Construction Regulation 10 explicitly prohibits roof work during inclement weather or when wind makes it unsafe.
- **Dust.** Everything cementitious, everything painted and every electrical enclosure suffers. Sequence so that dusty operations (chasing, cutting, grinding) finish before finishing trades start.
