# Quality, defects and permissible deviations

**Summary.** Most building defects have one of four root causes: **water in the wrong place**, **movement that was not allowed for**, **too much water in the mix**, or **the wrong thing done in the wrong order**. This file is a diagnostic catalogue — appearance, cause, diagnosis, remedy, prevention — followed by a consolidated table of permissible deviations drawn from the standards where they exist, and clearly flagged as trade practice where they do not. A note worth carrying: *"Neither the SABS specifications nor the National Building Regulations have measurable requirements covering hardened plaster quality"* (Cement & Concrete SA) — which is exactly why the acceptance criteria must be written into the specification before the work starts.

## 1. Defect catalogue — concrete

### Plastic shrinkage cracking
**Appearance.** Short, random, roughly parallel cracks appearing on a slab surface within the first few hours, before the concrete has hardened.
**Cause.** Surface water evaporating faster than bleed water rises — high air and concrete temperature, low humidity, wind, absorptive subgrade or formwork.
**Diagnosis.** Timing is the giveaway: they appear while the concrete is still plastic or within hours. They rarely penetrate the full depth.
**Remedy.** Shallow cracks: fill and seal, or overlay. Deep cracks: cut out and repair.
**Prevention.** Wind breaks, shade, cool mixing water, damp the subgrade and formwork, fog-spray between finishing operations, and start curing immediately. This is the number-one concrete defect at Okongo.

### Plastic settlement cracking
**Appearance.** Cracks following the line of top reinforcement, or at abrupt changes of section.
**Cause.** Fresh concrete settling around restraint before it sets.
**Diagnosis.** The crack pattern maps the reinforcement.
**Remedy.** Re-vibrate and close while still plastic; otherwise fill.
**Prevention.** Lower slump, adequate cover, good compaction, revibration before initial set.

### Crazing
**Appearance.** A fine hexagonal network in the surface skin only.
**Cause.** Over-trowelling a rich mix; rapid surface drying.
**Diagnosis.** Very fine and shallow; does not open and close.
**Remedy.** Cosmetic — a coating covers it.
**Prevention.** Do not over-trowel; cure early.

### Honeycombing
**Appearance.** Voids where mortar is partially or completely absent.
**Cause.** *"The use of concrete which contains too much stone, or... segregation of the stone and mortar"*, plus poor compaction.
**Diagnosis.** Visible on stripping formwork; sounds hollow; loose aggregate can be picked out.
**Remedy.** Remove **all** weak, soft or honeycombed material to expose hard sound concrete; avoid feather edges by saw-cutting the outline **at least 10 mm deep**; install mechanical anchoring devices if needed; remove all debris and dust; check and control surface absorptiveness; prime the substrate with a slurry immediately before placing the repair; patch with a polymer-modified plastic mixture (SBR or acrylic emulsion based) or, in confined cavities, a semi-dry mixture applied by a trained operator.
**Prevention.** Correct mix, placing in layers, no free-fall over ~1,5 m, proper vibration.

### Blowholes
**Appearance.** Small, roughly spherical voids in a formed face.
**Cause.** Inadequate compaction of the thin layer of concrete in contact with the formwork.
**Remedy.** Fill with a proprietary blowhole filler.
**Prevention.** Vibrate against the form; correct release agent, thinly applied.

### Misplaced surfaces
**Cause.** Formwork incorrectly aligned or positioned, movement of formwork during placing, or under/overfilling.
**Remedy.** Cut back and patch, or grind.
**Prevention.** Brace and check formwork before and during the pour.

### Cold joints
**Appearance.** A visible plane of weakness where fresh concrete met partly set concrete.
**Cause.** Delay between batches exceeding the setting time — accelerated dramatically by heat.
**Remedy.** Cannot be made invisible. Where unavoidable, form a proper construction joint with a stop end and a shear key.
**Prevention.** Plan the pour; use a retarder in hot weather; never let a face stand more than ~30 minutes at 38 °C.

### Dusting / weak floor surface
**Cause.** Bleed water trowelled back into the surface; concrete allowed to dry out; over-trowelling; too little cement.
**Remedy.** Surface hardener, or remove and re-top.
**Prevention.** Do not trowel bleed water; cure 7 days.

## 2. Defect catalogue — plaster

Cement & Concrete SA groups plaster defects as: non-structural cracks, structural cracks, debonding, lack of hardness, grinning, expansion and popping.

### Crazing (plaster)
Network of fine cracks in a hexagonal pattern, **5–75 mm** across each hexagon, very fine and shallow, not through the full depth. Caused by over-trowelling a rich mix, or a sand containing excessive dust (**more than 15 % by mass passing 0,075 mm**). Often occurs within hours and is barely visible until dust or moisture reveals it. Of little importance; covered with a reasonable-quality paint, with a glass-fibre tissue applied during painting if necessary.

### Map cracking
Similar to crazing but deeper (sometimes through the plaster) with hexagons up to **200 mm** across. Occurs with a high-cement mix or where the plaster dries too quickly. Causes of excessive early moisture loss: evaporation where the wall is not protected from sun and wind; suction into absorbent masonry that was not dampened; badly graded sand lacking fine material (**less than 5 % by mass passing 0,075 mm**); not using building lime or masonry cement where the sand lacks fines.

### Plastic shrinkage cracking (plaster)
Cracking from excessive water loss in the first hours after application. Map cracking can be due to plastic shrinkage, as can the horizontal cracks that form at corners and between windows. Often floated closed while plastic, only to reappear later. Fill with a proprietary filler and paint over; glass-fibre tissue can be applied during painting.

### Drying shrinkage cracking
Moisture loss after the plaster has hardened. *"Plaster will always shrink and crack so it is desirable that it should develop a large number of fine, unnoticeable cracks at close spacings."* Plasters with very high cement contents, and those made with poor-quality high-water-demand sands, develop a few widely spaced cracks instead. Plaster applied in layers that are too thick cracks the same way. These cracks are normally stable — fill and paint.

### Structural cracks
Cracks in the plaster that originate in the wall: differential foundation movement, moisture expansion or drying shrinkage of the masonry units, or thermal movement of the roof. **Diagnosis:** straight vertical or horizontal lines, or stepped diagonals; the crack width varies with the seasons. *"Because these cracks originate in the wall and not in the plaster, repairing the plaster is ineffective."* Call in a specialist to establish the cause. Remedies may include structural alterations that convert cracks into movement joints; visible joints can be hidden by cover strips fixed on one side, or sealed with an elastomeric sealant.

### Debonding
Hollow sound when tapped. Plaster curls and debonds because the outside skin shrinks at a different rate from the plaster in contact with the wall — especially with excessively thick plaster. Small areas (about the size of a plate) are not significant; larger areas must be removed and replaced. Prevention: clean dusty or oily wall surfaces thoroughly; allow the wall to reach the correct moisture content; use a cement slurry or spatterdash coat; use bonding liquids per the manufacturer's procedure.

### Lack of hardness
Five common causes: insufficient cement; sand with excessive dust (>15 % passing 0,075 mm); a mix with poor water retention; **retempering** (adding extra water some time after first mixing); and rapid drying from plastering in full sun or wind. Only "insufficient cement" can be confirmed by laboratory testing of hardened plaster, and only if representative sand and cement samples are available. Unpainted plaster can be treated with proprietary hardeners; painted plaster can only be removed and replaced. A good exterior paint normally reduces water penetration to acceptable levels provided the plaster is strong enough to hold the paint. Corners and impact-prone areas can be replastered with relatively little disruption.

*"It is often better to have a slightly weaker plaster that is less likely to show significant cracking or debonding than one which is too strong."*

### Grinning
The mortar joints show through the plaster. Caused by the difference in suction between masonry units and mortar; raking out mortar joints also causes it, *"and the practice should thus be limited to soft clay brickwork."* Unsightly but not progressive. Prevention: an undercoat or a spatterdash coat before plastering.

### Expansion / sulphate attack
Swelling, softening, layer cracking and spalling. *"Usually caused by the inclusion of proprietary gypsum-based products in the mix."* Under moist conditions the sulphate from the gypsum reacts with the portland cement paste to form compounds of increased volume which disrupt the plaster. **The only remedy is to remove and replace the plaster.**

### Popping / pop-outs
Conical fragments breaking out, leaving holes. Caused by contaminant particles in the mix — seeds, other organic material, or particles of dead-burnt lime — which react with moisture and expand. Remove the cause, fill and paint.

## 3. Defect catalogue — masonry

| Defect | Diagnosis | Cause | Remedy | Prevention |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Stepped diagonal cracking from a corner of an opening** | Follows the mortar joints diagonally | Foundation settlement, or lack of a lintel / inadequate bearing | Establish the cause first; underpin or stabilise if foundation movement; then repair | Correct foundation design, lintels with 150 mm bearing, control joints |
| **Vertical crack near a corner or at a change of height** | Straight, full height, wider at top or bottom | Missing control joint; differential movement between clay and concrete units | Cut a control joint at the crack, seal with backing rod and elastomeric sealant | Control joints per SANS 10400-K Table 19; do not mix clay and concrete units in one wall |
| **Horizontal crack under the roof/wall plate** | Along one bed joint near the top | Thermal movement of the roof pushing the wall; missing horizontal control joint | Cut a horizontal joint and seal; check roof fixings | 10 mm horizontal control joint at the top of clay masonry in framed buildings; slip layer under the wall plate |
| **Efflorescence** | White salt bloom on the face | Soluble salts carried to the surface by evaporating water | Brush off dry; treat the moisture source | Keep bricks dry in stacks, avoid saturating walls, use CEM II B-V/W plaster below DPC, keep DPC unbridged |
| **Bulging / bowing wall** | Out of plumb, often with cracking | Overloading fresh work, laying too many courses in a day, missing ties, wet mortar | Structural assessment; usually rebuild | Limit daily lift to ~1,2–1,5 m; correct ties |
| **Wall out of plumb** | Fails plumb check | Poor corner control, no gauge rod, working to the previous day's error | Rebuild if beyond tolerance; do not "make it up in the plaster" | Build corners first, plumb and gauge every course |
| **Mortar too strong** | Wall cracks through the units instead of the joints | 1:3 or richer mortar used instead of Class II | Cannot be reversed | Use Class II (50 kg : 200 ℓ) for all normal brickwork |
| **Frogs laid down** | Hollow when tapped; low compressive strength | Bricks laid frog-down | Rebuild | Frog up on structural walls (and remember it adds 15 % mortar) |

## 6. Quality-control routine

Inspect at the points where the work is about to be covered up. Each of these is a hold point:

1. **Foundation bottom** — prod-test, uniform penetration, no soft spots, correct depth, re-bottomed if dried out or softened. *Photograph.*
2. **Reinforcement before concrete** — bar sizes, spacing, laps, cover spacers.
3. **DPM before concrete** — laps, tears repaired, turn-ups, bonded to DPC. *Photograph.*
4. **Underslab drainage** — air test passed and recorded, before the slab.
5. **Concrete** — slump measured, cubes taken and labelled, curing started, curing method recorded.
6. **DPC course** — 150 mm above finished ground, full thickness, laps ≥ 150 mm, not bridged.
7. **Masonry** — plumb, gauge, brickforce, ties, control joints, lintel bearings, roof anchors built in and long enough. *Photograph anchors before the next course.*
8. **Roof structure** — truss positions, bracing complete and as drawn, anchors fixed with every nail hole filled.
9. **Roof covering** — laps, screw pattern, flashings, no swarf.
10. **Before plaster** — all conduits, boxes and pipes fixed and tested; chases made good; substrate roughness and absorption checked; DPC line marked.
11. **Waterproofing** — before covering: standing water test, upstands, terminations. *Photograph.*
12. **Before tiling** — substrate age and moisture, primer applied, falls correct, movement joints set out.
13. **Before painting** — substrate dry, clean, prepared, primed.
14. **Before handover** — full snag inspection, all certificates in hand.

## Open questions

- Masonry verticality, level, straightness and joint-thickness tolerances are trade practice; **SANS 2001-CM1:2012** contains the permissible deviations for masonry walling and could not be obtained in full. All values marked **(T)** in §5 need verification against it.
- Tile lippage and tiled-surface flatness tolerances are trade practice; SANS 10107 could not be obtained in full.
- Setting-out tolerances are trade practice; no South African standard specifying them was located.
- Cement & Concrete SA notes that neither the SABS specifications nor the National Building Regulations set measurable requirements for hardened plaster quality — so acceptance criteria for plaster hardness must be agreed in the specification before work starts.
