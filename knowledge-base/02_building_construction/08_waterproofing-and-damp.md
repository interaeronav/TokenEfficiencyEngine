# Waterproofing and damp

**Summary.** **SANS 10021** is the South African code for the waterproofing of buildings, including damp-proofing and vapour barriers. Damp arrives by four routes — rising, penetrating, condensation and leakage — and each has a different diagnosis and a different cure. The details that matter are cheap and irreversible: DPC 150 mm above finished ground and not bridged by plaster or soil, a DPM under the slab lapped to the DPC, waterproofing dressed **at least 150 mm up every vertical surface and 100 mm down into gutters and outlets**, and a **72-hour standing water test** on every flat roof and planter before it is covered.

## 1. The four sources of damp

### Rising damp

Moisture rises by capillarity through the fine pores of masonry and soil. *"Even if a site appears to be dry, water is always present to some extent, and the water table varies constantly... water-soluble salts rise with the moisture into unprotected materials and are deposited in these materials, causing chemical reactions and decomposition. Rising damp can occur in any wall and usually leaves a tell-tale tide mark on internal decorations. It rarely reaches more than a metre above floor level."*

**Diagnosis:** a horizontal tide mark up to about 1 m; salts (efflorescence) at the tide line; damp is worst at floor level and diminishes upward; present year-round rather than after rain; paint blistering and plaster crumbling in the lower band.

**Causes:** no DPC; a bridged DPC (plaster carried across it, soil or paving heaped above it, a flower bed against the wall, a screed lapping over it); a DPC placed less than 150 mm above finished ground; a DPM not lapped to the DPC.

**Cure:** find the bridge first — 8 times out of 10 that is the whole problem. Lower the ground, remove the render bridge, cut the plaster through at the DPC line, take the paving away from the wall. Where there is genuinely no DPC, remedial options are a physical DPC inserted by cutting the wall in short lengths, or an injected chemical DPC. Both are specialist work.

### Penetrating damp

*"Penetrating damp comes in sideways through walls and is dependent on the exposure given to the building components and on both the inherent and developed absorption and permeability characteristics of the building material(s)."*

**Diagnosis:** patches related to the direction of prevailing wind or driving rain, appearing after rain and drying between events; efflorescence and localised staining. It can also occur *"where a perfectly good damp-proof course is bridged by soil or other materials above the DPC level, or where the ground level falls towards a wall and storm water can dam up against the wall and rise above the DPC."*

Note the compounding effect: penetrating damp raises the thermal transmittance of the wall and adds moisture to the internal air, so penetrating damp and condensation frequently occur together.

**Cure:** in order — fix the source (cracked plaster, failed pointing, defective sill, blocked gutter, missing flashing, ponding at the base of the wall), then improve the wall's resistance (a good exterior paint system, or a breathable water repellent), and only then consider a cavity or a rain screen.

At Okongo, driving rain arrives in short intense summer storms on a strong wind. The vulnerable faces are north and east. The commonest builder-caused penetrating damp here is a cracked or crazed external plaster with no protective coating, and a downpipe discharging at the wall.

### Condensation

*"Condensation problems are associated with poor thermal insulation, ventilation and heating."*

- **Surface condensation** — permanent (humid internal air meets a permanently cold surface; only fixable by changing the conditions) or intermittent (a warm humid spell after cold weather, which re-evaporates).
- **Interstitial condensation** — occurs *inside* an element where the temperature falls below the dew point within it. *"If such interstitial condensation occurs, remedial work is difficult."*

**Prevention:** a **vapour barrier on the warm side of the insulation**. SANS 10021 is explicit: if the barrier is on the cold side it will not prevent vapour reaching the dew-point plane; it merely stops the moisture evaporating outwards. Seal the joints of the barrier, particularly where it is integral with the insulation.

In this climate condensation is usually a minor issue in the dry season and can matter in the humid summer months, especially in air-conditioned rooms where a cold interior surface meets humid outside air — the reverse of the temperate-climate case. Put the vapour barrier on the warm (outside) side of insulation in a continuously air-conditioned building, and ventilate the roof void.

### Leakage

Water arriving through a defect — a failed roof lap, an unsealed penetration, a cracked flat-roof membrane, a burst pipe. Distinguished from the other three by being localised, sudden and often traceable to a single point. Always look **uphill** of the stain: water runs along purlins, battens and slab soffits before it drops.

## 2. DPC and DPM detailing

The DPC/DPM system is one continuous tray. Detail rules:

- DPC materials must comply with SANS 248, SANS 298 or SANS 952-1, or hold an Agrément certificate.
- Minimum **150 mm above adjacent finished ground level**; solid wall — full thickness; cavity wall — each leaf gets its own DPC over the full thickness of that leaf, with the cavity extending 150 mm below and weepholes at ≤ 1 m centres in the course below.
- Transverse laps ≥ 150 mm; corners and junctions lapped by the full wall thickness.
- **Lap and bond the horizontal DPM under the slab to the wall DPC** before bedding anything on the brickwork. This is the joint that fails most often, because it is made by two different trades a week apart.
- **No horizontal DPC in a free-standing wall** (SANS 10400-K 4.2.4.3) — such a wall must dry both ways.
- Plaster must be **cut through to the substrate at the DPC line**.
- Under a window: DPC bedded in cement-sand mortar on a course of bricks not less than 150 mm above ground level, then the subsill bedded on top of the DPC.
- Under copings: a DPC directly under the coping. Copings profiled with a fall towards the roof (or both ways), drip grooves under the projecting edges, as long as practical to reduce joints, with expansion joints filled with a UV-stable non-hardening sealant.
- Sills: fall ≥ 3° on the underside, drip groove with its back edge ≥ 15 mm clear of the finished wall face, and side stoolings to throw water forward rather than into the reveals.

**DPM under the surface bed.** Fill well compacted, with a sand blinding of at least 20 mm (and ideally 50 mm above and below the membrane to prevent puncture). The membrane is dressed into the footing trenches and up the sides of the excavation, lapped and bonded to the vertical membrane behind a protective brick skin to **at least 2 courses above finished ground level**. Inspect and repair the membrane **immediately before the concrete is placed** — SANS 10021 calls this "simple additional supervisory precaution... recommended in order to prevent costly repairs at a later stage."

Where the ground water level is or can be high, the horizontal waterproof membrane should be placed **above the maximum level to which the water is likely to rise**, unless an effective tanking membrane is provided.

**Sulfate warning:** ground water or hardcore containing sulfates attacks concrete floors. Where moisture moves upward by capillarity, soluble salts concentrate under or in the slab; the reaction with portland cement can cause disintegration and expansion, pushing foundation walls outward or bowing the slab. Do not use unknown demolition rubble as fill.

## 3. Roof and wet-area waterproofing systems

### System types (SANS 10021 clause 9.3.3)

| System | Description | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Bituminous felt / torch-on** | Bitumen membranes (SANS 248), laid in layers with hot or cold adhesive or heat-welded | The workhorse of SA flat-roof waterproofing. End joints lapped **≥ 150 mm** and sealed. Felt cut into lengths ≤ 7 m and laid flat in the open to expand before laying; scrap lengths under 3 m not used |
| **Plastomeric membranes** (PVC, polyisobutylene, CSPE) | Light, single layer, loose-laid or fully bonded | High skill needed. Smooth surface aids inspection; normally used without surface protection; repairs by heat or solvent welding |
| **Elastomeric membranes** (butyl to SANS 187, chloroprene to SANS 580, EPDM) | Sheet rubber systems | Sealing overlaps with specific adhesives demands high workmanship |
| **Reinforced liquid-applied** | Acrylic or modified-acrylic binders reinforced with non-woven polyester or polypropylene fabric | *"Perform well as waterproofing membranes and show good durability."* Fully bonded; require detailed specification and strict supervision |
| **Unreinforced liquid-applied** | Modified bitumen emulsions, tar-modified polyurethane, spray-applied | *"Monitoring the thickness of application... is difficult."* Weakest control |
| **Self-adhering rubberised asphalt** | Single pliable layer with a polyethylene outer, ~1,5 mm total | Fully bonded, reasonably easy to apply, but needs an activating primer containing volatile flammable solvent — ventilate enclosed spaces |
| **Mastic asphalt** | SANS 298 / SANS 297 | Basement floors: 3 layers ≥ 30 mm total (or 2 coats ≥ 20 mm). Basement walls: 3 layers ≥ 20 mm. Fillets at all internal angles in two coats |

> ⚠️ *"Any coating applied to concrete will improve its watertightness, but no coating can prevent leaks if the concrete cracks afterwards."* A liquid membrane over a moving crack will tear. Detail the movement, then waterproof.

### Torch-on vs liquid — how to choose

| Consideration | Torch-on bituminous | Reinforced liquid-applied |
|---|---|---|
| Substrate tolerance | Needs a sound, dry, primed, even substrate | Tolerates complex geometry, penetrations, upstands |
| Detailing around penetrations | Awkward; requires careful cutting and boots | Straightforward — fabric is dressed into the shape |
| Skill required | Moderate; but flame risk | Moderate; thickness control is the weak point |
| Fire risk during application | **High** — open flame on a building | None |
| Repairability | Patch by torching a new piece over | Re-coat locally |
| UV | Needs mineral chip surfacing or a reflective coating | Usually UV-stable acrylic, but re-coat periodically |
| Suitability at Okongo | Good on a concrete flat roof; watch the flame near timber, thatch and dry grass | Good for parapet junctions, gutters, small complex areas |

Practically: use a torch-on system for large flat concrete areas and a reinforced liquid system for the details, junctions and small areas — but **never mix incompatible chemistries without checking with both manufacturers**.

### Protection of the membrane

*"Nearly all waterproofing needs protection against the destructive effects of the ultraviolet rays in sunlight."* Options: tiles, slates, precast concrete slabs, clay pavers or concrete blocks (light units bedded in bitumen or mortar, heavy units laid loose); or, where there will be no regular traffic, reflective aluminium-based bitumen paint, or a covering of coarse sand or crushed stone. Precast concrete units with feet raising them **20–40 mm** above the roof create an insulating air space and a drainage path. Provide soft joints between paving and perimeter walls or other protrusions.

In the Ohangwena UV environment this is not optional — an unprotected black membrane at 70 °C surface temperature will embrittle within a few seasons.

### Falls and drainage

- Waterproofing dressed **≥ 150 mm up** every vertical surface and **≥ 100 mm down** into gutters and downpipes.
- Gutters laid to a fall of **at least 1 in 500**; deflection under load causes ponding and silt.
- Roof outlets need a flange of **at least 100 mm** for the waterproofing to bond to.
- Downpipe inlets can act as a hydraulic control and cause spillage at the head of the gutter — provide a receiver box or tapered head. A conical head should be at least **1,5 × the downpipe diameter** across the top, with a tapered depth of at least **0,75 × the downpipe diameter**.
- Provide **overflow gargoyles** wherever possible for flash storms. Ohangwena rainfall arrives as high-intensity short-duration events; the critical rainstorm duration for conventional buildings is normally taken as **5 minutes**.
- External downpipes are preferred to cast-in or built-in ones, for maintenance, replacement, cleaning, damage and overflow reasons.
- Hail guards serve as hail protection, walkways, outlet protection and debris protection.

### Wet areas

Showers, bathrooms and balconies must be waterproofed **before tiling**, with:
- A coved fillet at every floor-to-wall junction.
- The membrane dressed up the shower walls to the full height of the tiling and at least 150 mm above the highest point of any water source; over the full floor of the wet area and 300 mm past the threshold.
- The membrane bonded to the outlet flange.
- Falls built into the screed, not the tile bed (see `07_finishes-plaster-screed-paint.md`).
- A flood test before tiling.

### The standing water test

*"It is prudent to insist on a standing water test on all waterproofed flat roofs and planters. The duration of such a test should be at least 72 h. This test should be repeated when the waterproofing has been covered with stone or paving. Only if no unexplained drop in water level occurs and no leaks are visible can it be concluded that the roof is waterproof."* Ensure the manufacturer is involved for the duration of the test.

Mark the water level, record it, photograph it, and record it again at 72 hours. On a remote site this consumes water — plan it.

## 4. Warranties and guarantees

Waterproofing is the trade with the widest gap between what is promised and what is enforceable. Practical rules:

1. **Specify by system, not by product.** "Three-layer torch-on with mineral chip cap sheet, primer, fillets and 150 mm upstands, applied by an applicator approved by the membrane manufacturer" is enforceable. "Waterproof the roof" is not.
2. **Insist on a manufacturer-backed guarantee**, issued by the material manufacturer on the strength of an approved applicator's certificate, not a guarantee issued only by the applicator. A one-man applicator's 10-year guarantee is worth exactly as long as the one man stays in business.
3. **Record the inspections.** Photograph the substrate, primer, each layer, all upstands and terminations, and the standing water test.
4. **Common exclusions to read for:** ponding water, movement/cracking of the substrate, mechanical damage, later penetrations by other trades (satellite dishes, PV mounting, air-conditioner brackets), lack of maintenance, and failure to keep outlets clear. In practice most guarantee claims are refused on one of these.
5. **Maintenance is part of the deal.** Clear gutters and outlets before each rainy season; inspect upstands, terminations and laps annually.

> ⚠️ Solar PV mounting on a waterproofed flat roof after the guarantee is issued voids most guarantees. Coordinate PV, satellite, aerial and air-conditioning fixings **before** the membrane goes down.

## 5. Site rules that prevent most damp problems

1. Slope the ground **away** from the building for about 3 m, and pave it if you can.
2. Never let soil, paving, a flower bed or a screed rise above the DPC.
3. Never let plaster or render bridge the DPC.
4. Lap the DPM to the DPC and inspect it immediately before the concrete goes in.
5. Discharge every downpipe at least 1,5–2 m from the wall, onto a hard surface or into a pipe.
6. Detail every abutment with a proper flashing and a DPC at least 150 mm above the roof.
7. Dress every membrane 150 mm up and 100 mm down.
8. Test flat roofs for 72 hours before covering them.
9. Ventilate roof voids and subfloor voids; air bricks at least 150 mm above finished ground.
10. Protect every membrane from UV.
