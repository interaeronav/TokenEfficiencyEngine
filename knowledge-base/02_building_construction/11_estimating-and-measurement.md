# Estimating and measurement

**Summary.** Estimating is two separate skills: **measuring** the work correctly (a discipline with published rules — the ASAQS *Standard System of Measuring Building Work*), and **pricing** it correctly (labour, material, plant, preliminaries, overhead, profit, risk). The measurement rules matter because they define what is and is not included in a rate: in the ASAQS system all work is measured **net as fixed in position**, with **no allowance for waste, passings or laps** — so waste lives entirely in the rate, not in the quantity. Get that principle wrong and every rate in the bill is wrong by 5–20 %.

## Key facts

| Rule | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Measurement basis | **Net as fixed in position**; all wants deducted; **no allowance for waste, passings or laps** except where stated | ASAQS SSM §6 |
| Rounding of measurements | To the nearest **10 mm** (5 mm and over up, less than 5 mm disregarded) | ASAQS SSM §6 |
| Fractional quantities | Rounded to the nearest whole unit; one decimal place where significant | ASAQS SSM §7 |
| Order within a trade | mass, volume, area, length, number; then approximate order of value, cheapest first | ASAQS SSM §4 |
| Order of dimensions | horizontal at right angles to line of sight, horizontal parallel to line of sight, vertical | ASAQS SSM §5 |
| Concrete | m³; no deduction for voids ≤ **0,1 m³**, longitudinal pipes ≤ 200 mm dia., chamfers, reinforcement or conduits | ASAQS SSM |
| Plastic sheeting under surface beds | m², **including turn-ups and turn-downs**; no deduction for openings ≤ 1 m² | ASAQS SSM |
| Reinforcement | kg to designed lengths, from SABS mass tables, **no rolling margin allowance**; bending, hooks, tying wire, cover blocks and spacers deemed included | ASAQS SSM |
| Fabric reinforcement | m²; bending deemed included; no deduction for openings ≤ 1 m² | ASAQS SSM |
| Masonry walls | m²; skins of hollow walls **not** measured separately; no deduction for openings and recesses ≤ **1 m²** | ASAQS SSM |
| Face brickwork | m² **extra over** brickwork | ASAQS SSM |
| DPC | m², no distinction for horizontal/vertical/cavity/under sills | ASAQS SSM |
| Roof trusses | given as **an item** (not m² or number) | ASAQS SSM |
| Roof coverings | m²; underlays, purlins, battens and fixing accessories **included in description**; eaves and verges deemed included; no deduction ≤ 1 m² | ASAQS SSM |
| Ridges, hips, valleys, flashings | m; ends and intersections deemed included | ASAQS SSM |
| Structural timbers (not trusses) | m, protective surface treatment deemed included | ASAQS SSM |
| Bricklaying output benchmark | *"One man — particularly if he is a weekend builder — can probably lay a little more than 60 bricks an hour"* | Cement & Concrete SA |
| Mortar wastage | **20–50 %**, more without good supervision | Cement & Concrete SA |
| Plaster wastage (worked example) | **20 %** | Cement & Concrete SA |
| Screed wastage | **10 %** | Cement & Concrete SA |

## 1. Taking off quantities

### Method

Work in the same order as the building is built, so nothing is forgotten:

1. **Site clearance and earthworks** — clearing (item, stating approximate area), topsoil strip (m², stating average depth and where deposited), bulk excavation (m³), trench and hole excavation (m³), extra-over for hard material, carting away (m³ extra over), filling under floors (m³ **measured after compaction**, stating method/degree of compaction), soil poisoning (m²).
2. **Concrete, formwork, reinforcement** — foundation concrete, surface bed, superstructure concrete (m³ each, given separately); formwork (m² to net supported surfaces); reinforcement (kg for bar, m² for fabric); plastic sheeting (m²).
3. **Masonry** — walls by thickness and unit type (m²), foundation walls separately, DPC (m²), face brickwork (m² extra over), brickforce/reinforcement, lintels (m or number), movement-joint material (m²).
4. **Roofwork** — wall plates (m), trusses (item), covering (m²), ridges/hips/valleys/flashings (m), insulation (m²), eaves soffits (m²), fascias and bargeboards (m).
5. **Carpentry and joinery** — structural timbers (m), doors, frames, cupboards (number), skirtings and architraves (m), ironmongery (number).
6. **Ceilings, partitions, access flooring** — m².
7. **Floor, wall and ceiling finishes** — screeds, plaster, tiling (m²); skirtings (m).
8. **Plumbing and drainage** — pipework (m by size and material), fittings (number), sanitaryware (number), drains (m stating average depth), gullies, inspection eyes, manholes (number), septic tank (item or number).
9. **Glazing** — m².
10. **Paintwork** — m² and m, by substrate and system.
11. **External works** — paving (m²), fencing (m), gates (number).

### The dimension paper discipline

Traditional take-off uses four columns: **timesing / dimension / squaring / description**. Even done in a spreadsheet, keep the structure:

- Write a **heading** for every group of dimensions saying what it is and where.
- Use **"waste" calculations** in the description column to show how any derived dimension was arrived at, so the take-off can be checked.
- Take off **girths** carefully: for a rectangular building, the **centre line of the external wall** = perimeter − (4 × wall thickness) for a wall measured on its centre line; the external face girth = perimeter measured on the outside face. Getting the wrong girth is the single commonest take-off error.
- **Adjust for openings** only where the rule requires it — remember no deduction for openings ≤ 1 m² in masonry, plaster, tiling, paintwork and roof coverings.
- **Check the take-off against a second method.** Roof tiles, for example, can be estimated three ways (rafter/eaves length method, trigonometrical method, roof area method) — Coverland's estimating tables use the rafter/eaves method: number of courses from the rafter length × number of tiles along the eaves × 2 sides, plus extra tiles at hips and valleys (each hip or valley = number of courses × 2).

## 2. Waste factors

Because the ASAQS system measures net, **the estimator adds waste inside the rate**. Values in general southern African practice:

| Material | Waste allowance |
|---|---|
| Bricks and blocks | 5 % (good supervision) to 10 % |
| Mortar | **20–50 %** (Cement & Concrete SA) |
| Concrete, ready-mix | 3–5 % |
| Concrete, site-batched | 5–10 % |
| Plaster | **20 %** (Cement & Concrete SA worked example) |
| Screed | **10 %** (Cement & Concrete SA) |
| Reinforcement bar | 5–7,5 % (cutting waste + laps if laps not measured) |
| Mesh reinforcement | 10–15 % (laps) |
| Roof sheeting | 5–10 % (end and side laps, cutting) |
| Roof tiles | 5 % plus the hip/valley cutting allowance |
| Timber | 10 % |
| Floor and wall tiles | 10 % (plain), 15 % (diagonal or patterned), 20 % (small rooms/many cuts) |
| Paint | 10 % |
| Pipework | 5–10 % |

> ⚠️ Waste factors above are conventional trade allowances, **not** sourced from a standard other than the mortar, plaster and screed figures. Record the actual waste on your own jobs and replace these with your own numbers — that is what separates a builder who makes money from one who does not.

## 3. Labour constants

A labour constant is the **hours of labour per unit of work**. Only two things make a labour constant reliable: your own past records, and a consistent definition of what the gang includes.

The only benchmark in the sources consulted is Cement & Concrete SA's: *"One man — particularly if he is a weekend builder — can probably lay a little more than 60 bricks an hour."* A trade bricklayer with a labourer serving him lays substantially more than that in stretcher bond on a straight run, and substantially less on a wall with many openings, piers and returns.

**How to build your own constants, which is the only defensible method:**

1. On every job, record for each trade: the measured quantity completed, the number of people, and the hours worked. Nothing else.
2. Divide: `constant = total gang hours ÷ quantity`. Record it per element, not per job.
3. Keep a factor table for conditions: openings, height above ground, curved or raking work, small isolated areas, restricted access, and — critically at Okongo — **heat**. Productivity in sustained 38 °C sun falls materially, and the working day effectively shortens.
4. Re-derive the constants at least annually.

**Structure of a labour rate:**

```
Basic wage per hour
+ statutory on-costs (leave pay, public holidays, sick leave,
  social security, workmen's compensation, bargaining council levies)
+ non-productive time (travel, toolbox talks, rain/heat stoppages)
= all-in hourly cost
× labour constant (hours per unit)
= labour cost per unit
```

## 4. Building up a rate

For each bill item:

```
Material cost delivered to site (incl. waste %)
+ Labour (all-in rate × constant)
+ Plant and small tools attributable to the item
= Net cost
+ Site overhead recovery (if not in Preliminaries)
+ Head-office overhead %
+ Profit %
+ Risk / contingency %
= Rate
```

Worked example — 110 mm brick wall, 222 × 90 × 73 units, Class II mortar (illustrative structure; insert your own current prices):

| Component | Basis |
|---|---|
| Bricks | 52 per m² + 7 % waste = 55,6 per m² × brick price |
| Cement | 0,0151 m³ mortar per m² (from 1,51 m³/100 m²) × 7 bags/m³ = 0,106 bags/m² + 30 % mortar waste = 0,137 bags/m² |
| Sand | 0,0151 m³ × 1,35 m³ sand/m³ mortar = 0,020 m³/m² + waste |
| Brickforce, ties | per m² per the design |
| Labour | bricklayer hours + labourer hours per m² from your constants |
| Scaffold, mixer, barrow | in Preliminaries or apportioned |

## 5. Preliminaries

The ASAQS system requires that *"items shall be given for specific requirements of the employer such as notice boards, offices, procedure of work, security, insurances, etc"*, and that the contractor must be given the opportunity to itemise his own requirements *"such as site supervision, plant, sheds, etc."*

A realistic Preliminaries schedule for a house at Okongo:

**Fixed (once-off)**
- Site establishment: fencing, gate, store/container, site office, ablutions, signboard
- Setting out and survey
- Water connection or water storage tanks; power connection or generator
- Access road / hardstanding on sand
- Statutory notices, permits, plan approval fees
- Insurance: contract works, public liability, employer's liability / social security registration
- De-establishment, final clean, making good

**Time-related (per month)**
- Site supervision (foreman/site agent)
- Site office running, communications, data
- Security guard
- Small plant hire: mixer, poker, level, scaffold
- Water and fuel deliveries
- Transport of labour
- Temporary protection, dust control
- Site records, photographs, safety officer time

**Value-related**
- Head-office overhead
- Profit
- Contract price adjustment provision (where allowed)
- Contingencies

For a remote site, **the time-related preliminaries are the killer**. Every extra week of programme costs the full monthly preliminaries divided by four, whether or not any work happened. This is the direct financial reason that lead times (file 12) matter so much.

The ASAQS system also requires **provisional sums** to be given separately for each class of work, with items for profit and attendance where required (attendance descriptions to include scaffolding, other facilities, protective measures), and **contingencies** and price-adjustment provision to be given in the summary at the end of the bills.

## 6. Pricing a bill of quantities

1. **Read the preambles and the specification first.** They define what the rates must include.
2. **Check the quantities** against your own take-off on at least the top ten items by value. An error in the quantity of brickwork is worth more than an error in the price of ironmongery.
3. **Price the big items properly and the small items quickly.** Typically 20 % of the items carry 80 % of the value.
4. **Price provisional and prime cost sums** for profit and attendance, not for the work itself.
5. **Do not front-load** — putting artificially high rates on early items (earthworks, foundations) to improve cash flow is common and is a breach of good faith that a competent quantity surveyor will spot and that will hurt you when quantities are remeasured.
6. **Price for the actual site**: the remoteness at Okongo affects delivery, labour availability, wet-weather risk in the rainy season, water cost, and plant standing time. Put that in the rates or the preliminaries, not in your hope.
7. **Check the total against a rate per square metre** for a similar completed building. If your bill total divided by the floor area is wildly different from your last house, find out why before you submit.

## 7. Cash-flow forecasting

A profitable contract can still kill a builder if the cash timing is wrong.

**Build the forecast:**
1. Take the programme (file 12) and allocate each bill section to the months in which it will be executed.
2. Produce a monthly **value of work done** curve. On a house it approximates an S-curve: slow at start (site establishment, foundations), steep in the middle (superstructure, roof), slow at the end (finishes, snagging).
3. Apply the **payment terms**: work done in month *n* is certified at month-end, and paid some days later. Under a JBCC-type agreement this is a monthly interim payment certificate with a defined payment period; under an informal domestic contract it is whatever you wrote down — so write it down.
4. Deduct **retention** from each certificate.
5. Overlay **your outflows**: materials on delivery or on 30-day account, wages weekly, subcontractors monthly, plant hire monthly, preliminaries monthly.
6. The gap between the outflow curve and the inflow curve is the **peak funding requirement**. That is the number you must be able to finance. On a typical house it is often 15–25 % of the contract value.

**Cash-flow rules for a remote site:**
- Materials must be bought early because lead times are long — which pulls cash forward and worsens the peak.
- Negotiate an **advance payment or a materials-on-site provision** in the contract so that cement, steel, trusses and sheeting delivered and stored on site can be certified before they are built in.
- Get a **deposit** on a domestic contract. It is normal and it funds the establishment.
- Never let the certified-but-unpaid amount exceed what you can survive.

## 8. Retention, variations and claims

**Retention.** A percentage withheld from each interim payment as security for completion and for making good defects — typically released half at practical completion and half at the end of the defects liability period. The specific percentages and release points are set by the contract; under the JBCC Principal Building Agreement (Edition 6.2, May 2018) and Minor Works Agreement (Edition 5.2, May 2018) these are contract-data items. Confirm the actual figures from the agreement in use — the percentages quoted informally in the industry are `needs-verification`.

**Practical points on retention:**
- Retention is *your money held by someone else*. Track it as an asset and chase its release actively.
- A **retention bond** or a payment guarantee can sometimes be substituted for cash retention — worth negotiating on a cash-tight job.
- Complete the snag list quickly: the second half of the retention is released after the defects period, and a defects period that never closes because of an open snag is an interest-free loan you are giving the client.

**Variations.** A change to the scope, instructed by the principal agent or client.
- **Get it in writing before you do it.** A verbal instruction executed and later denied is unrecoverable.
- Price a variation on the same basis as the contract rates where the work is of similar character and executed under similar conditions; on a fair valuation where it is not; on daywork where it cannot be measured.
- Notify the **time** effect at the same time as the **cost** effect. A variation that also delays the works entitles you to an extension of time, but only if you claim it.

**Claims.** The usual grounds on a small building contract: late possession of site, late information, variations, exceptional weather, and delays by other contractors or the client.
- Keep a **daily site diary** (file 12). It is the single most valuable document in any claim: weather, labour on site by trade, plant, deliveries, instructions received, visitors, delays and their causes.
- Notify in the time and manner the contract requires. Most claims fail on notice, not on merit.
- Quantify the loss: prolongation is the time-related preliminaries × the period, plus disruption if you can demonstrate it.
- Photograph everything, dated.

## Open questions

- The **Standard System of Measuring Building Work is now in its 7th edition (2015)**; the freely available full texts are the 1999 6th edition and the Small or Simple Buildings edition. Measurement rules quoted here should be checked against the 7th edition for contractual use.
- Labour constants (hours per unit) for southern African trades could not be sourced from a published authority and are deliberately **not** tabulated here; the file gives the method for deriving them instead.
- Waste factors other than mortar (20–50 %), plaster (20 %) and screed (10 %) are conventional trade allowances and are `needs-verification`.
- JBCC retention percentages, payment periods and guarantee amounts are contract-data items in a paid document and were not verifiable from a free source.
