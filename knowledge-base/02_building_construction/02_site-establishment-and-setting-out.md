# Site establishment and setting out

**Summary.** Setting out is the one operation on a building where an error cannot be absorbed later — every subsequent trade inherits it. The sequence is: fix the position of the building relative to the boundary, establish a permanent datum, set profiles clear of the excavation, string the building lines, prove the rectangle with diagonals, and only then dig. On a remote sandy site like Okongo, site establishment also means solving water, storage, security and ablutions before the first trench, because none of them can be improvised once work starts.

## Key facts

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| 3-4-5 triangle (metric working set) | 3 m : 4 m : 5 m; better on a house, 6 m : 8 m : 10 m |
| Diagonal check on a rectangle | Both diagonals equal; practical site acceptance ≤ 10 mm difference on a house-sized rectangle |
| Profile board offset from trench edge | 1,0–1,5 m clear of the excavation face |
| Profile peg spacing | Two pegs per profile, 1,0–1,2 m apart, driven ≥ 600 mm into sand |
| Datum peg (temporary benchmark) | Concrete-encased, outside the works, referenced to a permanent feature |
| Screed / floor surface regularity, Class 2 (most construction work) | ≤ 5 mm under a 3 m straight edge |
| Screed departure from datum, large areas, normal purposes | ± 15 mm |
| Plaster accuracy (SANS 2001-EM1:2007) | ≤ 6 mm under a 2 m straight edge |
| **[NA]** Notice of commencement of building work to Chief Inspector | ≥ 30 days before start (GN 156/1997 reg 20) |
| **[ZA]** Notification of construction work to provincial director | ≥ 7 days before start where excavation, work at height, demolition or explosives are involved |

## 1. Before anything moves

1. **Confirm the erf/plot boundary.** On a surveyed township erf, locate the survey beacons. If they are missing or the plot is on communal land held under a Permission to Occupy, get the boundary re-established or agreed in writing with the Traditional Authority and neighbours before you set out. Building over a boundary is the most expensive mistake possible.
2. **Check building lines and setbacks** with the local authority or regional council. Note also the servitude positions for water, power and drainage.
3. **Record the existing site**: spot levels on a grid, existing trees to be kept, the direction of surface flow after rain, and where the water will pond. In flat sandy country the difference between a dry and a wet house is 300 mm of level.
4. **Photograph everything** before clearing. This is your evidence of pre-existing condition.
5. **Give statutory notice** — see Key facts.

## 2. Site clearing

- Strip topsoil and all vegetable matter (grass, roots, stumps) from the entire building footprint plus a working margin of at least 2 m. Topsoil under a slab rots, compresses and feeds termites.
- Strip depth is typically 150–300 mm; on grassed Kalahari sand it is often shallower than expected because the organic horizon is thin. Strip until you reach clean sand.
- Stockpile topsoil separately, outside the building footprint and out of the way of vehicles, for later landscaping.
- Grub out tree stumps completely and backfill the void with compacted approved fill in layers — never with the arisings and never with topsoil.
- **Termite treatment** is a decision point on this ground. If soil poisoning is specified, it happens after clearing and again before the slab is cast; coordinate with the programme (file 12).
- Establish a temporary surface-water cut-off drain on the uphill side, however slight the slope.

## 3. Datum and levels

Everything vertical on the site is measured from one number.

1. Establish a **temporary benchmark (TBM)** outside the excavation and outside vehicle routes: a steel pin or rebar set in a 300 × 300 × 400 mm concrete block, painted, with the value written on it.
2. Relate the TBM to something you can recover if it is destroyed — a manhole cover, a boundary beacon, a kerb, a corner of an existing building. Record two independent references.
3. On a small building, set the TBM to a convenient round number, e.g. **finished floor level (FFL) = 100,000**, and work in relative levels. On a site with municipal drainage or in a flood-prone oshana margin, use the real reduced levels from the survey.
4. **Setting FFL.** Minimum practical rules for a hot dry, sandy site: FFL at least **150 mm above the highest adjacent finished ground level** (this is also the minimum height of the DPC above finished ground under SANS 10400-K 4.5.3.7), and preferably 300 mm above the natural ground on a flat site so that surface water runs away from the building.

### Levelling with a dumpy (automatic) level

- Set up midway between backsight and foresight to cancel collimation error.
- **Reduced level of instrument (RLI) = known RL + backsight**; **RL of new point = RLI − foresight**.
- Always close the traverse back onto the starting benchmark. A misclosure of more than about 5 mm on a house-sized loop means you repeat it.
- Check the level's collimation with a **two-peg test** at the start of every job and after any knock: set two pegs ~40 m apart, read both from the midpoint (this gives the true difference), then read both from close behind one peg. If the two differences disagree by more than ~5 mm over 40 m, the instrument needs adjustment.

### Levelling with a rotating laser

- Faster and effectively one-person, and much better in the wind than a staff-and-optics setup only if the tripod is stable — sand is a poor tripod footing, so use a plate or bed the legs in.
- Self-levelling lasers drift out of range if the tripod settles; re-check against the TBM every morning and after any bump.
- In bright northern-Namibian daylight, an unaided laser line is invisible outdoors: use a detector on the staff.

### Water level (clear hose)

Cheap, accurate and unaffected by heat haze or wind. A 20 m clear hose filled with water, with both ends open, gives a true level between the two menisci. It is the fallback when the laser battery dies and it is genuinely accurate to a few millimetres over a house.

## 4. Setting out the building

### The sequence

1. **Establish the base line.** Usually the front wall face, parallel to and a fixed distance from the boundary or street. Peg both ends.
2. **Set the first corner** by measuring along the base line.
3. **Turn the right angle** with 3-4-5 (use 6-8-10 or 9-12-15 on anything bigger than a garage — the longer the triangle, the smaller the angular error). Measure with a *steel* tape, not a fibreglass one, and keep it at constant tension.
4. **Complete the rectangle** by measuring the two remaining sides.
5. **Check the diagonals.** They must be equal. If they are not, the shape is a parallelogram, not a rectangle, and every internal wall and every roof member will be out.
6. **Erect profiles** (batter boards): two pegs and a horizontal board per corner leg, set ~1,0–1,5 m clear of the trench edge so they survive the excavation. Set the top of every profile board to one common level — usually the top of the foundation concrete or a fixed offset above FFL — using the level, so that the boards themselves become a level reference.
7. **Mark the lines on the profiles** with saw cuts or nails: wall face, foundation face, trench edge. Colour-code or label them. Cut, don't pencil — pencil disappears in a week of sun and dust.
8. **String the lines** and transfer them to the ground with a plumb bob and lime/sand marking, or spray paint.
9. **Re-check diagonals with the lines strung**, then remove the strings so they are not damaged by the excavator, leaving the profiles.

### 3-4-5 in practice

| Short leg | Long leg | Hypotenuse | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 m | 4 m | 5 m | Small structures, checking a corner |
| 6 m | 8 m | 10 m | Standard house setting out |
| 9 m | 12 m | 15 m | Long buildings, where accuracy matters most |

Angular error is roughly the linear tape error divided by the leg length: a 5 mm error on a 3 m leg is ~0,1°, which over a 12 m wall is 20 mm of drift. On a 9 m leg the same 5 mm error costs a third of that. Always use the longest triangle the site allows.

### Diagonal check arithmetic

For a rectangle of sides *a* and *b*, diagonal *d* = √(a² + b²). Worked example for a 12,000 × 8,400 house: d = √(144 + 70,56) = √214,56 = **14,648 m**. Both diagonals must read 14,648 m. A 20 mm difference means each corner is out by about 10 mm — correctable by rotating the rectangle slightly, not by moving one corner.

### Transferring levels and lines into the excavation

- Once the trench is open, the profiles remain the only reference. Never re-measure off the trench edge.
- Set **foundation level pegs** in the trench bottom: steel pins driven to the underside-of-concrete level, at ≤ 2 m centres and at every corner and step, levelled from the TBM. The concrete is then cast to the top of the pegs.
- On sloping ground, **step the foundation**: steps not exceeding 400 mm, each step a whole multiple of the masonry course height, and no step within 1,0 m of a corner.

## 5. Permissible setting-out deviations

South African construction standards do not publish a single consolidated setting-out tolerance table for small buildings, so the values below are trade practice, cross-checked against the downstream tolerances that *are* published. They should be treated as working targets, not as code.

| Item | Working tolerance | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Position of building relative to boundary | ± 25 mm | Trade practice; must never encroach a building line |
| Overall length/width of a set-out rectangle | ± 10 mm on dimensions up to 15 m | Trade practice |
| Difference between diagonals | ≤ 10 mm on a house-sized rectangle | Trade practice |
| Foundation trench width | +50 mm / −0 mm on the specified width | Trade practice; SANS 10400-H sets minimum widths |
| Level of underside of foundation concrete | ± 15 mm | Trade practice |
| Level of top of foundation concrete | ± 10 mm | Trade practice; sets the first course |
| Verticality of masonry (see file 13) | See `13_quality-defects-and-tolerances.md` | |
| Floor/screed surface regularity, Class 2 | ≤ 5 mm under a 3 m straight edge | Cement & Concrete SA, screeds leaflet, Table 1 |
| Floor level departure from datum, large areas | ± 15 mm | Cement & Concrete SA, screeds leaflet |
| Plaster | ≤ 6 mm under a 2 m straight edge | SANS 2001-EM1:2007, quoted in C&CSA plastering leaflet |

The Clay Brick Technical Guide states the requirement plainly for foundations: *"The accuracy of the setting out shall be achieved through positive control measures: their relative location to site boundaries and adjacent structures shall be verified. Regular checks on the trench widths, trench lengths and the length of diagonals across external corners must be carried out."*

## 6. Site layout — the things that are not the building

Plan these on a sketch before the first delivery. Retro-fitting them costs double.

**Access and turning.** A 10 m rigid truck delivering cement or a crane-truck delivering trusses needs a firm approach and somewhere to turn. On deep sand, lay a 150 mm compacted gravel/calcrete haul strip. A bogged delivery vehicle costs a day and a recovery fee.

**Material storage**
- **Cement** must be off the ground on pallets or timber bearers, under cover, in a lockable store, with the stack no more than 12 bags high and rotated first-in-first-out. Cement that has hard lumps that cannot be crumbled by hand is not fresh and must not be used. Plan for a maximum of 4–6 weeks' stock even though the supply line is long: cement deteriorates.
- **Sand and stone** on a hard, clean, drained platform, separated so they do not intermix. Site the stockpiles within barrow distance of the mixer — every extra 10 m of barrow run is real money over a slab.
- **Bricks and blocks** stacked on level ground, banded or stacked in a stable bond, clear of vehicle routes.
- **Timber and trusses** stacked flat, off the ground on bearers at ≤ 1,5 m centres, and covered — SA pine left in northern-Namibian sun bows and splits within days.
- **Sheeting** stacked flat with a fall to shed water and a cover; never stored wet and stacked, which causes white rust on galvanised and Chromadek finishes.

**Water.** Concrete, mortar and plaster water must be fit for drinking. Provide storage of at least one full day's peak demand plus curing — for a 60 m² slab pour with hand batching, budget on the order of 4 000–6 000 ℓ available on the day. On a borehole site, test the water for chlorides and sulphates before using it in concrete. **[NA]** The employer must provide an adequate supply of clean drinking water, kept cool in a hot environment (GN 156/1997 reg 46).

**Security.** Perimeter fencing (even a droppers-and-wire line), a lockable container or store for cement, tools, sanitaryware, taps, cable and sheeting, and a night watchman. In remote areas the high-theft items are cement, roof sheeting, copper, taps and PV panels.

**Ablutions and welfare.** **[NA]** GN 156/1997 Chapter 3 requires sanitary conveniences, washing facilities, rest and dining facilities and drinking water at workplaces; sanitary conveniences are required on a scale of one water closet per 15 employees or part thereof of each sex, with an additional closet per 25 employees above that, and one urinal per 25 male employees or part thereof. On a small remote site this usually means a chemical toilet or a properly constructed pit latrine sited well away from any borehole (see the 15 m / 30 m separation guidance for absorption fields in `09_plumbing-and-drainage.md`), plus a handwashing point and a shaded eating area. Shade is a safety item, not a luxury, at 38 °C.

**Site office and records.** Even a container: somewhere to keep the approved drawings, the site diary, delivery notes, the OHS file, and the first-aid box out of the dust.

**Waste and spoil.** Designate a spoil dump and a rubble skip position at set-out stage. Excavated sand is a resource — keep it for backfill and blinding rather than carting it away and buying it back.
