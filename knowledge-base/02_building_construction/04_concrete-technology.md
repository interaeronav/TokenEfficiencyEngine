# Concrete technology for builders

**Summary.** Concrete is cement, water, sand and stone. Its strength is governed almost entirely by the water:cement ratio and its durability by curing; everything else is refinement. In South Africa and Namibia all cement must comply with **SANS 50197** (common cement) or **SANS 50413** (masonry cement) and carry an NRCS Letter of Authority number on the bag — and masonry cement may **never** be used in concrete. This file gives the cement designations, the standard site mixes by both volume and mass, slump control, hot-weather curing regimes for a 35 °C site, cube testing procedure, and the causes and cures of the common defects.

## 1. Cement

### Strength classes (SANS 50197-1, Table 1)

The number is the guaranteed **28-day compressive strength in MPa** of a standard mortar prism at w/c = 0,50 — not of your concrete. The letter is the early-strength rate: **L** slow, **N** normal, **R** rapid.

| Strength class | 2-day min | 7-day min | 28-day standard strength | Initial set |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 32,5L ᵃ | – | ≥ 12,0 MPa | | |
| 32,5N | – | ≥ 16,0 MPa | ≥ 32,5 and < 52,5 MPa | > 75 min |
| 32,5R | ≥ 10,0 MPa | – | | |
| 42,5L ᵃ | – | ≥ 16,0 MPa | | |
| 42,5N | ≥ 10,0 MPa | – | ≥ 42,5 and < 62,5 MPa | > 60 min |
| 42,5R | ≥ 20,0 MPa | – | | |
| 52,5L ᵃ | ≥ 10,0 MPa | – | | |
| 52,5N | ≥ 20,0 MPa | – | ≥ 52,5 MPa | > 45 min |
| 52,5R | ≥ 30,0 MPa | – | | |

ᵃ L classes are defined only for CEM III cements.

Note the "window": 32,5 and 42,5 classes must fall **within** a strength band at 28 days, not merely exceed it. That is why a 42,5N cement is not simply "better" — it develops heat faster and, in a hot dry climate, can make plastic shrinkage cracking worse if curing is poor.

### Cement types (SANS 50197-1, Table 2 — abbreviated)

| Type | Name | Clinker (K) | Main extender |
|---|---|---|---|
| CEM I | Portland cement | 95–100 % | — |
| CEM II/A-S, B-S | Portland slag | 80–94 / 65–79 % | Blastfurnace slag 6–20 / 21–35 % |
| CEM II/A-V, B-V | Portland-fly ash | 80–94 / 65–79 % | Siliceous fly ash 6–20 / 21–35 % |
| CEM II/A-L(L), B-L(L) | Portland-limestone | 80–94 / 65–79 % | Limestone 6–20 / 21–35 % |
| CEM II/A-M, B-M, C-M | Portland-composite | 80–88 / 65–79 / 50–64 % | Two declared extenders |
| CEM III/A, B, C | Blastfurnace cement | 35–64 / 20–34 / 5–19 % | Slag 36–65 / 66–80 / 81–95 % |
| CEM IV/A, B | Pozzolanic cement | 65–89 / 45–64 % | Pozzolana + fly ash 11–35 / 36–55 % |
| CEM V/A, B | Composite cement | 40–64 / 20–38 % | Slag + pozzolana |

Sulfate-resisting variants are designated **-SR** (CEM I-SR 0/3/5, CEM III/B-SR, CEM III/C-SR, CEM IV/A-SR, CEM IV/B-SR).

**How to read a bag.** A bag marked *CEM II/A-L 42,5N* is a Portland-limestone cement, 80–94 % clinker, strength class 42,5, normal early strength. A bag marked *MC 22,5X* under SANS 50413 is a **masonry cement** — for bricklaying, block laying, rendering and plastering only, never concrete.

**Practical selection for Okongo:**
- General structural concrete: CEM I or CEM II/A at 32,5N or 42,5N. 32,5N is the workhorse and is more forgiving in heat.
- Concrete in ground with unknown sulfate content: consider a sulfate-resisting or slag-rich cement, and confirm with the supplier.
- Highly extended cements (CEM II/B, CEM III) develop strength slowly. They are excellent for mass concrete and reduce heat, but only use them where you can genuinely protect and cure the surface — otherwise early cracking is the result.
- Store bags off the ground, under cover, in a lockable store. If lumps cannot be crumbled by hand, the cement is not fresh: do not use it.

## 2. Aggregates and water

**Sand.** Concrete sand should be clean, well graded and free of organic matter, clay lumps and salt. The field test: fill a clear bottle two-thirds with sand, top up with water, shake, let stand — a silt/clay layer of more than about 5–8 % of the sand depth means the sand needs washing or rejecting. Dune and beach sands are often too single-sized for good concrete without blending.

**Stone.** 19 mm or 26 mm nominal size for concrete; 13,2 mm where the section is thin or reinforcement congested, and for infill concrete in masonry. Stone should be clean, hard and free of dust coating.

**Water.** Fit for drinking. On a borehole site, high chloride or sulphate content matters — chlorides attack reinforcement, sulphates attack the paste. If in doubt, have the borehole water tested before it goes into structural concrete.

## 3. Standard mixes

### 3.1 Volume batching — the two-bag mix (Cement & Concrete SA)

For **32,5N or 32,5R common cement with 19 mm stone**:

| Application | Strength at 28 d | Cement | Sand | Stone | Yield |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| House foundations, mass fill | LOW ~15 MPa | 2 × 50 kg | 3½ barrows (230 ℓ) | 3½ barrows (230 ℓ) | 0,35 m³ |
| House floors, footpaths, driveways | MEDIUM ~25 MPa | 2 × 50 kg | 2½ barrows (160 ℓ) | 2½ barrows (160 ℓ) | 0,26 m³ |
| Precast, heavy-duty floors, reinforced members | HIGH ~30 MPa | 2 × 50 kg | 2 barrows (130 ℓ) | 2 barrows (130 ℓ) | 0,22 m³ |

For **42,5N or 42,5R common cement with 19 mm stone**:

| Application | Strength | Cement | Sand | Stone | Yield |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 15 MPa | 2 × 50 kg | 4 barrows (260 ℓ) | 4 barrows (160 ℓ*) | 0,39 m³ |
| Medium | 25 MPa | 2 × 50 kg | 3 barrows (200 ℓ) | 3 barrows (200 ℓ) | 0,30 m³ |
| High | 30 MPa | 2 × 50 kg | 2½ barrows (160 ℓ) | 2½ barrows (160 ℓ) | 0,26 m³ |

\* as printed in the source table; the barrow count is the reliable figure.

For **32,5 cement with 13,2 mm stone**: Low 4,0 / 3,5 barrows (yield 0,33 m³); Medium 2,5 / 2,0 (0,24 m³); High 2,0 / 1,5 (0,20 m³).
For **42,5 cement with 13,2 mm stone**: Low 4,5 / 3,0 (0,37 m³); Medium 3,0 / 2,5 (0,28 m³); High 2,5 / 2,0 (0,24 m³).

### 3.2 Small batches — same container for everything

| Strength | Cement | Sand | Stone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 1 | 3½ | 3½ |
| Medium | 1 | 2½ | 2½ |
| High | 1 | 2 | 2 |

These small-batch proportions have already been adjusted for the bulking of cement in small containers. Use the same bucket/drum/tin for every material in the batch and fill it flush to the brim.

### 3.3 Mass batching — quantities per cubic metre

Using the CCSA mixes leaflet (32,5 cement, 19 mm stone):

| Strength | Cement | Sand | Stone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low (house foundations) | 290 kg | 0,65 m³ | 0,65 m³ |
| Medium (floors, paths, drives) | 385 kg | 0,62 m³ | 0,62 m³ |
| High (precast, heavy-duty floors) | 460 kg | 0,60 m³ | 0,60 m³ |

Bags per m³, from the *Quantities for ordering* leaflet: 32,5 cement with 19 mm stone — **5,8 bags** (15 MPa), **7,7 bags** (25 MPa), **9,2 bags** (30 MPa). With 42,5 cement and 19 mm stone — **5,1 / 6,6 / 7,8 bags** respectively.

> ⚠️ These batches do not *guarantee* a strength. Where a specific strength is required, do trial mixes and test cubes. All concrete structures must be designed by a professional structural engineer.

### 3.4 Water:cement ratio

The single most important number in concrete. Every extra litre of water per bag costs strength and adds shrinkage.

- The CCSA position is practical rather than numerical: *"Only sufficient water should be used to produce a workable mix of 60–100 mm slump. Too much mixing water will reduce the strength of the concrete."*
- Working guide for site concrete: a 25 MPa mix batched with 385 kg cement/m³ and around 190–210 ℓ of *total* water per cubic metre gives w/c ≈ 0,50–0,55. Remember that sand carries free water — damp sand can hold 5 % or more by mass, which is 30–50 ℓ per cubic metre of concrete. **Deduct it.**
- On a hot windy site the temptation is to add water to keep the mix workable as it stiffens. Do not. Use a plasticiser instead, or reduce the batch size and mix more often.

## 4. Mixing, placing, compacting

**Mixing.** Machine mixing is better than hand mixing. Charge some water first, then stone, cement and sand, then the rest of the water; mix at least 2 minutes after all materials are in. For hand mixing, mix sand and cement dry to a uniform colour before adding stone and water.

**Transport.** Keep the barrow run short and the route firm. Long rough runs segregate the mix.

**Placing.** Place in layers, not in heaps that are then raked — raking segregates. Never drop concrete more than about 1,5 m free-fall.

**Compaction.** Rod or vibrate until the surface closes and mortar appears. Over-vibration brings paste and water to the surface and leaves a weak, dusty, crazing-prone finish. Under-vibration leaves honeycombing.

**Finishing.** Do not trowel bleed water back into the surface. Wait until the surface water has gone, then float. Over-trowelling a rich mix causes crazing.

## 5. Curing — the make-or-break in hot dry weather

Cement hydrates only in the presence of water. *"Once the concrete is allowed to dry, no further strength development will take place."* Preventing water loss also prevents cracking, crazing and a weak dusty surface. **Cure for at least 7 days.**

### Why Okongo is a hard curing environment

Plastic shrinkage cracking is driven by the **rate of evaporation from the fresh surface**, which rises with air temperature, concrete temperature and wind speed, and falls with relative humidity. Northern Namibia in the dry season combines high air temperature, very low humidity and steady wind — the three worst factors at once. The conventional threshold in international practice is an evaporation rate around **1,0 kg/m²/h** (0,2 kg/m²/h for concretes containing silica fume or with little bleed), above which precautions are mandatory. This threshold is widely used but is **not** stated in the CCSA leaflets consulted and is flagged `needs-verification` against a primary source.

### Practical hot-weather regime

**Before the pour**
- Pour early morning or late afternoon; avoid casting a slab into the middle of the day.
- Shade the aggregate stockpiles; keep the sand damp so evaporative cooling works for you.
- Use the coolest available mixing water. Cold water is the cheapest way to drop concrete temperature.
- Dampen (do not soak) formwork, subgrade and blinding so they do not suck water out of the mix.
- Erect wind breaks — shade cloth on droppers on the windward side of a slab is cheap and works.
- Have the curing materials **on site before the concrete arrives**: plastic sheeting, hessian, sand, a water supply and a means of spraying.

**During and immediately after**
- Do not add water to restore workability. Use a plasticiser.
- Start curing as soon as the surface will take it without marking — in these conditions that can be within an hour of finishing, not the following morning.
- Fog-spray the surface between finishing operations if it is drying visibly.

**Curing methods, best to worst for a hot dry site**
1. **Ponding** (slabs with an edge) — most effective; not always practical with limited water.
2. **Wet hessian or sacking under plastic sheeting** — the standard. Keep it wet, keep the plastic weighted at the edges so wind does not lift it. This is the method CCSA specifies for curing test cubes on site.
3. **Damp sand layer** (25–50 mm) kept wet — excellent on foundations and floor slabs, and cheap on a sandy site.
4. **Plastic sheeting alone** — traps moisture but leaves a mottled surface; the sheeting must be sealed at the edges or the wind ventilates it and it does nothing.
5. **Curing compound** — sprayed membrane. Works, but coverage must be uniform and it will interfere with later bonding of plaster, screed or tile adhesive. Note that films from curing compounds must be removed **mechanically**, not with solvents, before plastering.
6. **Leaving formwork in place** — good for vertical elements; keep the top exposed surface covered as well.

**Curing periods**

| Element | Minimum |
|---|---|
| Foundations, mass concrete | 3 days covered; 7 days preferred |
| Floor slabs, driveways, footpaths | 7 days |
| Structural reinforced elements | 7 days minimum, longer for slag/fly-ash cements |
| Screeds and plaster (see files 07) | Protect and cure; C&CSA requires spatterdash to be kept damp ≥ 3 days |

## 6. Testing

### Slump test (SANS 5862-1)

Sample about half a wheelbarrow of freshly mixed concrete. Damp all tools. Place the mould narrow end up on a level 600 × 600 × 3 mm steel plate, stand on the footpieces. Fill in **three layers of about equal depth**, tamping each layer **25 times** with the rounded end of a 16 mm × 600 mm steel rod. Overfill the last layer, strike off with a sawing and rolling motion of the rod, step off, lift the mould straight up, invert it beside the concrete, lay the rod across it and measure the drop to the highest point of the concrete **to the nearest 5 mm**. If you do not get a true slump (shear or collapse), repeat.

Target: **60–100 mm** for hand-compacted work.

### 150 mm cubes (SANS 5861-3, SANS 5863)

Three cubes per test age — so six if testing at 7 and 28 days. Moulds clean, joint faces greased, bolts tight, release oil thinly smeared. Fill in **50 mm layers**, tamping each layer **at least 45 times** with the 16 mm rod, with the rod penetrating the previous layer by no more than 10 mm. Strike off level with a steel float. Label each cube (company, contract/reference number, date made) on absorbent paper pressed onto the top. Cover with damp sacking then plastic and store in shade, out of the wind, undisturbed. Next day: loosen bolts, strip, write the details on the cube with a lumber crayon, and place the cubes **under water at 22 °C–25 °C** until they go to the laboratory.

7-day strength is typically 65–75 % of 28-day strength for ordinary Portland-based mixes — useful as an early warning, not as an acceptance criterion.

## 7. Admixtures

| Type | Typical dosage | What it does | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plasticiser (water reducer) | 200–450 mℓ / 100 kg cementitious | Increases slump at same water, or cuts water ~10 % at same slump | Many contain a retarder; overdosing causes problems. Increases shrinkage and creep if used to raise workability |
| Superplasticiser | 750–2 500 mℓ / 100 kg cementitious | Water reduction 15–25 %; flowing concrete | Rapid workability loss — add just before placing |
| Air entrainer | per manufacturer | Freeze-thaw resistance, cohesion | Reduces strength; irrelevant for frost at Okongo but useful for cohesion in harsh sands |
| Accelerator | per manufacturer | Faster early strength | Chloride-based accelerators must never be used in reinforced concrete |
| Retarder | per manufacturer | Delays set — genuinely useful in 38 °C heat to prevent cold joints in a long pour | Excessive dosage can cause the concrete not to set |

Admixtures must comply with **SANS 50934** and are never a substitute for good mix design, good materials or good workmanship.

## 8. Common defects — cause and cure

| Defect | Appearance | Cause | Prevention | Repair |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Plastic shrinkage cracking** | Short, random, parallel cracks appearing within hours of placing, often on slab surfaces | Surface water evaporating faster than bleed water rises — hot, dry, windy | Wind breaks, shade, cool materials, fog spray, immediate curing | Not structural if shallow; fill and seal. Deep cracks may need cutting out |
| **Plastic settlement cracking** | Cracks over the line of top reinforcement or at a change of section | Concrete settling around restraint while still plastic | Lower slump, better compaction, revibrate before set | Re-vibrate/close while plastic; otherwise fill |
| **Crazing** | Fine hexagonal network in the surface skin | Over-trowelling a rich mix; rapid drying of the surface | Do not over-trowel; cure early | Cosmetic; a good coating covers it |
| **Honeycombing** | Voids where mortar is partially or completely absent | Too much stone, or segregation of stone and mortar; poor compaction | Correct mix, correct placing height, proper vibration | Cut out all weak/honeycombed material to sound concrete, saw-cut the edges ≥ 10 mm deep to avoid feather edges, install mechanical anchors if needed, prime with slurry, patch with a polymer-modified plastic mixture |
| **Blowholes** | Small roughly spherical voids in a formed face | Inadequate compaction of the thin layer against the formwork | Better vibration against the form; correct release agent | Fill with a proprietary blowhole filler |
| **Cold joint** | A visible plane of weakness where a new pour met a partly set one | Delay between batches exceeding the setting time, especially in heat | Plan the pour; use a retarder; never let a face stand more than ~30 min in 38 °C | Cannot be repaired invisibly; cut a proper construction joint if the delay is unavoidable |
| **Dusting / weak surface** | Surface powders under traffic | Bleed water trowelled back in; concrete allowed to dry out; over-trowelling; low cement | Do not trowel bleed water; cure 7 days | Surface hardener, or remove and re-top |
| **Misplaced surfaces** | Faces out of line | Formwork misaligned, moved during placing, or under/overfilled | Brace and check formwork; check before and during the pour | Cut back and patch, or grind |

## Open questions

- The 1,0 kg/m²/h evaporation-rate threshold for mandatory hot-weather precautions is international practice (ACI-derived) and was not found in the Cement & Concrete SA leaflets; treat as `needs-verification` for southern African use.
- Numerical maximum water:cement ratios by exposure class (as in SANS 10100-2) were not obtainable from a primary source; the w/c figures given are working guides derived from the published cement contents.
- The 7-day / 28-day strength ratio quoted is general engineering practice, not a sourced SANS figure.
