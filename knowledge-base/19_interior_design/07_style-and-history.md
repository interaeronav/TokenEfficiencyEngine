# A working history of interior style

**Summary.** A reference for naming and reproducing looks accurately. Each entry gives dates, defining features, materials, colours, the key furniture pieces, and key designers. Dates are conventional period boundaries, not hard edges — styles overlap, revive and are revived again, and most real interiors are hybrids. Contemporary African and Afro-modern work, and Southern African vernacular-inspired interiors, are covered at length because they are under-documented in the standard style literature and directly relevant to practice in the region.

> ⚠️ Style names are marketing categories as much as historical ones. "Japandi", "Farmhouse" and "Industrial" are 21st-century retail constructions, not historical movements. Use them for communication, not for scholarship, and do not conflate a revival with the original.

## Classical and Palladian (c.1550 Italy; British revival 1715–1760s)
Derived from Andrea Palladio's *I Quattro Libri dell'Architettura* (1570) and revived in Britain by Lord Burlington and William Kent. **Features:** absolute symmetry; the classical orders; rooms proportioned as cubes, double cubes and simple ratios; a defined base–middle–cornice on every wall. **Materials:** stone, marble, plaster, mahogany, gilt. **Colours:** stone, off-white, Pompeian red, deep green, blue-grey. **Furniture:** Kent's massive gilded console tables and settees, marble-topped side tables, giltwood mirrors, mahogany chairs. **Designers:** Palladio, Inigo Jones, William Kent, Robert Adam (later, lighter).

## Baroque (c.1600–1720)
**Features:** movement, drama, illusion; curved and broken pediments; theatrical light; ceiling painting. **Materials:** marble, gilding, heavy velvets and damasks, tortoiseshell and brass boulle marquetry. **Colours:** crimson, deep gold, blue-black, ivory. **Furniture:** the state bed, the boulle commode, the giltwood console, the tall-back upholstered chair. **Designers:** Bernini, Le Brun, André-Charles Boulle, Daniel Marot.

## Rococo (c.1720–1760)
The reaction against Baroque grandeur: smaller, lighter, more feminine. **Features:** asymmetric rocaille (shell and rock) ornament, cabriole legs, boiserie panelling, mirrors, intimate rooms for conversation. **Materials:** painted and gilded wood, silk, porcelain, marquetry. **Colours:** pale blue, pink, celadon, cream, gold. **Furniture:** bergère and fauteuil armchairs, the bombé commode, the bureau plat, the chaise longue, the console-desserte. **Designers:** Meissonnier, Nicolas Pineau, Cressent, Oeben.

## Georgian (1714–1830, Britain)
**Features:** the sash window, the six-panel door, symmetry, full-height panelling then dado-and-plaster, plaster cornices, the chimneypiece as the room's focus. **Materials:** painted softwood joinery, mahogany, marble, brass. **Colours:** the true Georgian palette is stronger than the modern revival supposes — drab, stone, "dead" white, sage, blue-verditer, and, in grand rooms, strong pea green and Prussian blue. **Furniture:** the Chippendale, Hepplewhite and Sheraton chair; the tripod table; the bureau bookcase; the tallboy; the Pembroke table. **Designers:** Robert Adam, Thomas Chippendale, George Hepplewhite, Thomas Sheraton.

## Regency (c.1811–1830)
**Features:** sharper, more archaeologically informed classicism, with Egyptian, Greek and Chinese exoticism; strong verticals; drapery as architecture. **Materials:** rosewood, ebonised beech with brass inlay, ormolu, silk. **Colours:** stronger and more contrasting than Georgian — deep yellow, crimson, black, bronze green, with white and gold. **Furniture:** the klismos chair, the sabre-leg chair, the sofa table, the chiffonier, the convex girandole mirror. **Designers:** Thomas Hope, Henry Holland, John Nash, George Smith.

## Victorian (1837–1901)
**Features:** density and layering; deep-buttoned upholstery; multiple pattern; heavy curtains and portières; the mantelpiece crowded with objects; separate rooms for separate functions. **Materials:** mahogany and walnut, horsehair, plush, chenille, cast iron, encaustic tile, papier-mâché. **Colours:** dark and rich — maroon, bottle green, plum, ochre, with the new aniline dyes producing magenta and mauve after 1856. **Furniture:** the chesterfield, the balloon-back chair, the whatnot, the aspidistra stand, the overmantel mirror. **Designers:** Pugin (Gothic Revival), Owen Jones (*The Grammar of Ornament*, 1856), Christopher Dresser.

## Arts and Crafts (c.1860–1910)
The reaction against industrial ornament. **Features:** honest, visible construction; handwork; nature-derived pattern; the inglenook; built-in furniture; simple planes of colour. **Materials:** oak (often quarter-sawn and fumed), hand-blocked wallpaper, hand-woven textiles, hammered copper, leaded glass, tile. **Colours:** the Morris palette — indigo, madder red, olive, ochre, sage. **Furniture:** the Morris chair, the settle, the sideboard with visible pegged joints, the ladder-back rush-seated chair. **Designers:** William Morris, Philip Webb, C. F. A. Voysey, Charles Rennie Mackintosh, Gustav Stickley, Greene & Greene, Frank Lloyd Wright (Prairie).

## Art Nouveau (c.1890–1910)
**Features:** the whiplash curve; total design where architecture, furniture and fittings are one; organic and botanical motifs; stained glass. **Materials:** wrought iron, carved and bent wood, glass, ceramic, mosaic. **Colours:** mauve, olive, peacock blue, gold, muted violets. **Furniture:** Guimard's fluid cabinetry, Majorelle's marquetry, Thonet bentwood, Tiffany lamps. **Designers:** Victor Horta, Hector Guimard, Louis Majorelle, Antoni Gaudí (Modernisme), Louis Comfort Tiffany.

## Art Deco (c.1920–1939)
**Features:** geometry — chevron, sunburst, ziggurat, stepped forms; symmetry; lacquered surfaces; the celebration of luxury materials and of machine modernity together. **Materials:** shagreen, parchment, macassar ebony, ivory and its substitutes, lacquer, chrome, mirror, marble, Bakelite. **Colours:** black and gold; silver and cream; jade, coral, ivory; strong contrast. **Furniture:** the Ruhlmann cabinet on tapered legs, the Eileen Gray Bibendum chair and E-1027 table, the Jean Dunand lacquer screen, the club chair. **Designers:** Émile-Jacques Ruhlmann, Eileen Gray, Jean Dunand, Pierre Chareau, and — as the movement's minimalist wing — **Jean-Michel Frank**, whose parchment, shagreen, mica and straw-marquetry rooms, produced with Adolphe Chanaux and the Giacometti brothers, are the direct ancestor of contemporary luxury minimalism.

Parallel and worth naming separately: **Syrie Maugham's all-white interior** of the 1920s–30s (mirrored screens, indirect lighting, white upholstery) — the first modern "look" invented by a decorator rather than an architect.

## Bauhaus and Modernism (school 1919–1933; movement to the 1960s)
**Features:** function determines form; no applied ornament; open plan; the free façade; standardisation; the honest expression of material and structure. **Materials:** tubular steel, plate glass, plywood, concrete, leather, canvas. **Colours:** white, black, grey, plus the primaries used as signal rather than decoration. **Furniture:** Breuer Wassily and Cesca chairs; Mies van der Rohe Barcelona chair and stool; Le Corbusier/Perriand LC2, LC4 chaise; Alvar Aalto Paimio chair and Stool 60. **Designers:** Gropius, Mies, Breuer, Le Corbusier, Charlotte Perriand, Lilly Reich, Aalto.

## Mid-Century Modern (c.1945–1969)
**Features:** organic and sculptural form; open plan; indoor-outdoor connection; low horizontal furniture; large glass; the fireplace as a freestanding object. **Materials:** teak, walnut, rosewood, moulded plywood, fibreglass, aluminium, wool boucle, terrazzo. **Colours:** warm neutrals with mustard, burnt orange, avocado, teal, and strong single accent walls.

**Furniture canon** — the pieces that define it:
- Charles and Ray Eames: Lounge Chair and Ottoman (670/671, 1956), DCW/DCM plywood chairs, Eames Shell chair, ESU storage
- Eero Saarinen: Tulip table and chair (1957), Womb chair (1948)
- Arne Jacobsen: Egg (1958), Swan (1958), Series 7 (1955), Ant (1952)
- Hans Wegner: Wishbone CH24 (1949), Round Chair "The Chair" (1949), Papa Bear
- Finn Juhl: Chieftain chair (1949), Pelican chair
- Poul Henningsen: PH Artichoke (1958), PH5 lamps
- George Nelson: Marshmallow sofa, Coconut chair, Ball clock, Platform bench
- Florence Knoll: sofas and credenzas — the office aesthetic of the century
- Isamu Noguchi: coffee table (IN-50, 1947), Akari paper lanterns
- Pierre Jeanneret: Chandigarh chairs
- Serge Mouille, Gino Sarfatti, Achille Castiglioni (Arco, 1962): the lighting canon
- Jean Prouvé: Standard chair, Compas desk
- Gio Ponti: Superleggera (1957)

## Brutalism (c.1950–1975)
**Features:** mass, texture and the honest expression of structure; board-marked in-situ concrete; deep reveals; monumental interior volumes; sculptural stairs. **Materials:** concrete, brick, timber shuttering marks, cork, slate, hammered metal. **Colours:** grey, ochre, burnt orange, brown, moss. **Furniture:** Paul Evans brutalist cabinets, Pierre Chapo oak furniture, Jean Prouvé, Charlotte Perriand's Les Arcs work. **Designers:** Le Corbusier (late), Alison and Peter Smithson, Paul Rudolph, Lina Bo Bardi. Currently the strongest revival influence in high-end residential.

## Postmodernism (c.1975–1990)
**Features:** historical quotation used ironically; the return of colour, pattern and ornament; the broken pediment as a joke; the flat scenographic surface. **Materials:** laminate, terrazzo, coloured marble, glass block, neon. **Colours:** salmon, mint, grey, black-and-white grid, primary accents. **Furniture:** Michael Graves for Alessi, Robert Venturi's plywood chair series, Philippe Starck's early work. **Designers:** Venturi, Graves, Charles Moore, Aldo Rossi, Ettore Sottsass.

## Memphis (1981–1987)
A Milan group founded by **Ettore Sottsass**, formally short-lived and disproportionately influential. **Features:** deliberate refusal of good taste; cheap materials in expensive objects; clashing pattern; unstable geometry. **Materials:** plastic laminate, terrazzo, coloured glass, neon. **Colours:** primary plus pink, turquoise, black-and-white squiggle. **Furniture:** Sottsass Carlton room divider (1981) and Tahiti lamp, Michele De Lucchi First chair, Peter Shire, Nathalie du Pasquier's patterns, George Sowden. Its DNA runs directly into contemporary colour-forward practice — India Mahdavi's Sketch is unimaginable without it.

## Minimalism (c.1985–present)
**Features:** reduction to essentials; the junction as the only ornament; shadow gaps; concealed everything; light as the subject; generous void. **Materials:** plaster, concrete, pale timber, limestone, glass, blackened steel. **Colours:** white, greige, sand, black, single deep accent. **Furniture:** low, few, and often bespoke; Poul Kjærholm PK22, Donald Judd furniture, Jean-Michel Frank reissues, custom banquettes. **Designers:** John Pawson, Claudio Silvestrin, Tadao Ando, Peter Zumthor (adjacent), **Vincent Van Duysen**, **Joseph Dirand**, **Studio KO**. Trace it back to Jean-Michel Frank and forward through **Andrée Putman's Morgans Hotel (1984)**, which proved austerity could be commercial.

## Scandinavian (c.1930–present)
**Features:** daylight maximised; light woods; functionalism softened by warmth (*hygge*); democratic, affordable design; craft continuity. **Materials:** birch, beech, pale oak, wool, linen, sheepskin, enamel, paper. **Colours:** white, pale grey, soft blue, sage, with black accents and natural wood. **Furniture:** Aalto Stool 60 and Paimio chair, Wegner's chairs, Børge Mogensen Spanish chair, Kaare Klint, Poul Kjærholm PK22, the Louis Poulsen PH range, Marimekko textiles. **Designers:** Aalto, Klint, Wegner, Jacobsen, Mogensen, Kjærholm, Bruno Mathsson.

## Wabi-sabi and Japandi
**Wabi-sabi** is a Japanese aesthetic of impermanence, imperfection and incompleteness, expressed in interiors as raw plaster, unfinished timber, hand-thrown ceramics, asymmetry, restraint and the acceptance of wear. In the Western design world its principal exponent is **Axel Vervoordt**, whose book *Wabi Inspirations* and 2007 Venice exhibition *Artempo — Where Time Becomes Art* argue that time is the material. **Materials:** lime plaster, char-burnt timber (*shou sugi ban*), linen, raw silk, stone, paper, iron. **Colours:** off-white, ash, clay, charcoal, ochre.

**Japandi** is a 2010s retail hybrid of Japanese minimalism and Scandinavian warmth: low furniture, pale-to-mid timber, black accents, linen, ceramics, and a strictly limited palette. It is a look, not a movement — useful shorthand with clients, but do not present it as historical.

## Mediterranean and Spanish colonial
**Vernacular, with an American revival c.1915–1940.** **Features:** thick lime-plastered walls, arches, courtyards, tiled floors, exposed timber beams, wrought iron, deep reveals, whitewash. **Materials:** lime plaster, terracotta, clay tile, olive and chestnut timber, wrought iron, glazed ceramic. **Colours:** white, ochre, terracotta, olive, indigo, with cobalt-and-white tile. **Furniture:** rush-seated chairs, refectory tables, carved chests, low daybeds, woven pendant shades. **Designers:** the American revival names are Addison Mizner, George Washington Smith, Wallace Neff; the contemporary Mediterranean revival is dominated by Spanish, Greek and Ibizan practices.

## Moroccan
**Features:** courtyard-centred planning; carved and pierced plaster (*gebs*); ceilings of painted cedar; built-in banquette seating; layered rugs; lanterns casting patterned light. **Materials:** **zellige** (hand-cut glazed terracotta tile with its characteristic irregularity), **tadelakt** (polished waterproofed lime plaster), carved plaster, cedar, brass, wool, bejmat floor tile. **Colours:** the pinks and ochres of Marrakech; white plaster; jewel-toned tile in emerald, cobalt, saffron. **Furniture:** low banquettes, poufs, Beni Ourain and Boucherouite rugs, brass and pierced-metal lanterns, carved wooden screens. **Key reference project:** **Studio KO's Musée Yves Saint Laurent Marrakech (2017)**, the definitive contemporary translation of Moroccan craft into modern building.

## Industrial (c.1990s onward)
Not a historical style but a conversion aesthetic derived from New York and London loft living. **Features:** exposed brick, structure, ductwork and services; steel-framed glazing (Crittall); polished concrete; open volumes; salvaged furniture. **Materials:** blackened steel, brick, concrete, reclaimed timber, glass, leather, galvanised metal. **Colours:** brick red, black, grey, oxblood, tan. **Furniture:** Tolix chair, Jieldé lamp, factory carts, drafting stools, Prouvé reissues.

## Farmhouse and Modern Farmhouse (c.2010s)
Largely a North American media construction. **Features:** shiplap or board-and-batten walls, painted tongue-and-groove, Shaker cabinetry, apron-front sinks, black window frames, barn doors. **Materials:** painted softwood, white oak, marble or quartz, matt black metal, jute. **Colours:** white, black, warm grey, natural wood. It is a decorating vocabulary rather than a movement, and it dates quickly — advise clients accordingly.

## Maximalism (c.2015–present)
The reaction against a decade of grey minimalism. **Features:** pattern on pattern; saturated colour on walls, ceilings and joinery; collections displayed rather than stored; antique and contemporary mixed; the deliberate over-furnishing of a room. **Materials:** wallpaper, velvet, chintz, lacquer, brass, marble. **Colours:** everything, but in a controlled relationship — the discipline is a repeating palette, not an absence of one. **Designers:** the tradition runs from Victorian density through Sister Parish's anti-matching layering to Miles Redd, Martyn Lawrence Bullard, Kelly Wearstler, India Mahdavi and Studio Ashby.

## Contemporary African and Afro-modern

This is the most commercially and culturally significant development in interiors on the continent, and it is systematically under-recorded in Northern-hemisphere style literature. It is not a single style; it is a family of positions.

**What it is.** Contemporary interior work that treats African craft, material and pattern as a **living contemporary practice** rather than as ethnographic decoration. Its defining move is the pairing of a modern or minimal spatial frame with objects that carry specific, attributable craft provenance.

**Features.** Rigorous modern plan and section; handmade and hand-marked surfaces; strong graphic pattern used architecturally rather than as an accessory; commissioned rather than imported furniture; artwork by living African artists as the anchor of a room; deep engagement with climate — shade, ventilation, thermal mass, indoor-outdoor living.

**Materials.** Rammed earth and earth plaster; sun-dried and fired brick; polished screed; hardwoods (iroko, sapele, kiaat, wild olive, tamboti — use only with verified sustainable provenance); woven grass, ilala palm and sisal; mud cloth (bògòlanfini, Mali); kuba cloth and raffia (DRC); shweshwe printed cotton **[ZA]**; kente (Ghana); Basotho blankets **[ZA]**; beadwork (Zulu, Ndebele, Maasai); brass and recycled aluminium casting; hand-thrown and pit-fired ceramics; recycled-glass and paper beads; Tuareg leather and reed mats; Berber and Ethiopian weaving.

**Colours.** Two distinct positions coexist. The **earth position**: ochre, terracotta, umber, charcoal, bone, indigo — drawn from soil, clay and dye. The **graphic position**: high-contrast black and white (Ndebele wall painting, kuba geometry) with saturated accents in cobalt, emerald, orange and pink.

**Key furniture and object types.** The Senufo stool and daybed (Côte d'Ivoire); the Ashanti stool (Ghana); the Nupe and Dogon ladder and granary door; Cameroon Bamileke feather headdresses and juju hats; Zulu and Tonga baskets; Ndebele beaded blankets; Moroccan and Berber rugs; contemporary pieces by African designers and studios — including South African furniture and lighting makers, and West African brass and bronze casting.

**Key names.** *Architecture and interiors:* **Francis Kéré** (Burkina Faso — earth construction and climate-first design, Pritzker 2022); **David Adjaye** (Ghana/UK); **Mokena Makeka** and **Mphethi Morojele** **[ZA]**; **Studio KO** (French, but their Marrakech work is the reference for craft-into-modern translation). *Design and craft:* **Cheick Diallo** (Mali); **Babacar Niang**; **Bibi Seck** (Senegal); **Ifeanyi Oganwu** (Nigeria); **Hamed Ouattara** (Burkina Faso — recycled-oil-drum furniture); **Jomo Tariku** (Ethiopia/US); **Peter Mabeo** (**Botswana** — Mabeo Furniture, arguably the most internationally significant Southern African furniture studio); **Egg Designs** **[ZA]**; **Ashanti Design** **[ZA]**; **Houtlander** **[ZA]**; **Dokter and Misses** **[ZA]**. *Textiles:* **Aboubakar Fofana** (Mali, natural indigo); **Adele Dejak** (Kenya); **Mungo** **[ZA]** (woven textiles, Plettenberg Bay).

> ⚠️ The line between homage and extraction is real and clients will be asked about it. Buy from named makers, pay properly, credit them in published photography, and never present a mass-produced "tribal-print" import as African craft. This is both an ethical position and, increasingly, an award-jury criterion (file `03`).

## Southern African vernacular-inspired interiors **[NA] [ZA]**

**Sources of the vocabulary.** Namibian and South African vernacular building is not one tradition. The relevant strands:
- **Herero and Himba homesteads** (northern Namibia) — circular plan, timber frame, earth-and-dung render, a defined hearth and a defined threshold.
- **Ovambo homesteads** — palisade enclosures of mopane poles, a sequence of enclosed and semi-enclosed spaces, deep shade.
- **Nama and Damara matjieshuis** — the portable domed reed-mat house; the reed mat as a working environmental material.
- **Cape Dutch** **[ZA]** (17th–19th c.) — thick lime-washed walls, gabled façades, yellowwood and stinkwood joinery, brandsolder ceilings, riempie (thonged leather) seating, klompje brick or slate floors, shuttered small openings.
- **Karoo and Bushveld farmhouse** — corrugated iron roofs, stoeps (verandas), pressed-metal ceilings, deep shade, whitewash, sash windows.
- **Ndebele wall painting** **[ZA]** — the high-contrast geometric mural tradition, now the most internationally recognised Southern African graphic language.
- **Sotho and Tswana litema** — incised and painted earth-wall relief patterning.
- **German colonial Namibia** **[NA]** — the Swakopmund/Lüderitz Jugendstil and Wilhelmine legacy; pressed-metal ceilings, timber verandas, tall casements. Handle with historical awareness; it is contested heritage.

**How it translates into contemporary interiors.**
- **Palette.** The literal ground: bone, sand, ochre, rust, burnt umber, dust grey-green (from mopane, camelthorn and euphorbia), charcoal from fire, and the sharp accent of Himba red ochre (*otjize*) or a Basotho-blanket blue. Avoid cool northern greys — they read as dead under Southern African light.
- **Materials.** Lime and earth plaster; rammed earth; face brick and polished screed; thatch and reed ceilings; local hardwoods and reclaimed railway timber; riempie and rawhide; sisal, ilala palm and grass weaving; ostrich eggshell, horn and bone (verify legality and provenance); local stone; corrugated and rusted steel used deliberately; recycled glass.
- **Spatial moves.** The stoep as the principal living room; deep overhangs and shaded thresholds; cross-ventilation and high-level openings; thermal mass with small, deeply reveal-set windows; a defined hearth; an outdoor kitchen or braai as a designed room, not an afterthought; the courtyard.
- **Light.** Southern African light is intensely bright and high in UV. Design for **shade first**: deep reveals, shutters, screens, brise-soleil, pergolas, and interior surfaces that are matt rather than reflective. Specify high-lightfastness fabrics (file `06`).
- **Furniture and objects.** Riempie benches and chairs; kiaat and wild olive; Karoo farm tables; Tonga and Zulu baskets; Himba and Herero objects; Basotho blankets as throws and wall hangings; contemporary local makers (Mabeo, Houtlander, Dokter and Misses, Ashanti Design, Egg Designs); pit-fired and wood-ash-glazed ceramics.
- **Contemporary reference points.** The Namibian and South African lodge tradition — desert and bush lodges that combine minimal modern volumes with local craft, thatch and earth — is the region's most developed body of interior work and the most useful precedent set for anyone practising there. Individual project and studio credits could not be verified in this research and are `needs-verification`.

**What to avoid.** Generic "safari lodge" pastiche — mass-produced animal print, imported "tribal" carving of no provenance, and colonial-nostalgia styling. The strong contemporary work in the region is specific, sourced and climate-driven.
