# Patterns of thinking among leading interior designers

**Summary.** Fourteen recurring habits of mind, drawn where possible from documented statements. This file is deliberately conservative about quotation: **only quotations that could be traced to a retrievable source during research are given as quotations.** Where a saying is famous but its source could not be confirmed, it is described as attributed and flagged. The through-line is that the strongest practitioners think about **effect on a person in a room** before they think about style, and about **editing** before they think about adding.

> ⚠️ Interior design is drowning in unsourced aphorisms. Several of the most-repeated "quotes" in the field circulate without a verifiable origin. Treat every unattributed decorating maxim as folklore until you have found it in a book with a page number.

## 1. Atmosphere and feeling before style — Ilse Crawford

The single most consequential shift in interiors thinking in thirty years is Crawford's insistence that the subject of interior design is not the room but the person in it. She founded the **Man and Well-Being** department at Design Academy Eindhoven in **2000** — the name is the argument. Her book titles carry the same thesis: ***The Sensual Home*** (1997), ***Home Is Where the Heart Is?*** (2009), ***A Frame for Life*** (2014). "A frame for life" is the most compact statement of the position available: the interior is not the picture, it is the frame; the life is the picture.

**What this means operationally.** Design the *sequence of experiences* first: arriving, taking off a coat, sitting down, being warm, seeing someone across a room, being alone. Specify for the senses — what does this surface feel like at 6 a.m. on bare feet; what does this chair do to a body after two hours; what does the room sound like when four people talk. Style then follows as a consequence rather than as a starting point. Her studio's projects — Soho House New York, Babington House, the Electric Cinema, Duddell's — are all rooms people want to stay in longer than they intended, which is the measurable version of the claim.

## 2. Patina, imperfection and time as material — Axel Vervoordt

Vervoordt's 2007 Venice exhibition ***Artempo — Where Time Becomes Art*** states the position in its title: the passage of time is not damage, it is content. His practice grew out of buying and restoring 16th-century houses on the **Vlaeykensgang** in Antwerp in 1969 and out of decades of dealing — so his aesthetic is built on handling old things, not on imagining them. His book ***Wabi Inspirations*** connects this to the Japanese wabi tradition of the modest, the worn, the asymmetric and the incomplete.

**What this means operationally.** Prefer materials that improve as they age: unlacquered brass, oiled oak, lime plaster, linen, leather, natural stone with a honed rather than polished finish. Avoid finishes that can only deteriorate — high-gloss lacquer, chrome, thin veneers, printed laminates. Accept and design for wear patterns. Mix periods so that no object can be dated as "the year this room was done".

## 3. The layered, collected interior versus the decorated set

The recurring contrast in the literature is between a room that has been **assembled over time** and a room that has been **installed on one day**. Sister Parish's method is the documented anti-matching position: painted furniture, chintz, needlepoint, quilts and botanical prints, deliberately **avoiding matching patterns**, described by *Architectural Digest* as "refreshingly unstudied, unself-conscious". Her achievement was making a room look as though nobody had decorated it.

**What this means operationally.** Never buy a set. Vary provenance, period, price and formality within a room. Include at least a few things the client already owned. Let the room accept later additions — leave a shelf half empty, a wall with room for another picture. Test: could this room absorb an unplanned inherited object without collapsing? If not, it is a set, not an interior.

## 4. Proportion and scale as the foundation

Every technically-trained designer in file `02` — Marino, Mahdavi, Dirand, Grange — came through architecture or the decorative-arts schools, where proportion is taught before decoration. The practical hierarchy in a room is: **volume → openings → furniture footprint → surface**. A room with the wrong proportions cannot be rescued by colour; a room with the right proportions is very hard to ruin.

**What this means operationally.** Fix the plan and the section before anything else. Use the ceiling height to set the datum lines — picture rail, door head, joinery top, curtain track — and align them. Draw furniture at actual size. When something feels wrong and you cannot say why, measure it: it is almost always scale.

## 5. Working from the plan versus working from an object

Two legitimate starting points, and confusing them wastes months.
- **From the plan** — the architectural route. Marino, Dirand, Van Duysen, Studio KO. The spatial idea comes first and everything is specified to serve it. Better for large, technical, hospitality and workplace projects.
- **From an object** — the decorator's route. Jean-Michel Frank building rooms around a Giacometti plaster lamp; Vervoordt building around a piece of 15th-century furniture; Jacques Grange decorating **Château Gabriel** around themes from Proust's *In Search of Lost Time* because Saint Laurent loved Proust. Better for residential work with a collection or a narrative.

Both work. What fails is starting from an object and then discovering the plan does not accommodate it, or starting from the plan and then bolting on objects that carry no meaning.

## 6. The discipline of editing

The strongest habit shared across the field is **subtraction**. Frank's luxury minimalism was defined by what he removed; Putman's Morgans Hotel was explicitly "high-standard hotel with a **small budget**", achieved by austerity rather than accumulation; Dirand's signature is described as strong lines and near-monochrome.

**What this means operationally.** Design the room fully, then remove one thing from every surface and one piece of furniture from the room. Photograph the room and look at the photograph — the camera edits more honestly than the eye. Keep a "removed" box rather than throwing things away; half of it comes back, half never does.

## 7. Colour intuition versus colour theory — India Mahdavi

Mahdavi's Sketch London (2014) — the pink Gallery room with 250 David Shrigley drawings — is the clearest recent demonstration that colour is a psychological instrument, not a decorative one. Her own claim, in her words:

> *"I don't want to sound like I'm boasting, but I changed the way people thought about pink."*
> — India Mahdavi ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India_Mahdavi))

Note that Mahdavi is one of the most systematically trained designers in the field (Beaux-Arts, Parsons, SVA) and nonetheless works intuitively with colour. The pattern is general: **theory to diagnose, intuition to decide.** Use colour systems (Munsell, NCS — file `06`) to *describe* what you are doing and to communicate it to a supplier; do not use them to generate schemes. And test everything in the actual room, because a colour is a function of the light falling on it.

The counter-case is instructive too: Syrie Maugham built a reputation on all-white rooms in the 1920s–30s, then by the mid-1930s **moved to emerald, magenta and shocking pink**. A signature palette is a position, not a permanent identity.

## 8. Light as a material

Light is the only "material" in an interior that changes hourly and that most schemes are never tested against. Vervoordt titled a book ***Living with Light***. Crawford's sensory approach makes light the first variable. Putman's Morgans achieved its effect largely through restraint in lighting.

**What this means operationally.** Visit the site at three times of day before designing. Design daylight first — where it enters, what it hits, what it reflects off. Then layer artificial light: ambient, task, accent, decorative, and a very low-level night layer. Never light a room from a single central source. Specify colour temperature consistently within a space (file `06`), dim everything, and put the controls where a person's hand actually goes.

## 9. Constraint as the generative device — Andrée Putman

The Morgans Hotel brief, in Putman's framing, was to make a "high-standard hotel with a small budget" — and that constraint produced the aesthetic that made her internationally famous. The same pattern recurs: Sister Parish started in a **14 × 14 ft office at $35 a month** in the Depression; Syrie Maugham started on a **borrowed £400** at 42; Kelly Wearstler funded her firm from a single photoshoot fee.

**What this means operationally.** Ask what the *scarce* resource is — money, time, ceiling height, daylight, a heritage listing — and make it the organising idea rather than the apology. Projects with no constraints tend to produce unmemorable interiors, which is why the most expensive commissions are so often the dullest.

## 10. Doubt whether the thing should exist — Faye Toogood

> *"the world doesn't necessarily need any more chairs, but if I'm going to do something, then I hope that it will help push down barriers."*
> — Faye Toogood ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faye_Toogood))

This is the field's most honest statement about its own material consequences, and it is increasingly the criterion by which award juries judge (file `03`): reuse, longevity and end-of-life reasoning now carry weight against novelty.

**What this means operationally.** Reuse the client's furniture. Reupholster rather than replace. Specify things that can be repaired, and record how. Prefer demountable to glued. Ask of every specification: what happens to this in twenty years?

## 11. Narrative and the client's life

The best residential work is biographical. Grange's Château Gabriel was organised around Proust because his client loved Proust. Michael S. Smith's Obama Oval Office used **Native American baskets and ceramics** in a taupe-and-beige scheme — a specific argument about whose objects belong in that room. Nate Berkus built a bestselling book on the premise that objects with histories are what make a house habitable (*The Things That Matter*, 2012).

**What this means operationally.** Find the one true thing about this client — a place, a collection, a discipline, an inheritance — and let it structure the scheme without becoming a theme. The line between narrative and pastiche is whether the objects are genuinely theirs.

## 12. "One thing that doesn't belong"

A widely-taught tension principle: every well-composed room contains one element that argues with the rest — an ungainly antique in a modern room, a rough object among refined ones, an aggressive colour in a quiet scheme. Its function is to stop the room reading as a showroom and to give the eye somewhere to catch.

**Attribution status: `needs-verification`.** This principle is repeated across the decorating literature and is variously credited to Billy Baldwin, David Hicks, Dorothy Draper and others; **no attributable original statement could be confirmed during this research.** Describe it as a common working principle, not as a quotation from anyone.

Operationally it is real regardless of provenance: Vervoordt's practice of setting a contemporary artwork against 15th-century plaster is this principle at high volume, and Sister Parish's refusal to match patterns is a distributed version of it.

## 13. Craft knowledge underneath taste

Maugham's documented apprenticeship under **Ernest Thornton-Smith** taught her furniture restoration, trompe-l'œil, curtain design and upholstery *before* she opened a business. Vervoordt learned by restoring buildings. Bullard and Michael S. Smith learned by dealing. The recurring finding of file `02` is that people who can identify, date, price and repair objects make better decorating decisions than people who can only recognise them.

**What this means operationally.** Learn to make at least three of the things you specify (file `09`). Handle the samples. Visit the workshops. Being able to tell a joiner exactly what shadow gap you want, in his vocabulary, is worth more than any mood board.

## 14. Argue the project aloud

INSIDE, the World Festival of Interiors, is "the only global awards event where designers **present their work live to an expert panel of judges and an audience of peers, clients and press**" — and this is not just an award format, it is a test of whether the project has an idea. A scheme that cannot be defended in five minutes under questioning usually does not have one.

**What this means operationally.** Before presenting to a client, say the scheme out loud to someone who will push back. Reduce it to one sentence. If you need the images to make the argument, the argument is not there yet.

## How the best of them handle taste disagreements

This is the practical end of every pattern above, and the literature is unhelpfully silent on it. The working method that follows from the documented behaviour of the practitioners in file `02`:

1. **Separate function from taste.** "This sofa is too big for the circulation" is a fact and you should win it. "I don't like the green" is a preference and you should usually lose it.
2. **Trade, don't argue.** Concede three small preferences to hold the one structural decision that carries the scheme.
3. **Make them see it, not imagine it.** Most disagreements are failures of visualisation. A large sample, a mock-up, a paint board, a chair delivered on approval, or a night-time lighting trial resolves more disputes than any argument.
4. **Write it down.** Record the client's instruction and your advice against it in an email. This protects the relationship as much as it protects you — it converts an argument into a decision with a date.
5. **Know when the room is not yours.** Sister Parish's rooms were praised for looking "unstudied, unself-conscious" precisely because they submitted to the way the client lived. A designer who cannot tolerate a client's ugly chair in the finished photograph is designing for the photograph.
6. **Fire the client rarely, and early if at all.** Disagreement about taste is survivable; disagreement about *process* — a client who will not sign off, or who changes decisions after they are ordered — is not. Deal with that at stage 1 (file `04`), not at stage 6.

## Open questions

- The "one thing that doesn't belong" principle has **no confirmed attribution**. Do not credit it to a named designer.
- Billy Baldwin's, David Hicks's and Albert Hadley's most-quoted maxims are widely circulated but were **not verifiable from a retrievable source** during this research and are therefore omitted rather than quoted. They should be sourced from their own books (file `08`) with page references before use.
- Ilse Crawford's own statements on atmosphere are paraphrased here from her department name and book titles, not quoted. Her Netflix *Abstract* episode and her published writing are the correct primary sources.
- Vervoordt's stated wabi-sabi philosophy is inferred from his exhibition and book titles; the encyclopaedic source does not set out the philosophy in his words.
