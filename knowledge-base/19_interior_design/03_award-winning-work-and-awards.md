# Interior design awards and award-winning work

**Summary.** Interior design has no Pritzker. Instead it has three separable systems: **editorial lists** (AD100, Elle Decor A-List) which confer status but are not competitions and cannot be entered; **paid-entry competitions** (SBID, FRAME, Dezeen Awards, Restaurant & Bar Design Awards, INSIDE, SA Décor and Design Awards) which are the practical route to publication for a small studio; and **professional-body honours** (IIDA, ASID, IID **[ZA]**) which reward service, research and career contribution as much as projects. Knowing which is which determines strategy: you cannot apply to AD100, and winning SBID will not by itself get you into it.

## 1. Editorial lists — status you cannot buy

### AD100
*Architectural Digest*'s annual list of the designers and architects the magazine considers the most influential. It is compiled editorially by AD's staff; there is no submission process, no criteria published as a rubric, and no jury in the competition sense. Inclusion follows from published work in AD and its international editions, from client calibre, and from editorial relationships. AD publishes national variants (AD France, AD Italia, AD India, AD Middle East, AD Germany, AD Spain) each with their own AD100 or equivalent, which matters strategically — the regional lists are far more reachable than the US one.
> `needs-verification`: the current AD100 selection methodology, list size confirmation and recent inductees. The Architectural Digest site returned HTTP 403 to automated fetching during this research.

### Elle Decor A-List
*Elle Decor*'s annual roster of leading interior designers, also editorially chosen, published with a top tier (variously called the "A-List Titans" or similar) and a broader list. Same logic as AD100: it is an output of coverage, not an input to it.
> `needs-verification`: current list size, tier names and methodology. The Elle Decor site returned HTTP 403 during this research.

**What actually gets you onto these lists:** consistently published projects in the parent magazine and its peers; a recognisable signature that an editor can describe in one sentence; a photographer whose images work at magazine scale; and a PR representative who talks to those editors. It is a media outcome and should be pursued as one (file `10`).

## 2. The Andrew Martin Interior Designer of the Year Award

Run by the British furnishings brand **Andrew Martin**, this is the most idiosyncratic and most commercially useful award in decoration. It is a single overall prize rather than a category system; entrants submit a body of work rather than one project; and the substantive prize is inclusion in the annual ***Interior Design Review*** book, which functions as the trade's global directory of current decorating work. The jury is deliberately not made of interior designers — historically it has been drawn from broadcasters, novelists, editors, retailers and public figures, on the argument that interiors are judged by clients, not peers.

**What it rewards:** breadth and consistency across a portfolio; a distinctive and photogenic personal style; rooms that read as *lived in* rather than as installations.

## 3. Paid-entry competitions — the working designer's route

### SBID International Design Awards
Organised by the **Society of British & International Interior Design**. Three broad families of category: **Interior Design** (restaurants, retail, healthcare, offices, show homes, luxury apartments and more), **Product Design** (commercial and residential), and **Interior Fit-Out** (contract, residential, workplace). Entries are "evaluated by a revered panel of leading industry experts for **technical content and creativity**" — the explicit pairing of technical with creative is the distinguishing feature and is why SBID rewards well-documented submissions with drawings, specifications and performance data, not only photographs. Attracts entries from **over 90 countries**, and holds **GOLD Standard accreditation from The Independent Awards Standards Council**, which is a meaningful integrity signal in a field with several pay-to-win schemes.
**What juries reward:** a clear technical narrative — how the problem was solved, what was specified and why — alongside the images. SBID also runs a public-vote component in some editions; check the current rules before promising a client a purely jury-judged outcome.

### Dezeen Awards
The most competitive open architecture/interiors programme by volume: **over 4,300 entries across 49 categories** in 2026. Interiors categories typically cover house interior, apartment interior, hotel and short-stay interior, restaurant interior, bar and club interior, workplace interior, retail interior, civic and cultural interior, health and wellbeing interior, small interior, and interior of the year, plus studio-level and emerging-studio prizes. 2026 timeline: standard deadline **27 May 2026**, late entries to **3 June 2026 23:59 London**, party **16 November 2026 at the Royal Opera House**. The 2026 panel included **Norman Foster**, **Martin Lotti** (Nike chief design officer), **Corey Damen Jenkins** and **Mark Grattan**. Headline sponsor Trimble; other sponsors Framery, Gaggenau, Programa, Sanderson Design.
**What juries reward:** a strong single idea, legible in three images; originality over polish; sustainability and reuse credentials, increasingly decisively; and projects that photograph well on a phone screen, since Dezeen is a digital publication first.

### Restaurant & Bar Design Awards
Running **since 2009**, operated by **Erbda Ltd** from Cardiff Bay, and described by its organisers as "the world's most important and influential design competition for food and beverage spaces worldwide." Categories split restaurant and bar, with national/regional and standalone-element categories (surface, lighting, identity). **2025** results included **Litt** as Best Overall Bar and **Suparnin** as Best Overall Restaurant; **2026** winners are announced the week of **5 October 2026**. Coverage extends to *Tatler*, *Vogue*, *Elle Décor* and *GQ*.
**What juries reward:** atmosphere and identity above technical resolution; a single strong material or spatial gesture; and evidence that the design works commercially — covers, dwell time, the bar as a room. IHG's Diana Martinez frames the sector as blending "culinary arts, mixology, interior design, and social science… into a unique, multi-sensory experience," which is a fair description of the judging temperament.

### INSIDE World Festival of Interiors
Part of the **World Architecture Festival**, and structurally unique: it is "the only global awards event where designers **present their work live to an expert panel of judges and an audience of peers, clients and press**." Ten INSIDE awards are made across disciplines including health, hospitality and transport, culminating in **World Interior of the Year**. The seminar programme has featured Ilse Crawford, Ben van Berkel, André Fu and Mathieu Lehanneur. Media partner Dezeen, reaching roughly two million visitors a month.
**What juries reward:** the ability to *argue* the project. Because judging is a live crit, the win goes to a clear thesis defended under questioning — the closest thing in interiors to an architecture-school jury. Practise the presentation; the entry alone does not win it.
> `needs-verification`: recent World Interior of the Year winners could not be retrieved.

### FRAME Awards
Run by **Frame**, the Amsterdam-based spatial-design publisher. Categories are organised around spatial typologies (retail, hospitality, workplace, wellness, cultural, residential) plus emerging-designer, execution and material awards. Frame's editorial position is more experimental and more retail/brand-facing than Dezeen's.
> `needs-verification`: current categories, judging process, entry fees and recent winners. The Frame Awards site redirected to frameweb.com and returned only metadata to automated fetching.

## 4. Professional-body honours

### IIDA (International Interior Design Association)
Runs a large rolling programme — its submissions platform listed **183 entries** across 21 pages at the time of research. Confirmed live or forthcoming at that date: **2027 IIDA Student Design Competition**, **2026 IIDA/WestEdge Design Fair Booth Awards**, **2026 IIDA/WestEdge Design Fair Product Awards**, **2026 IIDA Global Excellence Awards**, **2026 IIDA Educator of Impact Award**, **2026 IIDA Healthcare Design Awards**, **2027 IIDA Emeritus**. Separately, IIDA makes **honours** — the **ICON Award**, **Member of the Year**, the **College of Fellows**, and educator, student and chapter recognitions. IIDA also historically runs the **Will Ching Design Competition** for practices of a small headcount, which is the single most useful award in this file for a one-to-five-person studio; confirm its current status. `needs-verification`
**What IIDA rewards:** research-backed, evidence-led commercial interiors — particularly workplace and healthcare, where measured outcomes (utilisation, patient outcomes, staff retention) carry weight. This is the most "professional" of the award cultures and the least about styling.

### ASID (American Society of Interior Designers)
Runs **Design Excellence** awards, the **Ones to Watch** scholarship/recognition programme for emerging designers, and the **College of Fellows** for career contribution, alongside chapter-level awards.
> `needs-verification`: ASID's current award list, criteria and entry routes — the ASID awards page returned 404 during research.

## 6. What juries actually reward — a synthesis

Across every scheme above, judging conversations turn on the same handful of things.

1. **One legible idea.** Juries see hundreds of entries in a day. The projects that survive have a thesis expressible in a sentence — "a bar built as a single continuous banquette", "a house organised around a courtyard of water", "an office where nobody has a desk". Beautiful projects without a thesis lose to less beautiful projects with one.
2. **Evidence of difficulty overcome.** Constraint is rewarded: a tight budget, a listed building, a bad plan, an impossible site. Entries that hide the constraint lose the credit for solving it.
3. **Material honesty and craft.** Close-up images of junctions, edges and joinery do disproportionate work. This is where SBID's "technical content" criterion bites.
4. **Photography.** A weak photographer will sink a strong project. Every scheme is judged from images. Budget for a specialist interiors photographer, shoot at the right time of day, and get plans and sections drawn properly for the submission.
5. **Sustainability and reuse.** Now decisive rather than decorative in the European schemes. Reused structure, reclaimed material, demountable construction, low-carbon finishes and honest end-of-life reasoning all score.
6. **Narrative fit to the organiser.** Dezeen wants a story; SBID wants technical rigour; the Restaurant & Bar awards want atmosphere and commercial performance; IIDA wants measured outcomes; INSIDE wants an argument you can defend live. Rewrite the same project three ways for three schemes.
7. **Credit everyone.** Photographer, architect, contractor, joiner, lighting designer, stylist, client. Entries with missing credits get marked down and, worse, generate the sector's most common public disputes.

## 7. How a small studio should actually use awards

- **Year 1–2:** enter one emerging-designer category and one project category at a mid-tier scheme. Budget one professional photoshoot per year and reuse it across every entry.
- **Year 3–5:** target Dezeen Awards and one sector specialist (Restaurant & Bar if hospitality, IIDA if workplace, SBID if you can document technically).
- **Any year:** enter INSIDE only if you can present live and travel to the festival — otherwise the fee is wasted.
- **Always:** treat the shortlist, not the win, as the deliverable. A shortlist gives you a press release, a logo, a client-facing credential and an editor's attention, at a fraction of a win's improbability.
- **Never:** pay for an award that does not publish its jury. The proliferation of pay-to-win "international design awards" is the field's main integrity problem; SBID's Independent Awards Standards Council accreditation exists precisely because of it.
