# Interior design principles and technical knowledge

**Summary.** The craft knowledge an interior designer needs that is not taste: proportion, human dimensions and clearances, colour systems, lighting, acoustics, materials, textiles and the scale rules for art and rugs. Figures below are **conventional design values in wide professional use**, drawn from the standard references named in file `08` (Ching, Panero & Zelnik, Neufert, Time-Saver Standards). They are dimensioning guidance, not code.

> ⚠️ **None of the numbers in this file substitutes for the building code or accessibility standard applying to your jurisdiction.** Egress widths, accessible clearances, fire ratings, guarding heights and lux minima for workplaces are all statutory. **[ZA]** check the relevant SANS 10400 parts; **[NA]** check the Namibian building regulations; UK check Approved Documents; US check IBC/ADA. All statutory values are `needs-verification` here and must be read from source.

## 1. Proportion systems

**The golden section (φ ≈ 1.618).** A rectangle of 1:1.618. Its practical use in interiors is not mystical — it is a *reliable* default when nothing else determines a ratio: a picture-rail height, a panel division, a rug-to-room relationship, a coffee-table-to-sofa length. Its cousin ratios do the same job: √2 (1:1.414, the A-paper ratio), 2:3, 3:5, 5:8.

**Practical proportion rules that actually get used:**
- **Wall panelling / dado:** dado rail at roughly one-third of wall height (typically 900–1100 mm); picture rail at roughly two-thirds to three-quarters (typically 1800–2100 mm in a 2700 mm room).
- **Skirting to ceiling height:** roughly 1/18 to 1/16 of ceiling height. 2400 mm ceiling → 130–150 mm skirting; 3000 mm → 170–190 mm; a 4000 mm Georgian room can carry 250–300 mm.
- **Cornice depth:** roughly equal to or slightly greater than skirting height in traditional rooms.
- **Datum alignment.** The single most effective proportional device: pick one horizontal line (usually the door head, 2040 mm) and align joinery tops, mirror tops, picture-hanging centres and shelf lines to it or to a clean sub-multiple.
- **Rule of thirds in elevation.** Compose each wall as a picture; avoid dead centre for everything.

**Scale versus proportion.** *Proportion* is the relationship of parts to each other; *scale* is the relationship of the object to the human body. A room can be perfectly proportioned and badly scaled — this is what makes hotel lobbies feel wrong.

## 3. Colour theory and colour systems

### The systems, and what each is actually for

| System | Structure | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| **Munsell** | Hue / Value / Chroma — e.g. `5R 4/14`. Perceptually spaced. | Teaching, soil and materials science, describing colour relationships precisely |
| **NCS (Natural Colour System)** | Notation like `S 2010-Y30R`: blackness, chromaticness, hue between the six elementary colours. Scandinavian/European standard. | Architectural specification in Europe; the best system for *describing* a paint colour unambiguously to any manufacturer |
| **RAL** | Discrete numbered catalogue (RAL Classic e.g. `RAL 9010`; RAL Design; RAL Effect). German industrial standard. | Powder coating, metalwork, joinery spray finishes, ironmongery |
| **Pantone (PMS)** | Proprietary spot-colour library; also TPX/TCX for textiles. | Print, branding, textile matching — **not** ideal for architectural paint |
| **Manufacturer libraries** | Farrow & Ball, Dulux, Plascon **[ZA]**, Little Greene, Benjamin Moore | Actual specification, because the client will buy a tin |

**Practical rule.** Specify paint by **manufacturer name + code**, and record the **NCS or RAL equivalent** as a fallback so the colour survives if the product is discontinued or unavailable in-country. **[ZA] [NA]** This matters enormously: imported paint ranges are often unavailable, and a local supplier can colour-match from an NCS or RAL reference far more reliably than from a swatch name.

### Working colour theory
- **Hue, value, chroma** are the three variables. Amateurs adjust hue; professionals adjust **value** (lightness) and **chroma** (saturation). Most failed schemes have correct hues at wrong values.
- **The 60-30-10 distribution** — dominant / secondary / accent — is a useful crutch, not a law.
- **Undertone is everything.** Every "white" has an undertone (yellow, pink, green, blue, grey). Whites with different undertones next to each other read as dirty. Test whites against a pure white card.
- **Colour is a function of light.** North light (southern hemisphere: south light) is cool and steady; it kills warm greys. Low sun makes everything warmer. Always test samples in the actual room at three times of day, painted on large boards, moved to different walls.
- **Metamerism** — two colours matching under one light source and not another — is why you must approve finishes under the project's actual lamps.
- **Chromatic adaptation.** Spend ten minutes in a red room and you stop seeing the red. Judge colour on entering, not after sitting.
- **Sheen changes colour.** The same pigment in matt, eggshell and gloss reads as three colours. Specify the sheen level explicitly.

### Colour psychology, honestly
Popular colour psychology ("blue calms, red stimulates, yellow makes you happy") is **weakly supported and heavily culture-dependent**. What is better supported:
- **Brightness and contrast** affect arousal and perceived spaciousness more reliably than hue does.
- **Colour temperature of light** affects alertness through the melanopic pathway (see §4) — this is a *light* effect, not a *pigment* effect, and is routinely confused with colour psychology.
- **Colour meaning is cultural.** White is mourning in parts of Asia; red is auspicious in China; in Southern African contexts colour carries specific ethnic and regional associations that a foreign designer should ask about rather than assume.
- **Preference is individual and stable.** Ask the client; do not diagnose them.
Treat colour psychology as a vocabulary for discussing intent with a client, not as a science you can cite.

## 4. Lighting design fundamentals

### Illuminance (lux) by task — conventional design values

| Space / task | Maintained illuminance (lux) |
|---|---|
| Circulation, corridors | 100–150 |
| Living room general | 100–200 |
| Living room reading | 300–500 at the task |
| Dining table | 150–300 (low, warm, dimmable) |
| Kitchen general | 300 |
| Kitchen worktop | 500 |
| Bathroom general | 150–200 |
| Bathroom mirror/grooming | 300–500, **lit from both sides at face height** |
| Bedroom general | 100–150 |
| Bedside reading | 300 at the book |
| Home office / general office | **500** at the desk |
| Detailed drawing/technical work | 750–1000 |
| Retail general | 300–750; feature/display 1000–3000 |
| Restaurant dining | 50–150, plus accent |
| Stairs | 100–150, with the tread nosings visible |
> Workplace values are statutory or standardised in most jurisdictions (EN 12464-1 in Europe; SANS equivalents **[ZA]**). `needs-verification`

### Colour temperature (CCT)

| CCT | Character | Use |
|---|---|---|
| 1800–2200 K | Candle / amber | Decorative, restaurants, evening |
| 2400–2700 K | Warm, domestic | Residential standard, hospitality |
| 3000 K | Warm white | Kitchens, bathrooms, retail warm |
| 3500 K | Neutral | Workplace, some retail |
| 4000 K | Cool white | Offices, clinical, task |
| 5000–6500 K | Daylight | Medical, industrial, colour-matching |

**Rule:** one CCT per space, and generally one per dwelling. Mixed CCT is the single most visible amateur lighting error. Warm-dim LEDs (which shift from 2700 K to ~1800 K as they dim, mimicking incandescent) are worth the premium in residential work.

### Colour rendering
- **CRI (Ra)** — 0–100. **Ra ≥ 90 minimum for residential and retail; Ra ≥ 95 for art, textiles and anywhere skin tone matters.** Ra 80 is acceptable only in back-of-house.
- **R9 (deep red)** is not included in Ra's headline average and is the value that determines whether skin, wood and red fabric look alive. **Ask for R9 ≥ 50.**
- **TM-30 (Rf/Rg)** is the better modern metric — fidelity and gamut — and serious lighting manufacturers publish it.

### Layering
Five layers, and a good scheme has at least three:
1. **Ambient** — general fill. Often the least important; frequently over-specified.
2. **Task** — at the work plane, controllable separately.
3. **Accent** — art, objects, texture. Typically **3:1 to 5:1** ratio against ambient for a visible highlight; 10:1 for drama.
4. **Decorative** — the fittings you see: pendants, table lamps, wall lights. These carry the room's character.
5. **Night/low-level** — under-cabinet, step, plinth. 1–10 lux for moving through a house at 3 a.m.

### Beam angles
- **Narrow spot** 10–15° — art, sculpture, tall rooms.
- **Spot** 24° — objects, feature walls.
- **Flood** 36–40° — general downlighting, worktops.
- **Wide flood** 60°+ — wall washing, low ceilings.
**Rule of thumb:** beam diameter ≈ 2 × distance × tan(angle/2). A 24° beam at 2.4 m throws roughly a 1.0 m pool.

### Glare, position and control
- **Unified Glare Rating (UGR)** ≤ 19 for offices, ≤ 22 general. Deep-recessed, baffled or honeycomb-louvred fittings control it.
- Downlights **should not sit in a grid** in residential work. Position them to do a job — wash a wall, light a worktop, graze a texture — typically 600–900 mm off the wall for wall-washing.
- **Never put a downlight directly above where a head will be.** It creates eye-socket shadows.
- **Dimming:** specify the protocol — **TRIAC/leading edge**, **trailing edge**, **0–10 V**, **DALI**, **DALI-2**, **Casambi/Bluetooth**, or a proprietary system (Lutron, Rako, Control4). Confirm driver-to-dimmer compatibility **in writing**; incompatible LED dimming (flicker, drop-out, buzz) is the most common lighting defect on site.
- **Scenes:** four to six per room maximum — e.g. Morning, Day, Evening, Dinner, Night, Off. More than six and nobody uses any of them.

### Circadian considerations
Melanopic (ipRGC) response peaks around **480 nm** blue. Practical, defensible design moves:
- High CCT and high illuminance in the morning; low CCT and low illuminance after sunset.
- Keep bedrooms warm and dim in the evening; use ≤ 2200 K for night circulation.
- Maximise daylight access and views — the largest circadian effect available and far larger than any luminaire.
- Be sceptical of "circadian lighting" product claims; the evidence supports daylight and evening dimming much better than it supports proprietary tunable systems. `needs-verification`

## 5. Acoustics in interiors

- **Reverberation time (RT60)** — the time for sound to decay by 60 dB. Targets: living rooms **0.4–0.6 s**; open-plan offices **0.5–0.8 s**; restaurants **0.6–0.9 s** (below 1.0 s or conversation fails); classrooms **0.4–0.6 s**; music rooms longer by design.
- **Sabine's formula:** RT60 = 0.161 × V / A, where V is volume in m³ and A is total absorption in m² sabins (Σ surface area × absorption coefficient).
- **Absorption coefficient (α)** runs 0 (perfect reflector) to 1 (perfect absorber), frequency-dependent. Indicative mid-frequency values: painted plaster/concrete **0.02–0.05**; glass **0.03–0.05**; timber floor **0.06–0.10**; heavy curtain in deep folds **0.5–0.7**; thick wool carpet on underlay **0.4–0.6**; upholstered seating **0.6–0.8**; 50 mm mineral-wool acoustic panel **0.85–1.0**; acoustic ceiling tile **0.6–0.9**. `needs-verification` — use the manufacturer's tested figures for anything you are calculating with.
- **NRC (Noise Reduction Coefficient)** — the arithmetic average of α at 250, 500, 1000 and 2000 Hz, rounded to the nearest 0.05. A single number for comparing absorbers; useless for low-frequency problems.
- **Where absorption actually goes:** the ceiling first (largest uninterrupted surface), then soft floor, then curtains and upholstery, then wall panels at head height on the first-reflection surfaces.
- **Sound isolation is a different problem.** Absorption fixes reverberance *within* a room; isolation (mass, decoupling, damping, sealing) stops sound moving *between* rooms. Rated by **Rw / STC** for airborne and **Ln,w / IIC** for impact. A rug does not fix a party wall.
- **Practical interventions in order of cost-effectiveness:** heavy interlined curtains; a large wool rug with underlay; upholstered rather than hard seating; a bookcase full of books; an acoustic-plaster or fabric-wrapped ceiling raft; door seals and drop-seals; a second layer of plasterboard on resilient bars.

## 6. Materials — tactile and maintenance properties

| Material | Feel / character | Maintenance reality |
|---|---|---|
| Solid oak, oiled | Warm, open grain, ages well | Re-oil 1–2 yearly; marks but repairs by sanding |
| Solid oak, lacquered | Harder, flatter, less tactile | Cannot be spot-repaired; whole-surface refinish |
| Walnut | Dark, fine, luxurious | Fades in UV — keep out of direct sun |
| MDF sprayed | Perfectly flat, any colour | Chips at edges; swells if wet; not for wet areas |
| Veneered board | Real timber look at panel scale | Cannot be sanded more than once; watch edge detail |
| Marble | Cool, luminous, veined | **Etches with acid** (lemon, wine, cleaning products); stains; patinates. Honed hides more than polished |
| Granite / quartzite | Hard, cool | Very durable; can look inert |
| Engineered quartz | Consistent, non-porous | Durable but scorches and shows heat damage permanently |
| Porcelain / sintered stone | Hardest practical worktop | Near-indestructible; edges are the weak point |
| Concrete / microcement | Monolithic, soft-textured | Hairline cracks are inherent; needs sealing; re-seal 2–5 yearly |
| Lime plaster / tadelakt | Alive, mottled, breathable | Tadelakt is water-resistant when soaped; repairs blend well |
| Unlacquered brass | Warm, mirror to matt | Patinates to brown-gold; the point |
| Stainless steel | Neutral, hygienic | Shows fingerprints (brushed less than polished) |
| Blackened/waxed steel | Matt, industrial, soft-black | Rusts in humidity unless waxed/lacquered |
| Natural leather | Improves with use | Patinates; dries out — condition annually |
| Wool carpet | Warm, resilient, quiet | Naturally soil- and flame-resistant; good recovery |
| Sisal / seagrass | Textural, natural | **Stains permanently with water** — never in bathrooms or with pets |
| Terrazzo | Graphic, hard-wearing | Seal; cement-based versions are porous |
| Rattan / cane | Light, airy, handmade | Dries and cracks in low humidity; not for hot dry climates without care **[NA]** |

**[NA] [ZA] Hot-dry-climate notes.** Timber moves: specify quarter-sawn, acclimatise on site, and design joinery with generous expansion allowances. Solid timber panels and cane will crack in Namibian interior humidity unless humidified or detailed for movement. UV is brutal — specify high-lightfastness fabrics and keep walnut, silk and untreated timber out of direct sun. Thermal mass, deep reveals, external shading and light-coloured surfaces do more for comfort than any interior treatment.

## 7. Textiles

### Weave and construction types
- **Plain weave** — simplest, one over one. Includes cotton duck, linen, canvas, taffeta, chintz (glazed plain-weave cotton).
- **Twill** — diagonal rib. Denim, herringbone, houndstooth. Durable, drapes well.
- **Satin/sateen** — long floats, lustrous, snags easily.
- **Jacquard** — pattern woven in on a jacquard loom: damask (one warp/weft, reversible), brocade (supplementary weft, not reversible), matelassé (quilted appearance).
- **Pile** — velvet (cut warp pile), mohair velvet (extremely durable), corduroy, chenille, bouclé (looped novelty yarn).
- **Dobby** — small geometric woven figures.
- **Crypton / performance weaves** — engineered stain- and moisture-barrier constructions for contract use.

### Fibre performance

| Fibre | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| Wool | Resilient, naturally flame-resistant, soil-shedding, dyes richly | Moths; can pill; costly |
| Mohair | Exceptionally durable, lustrous | Expensive |
| Linen | Strong, cool, superb texture and drape | Creases; low abrasion resistance; stretches |
| Cotton | Versatile, breathable, takes print | Fades in UV; moderate abrasion; shrinks |
| Silk | Unmatched lustre and drape | Weak in sunlight and water; almost never for upholstery |
| Viscose/rayon | Silk-like, cheap | Weak wet; poor abrasion; watermarks |
| Polyester | Strong, colourfast, cheap, easy-clean | Pills; feels synthetic unless well engineered |
| Nylon/polyamide | Highest abrasion resistance | Fades; static |
| Acrylic | Good UV resistance — outdoor use | Pills |
| Solution-dyed acrylic (e.g. outdoor grades) | Excellent lightfastness and cleanability | Limited hand |
| Leather | Durable, ages beautifully | Scratches; temperature-sensitive; needs hide-yield calculation |

### Durability and safety ratings
- **Martindale (EN ISO 12947)** — rub cycles to failure. Indicative bands: **≥ 10,000–15,000** decorative/occasional; **≥ 20,000–25,000** general domestic; **≥ 30,000** heavy domestic; **≥ 40,000** general contract; **≥ 50,000+** heavy contract/hospitality. `needs-verification` against the current standard — banding conventions differ by market.
- **Wyzenbeek (ASTM D4157)** — the US double-rub equivalent. **15,000 double rubs** heavy duty is a common contract threshold. Martindale and Wyzenbeek results **do not convert** to each other; never claim an equivalence.
- **Lightfastness (ISO 105-B02)** — scale 1–8. Specify **5+** for anything near a window; **6–7** for hot, high-UV climates **[NA] [ZA]**.
- **Pilling (ISO 12945)** — 1–5, higher is better.
- **Fire ratings** — **UK**: BS 5852 (match/cigarette source) and the Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations for domestic upholstery; BS 5867 for curtains in contract use. **US**: CAL 117-2013; NFPA 260/261; NFPA 701 for drapery. **EU**: EN 1021-1/-2. **[ZA]** SANS equivalents apply for public buildings. **All fire-rating references above are `needs-verification` and must be confirmed against the current standard and jurisdiction before specification.**
> ⚠️ Fire performance in contract, hospitality, healthcare and public work is a legal requirement, not a preference. Get a written certificate from the mill for every fabric, and confirm whether it is inherently flame-retardant or FR-treated (treatments can wash out).

**Fabric quantity rules of thumb.** Standard upholstery width 137–140 cm. A two-seat sofa: 12–16 m plain; add 25–50% for a pattern repeat and always calculate the actual repeat. Full-length curtains: fullness factor 2.0–2.5× for pinch pleat, 2.5–3.0× for pencil pleat or gathered, 1.15–1.3× for wave/ripplefold; plus hems (usually 150–200 mm bottom double hem, 100–150 mm heading) and the pattern repeat per drop.

## 8. Window treatments

| Type | Construction | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Pinch pleat (French/triple)** | Buckram-stiffened heading, hand-pleated | Formal; 2.0–2.5× fullness; hooks into track or rings on pole |
| **Goblet pleat** | Padded cylindrical pleats | Very formal, tall rooms |
| **Pencil pleat** | Gathered tape | Domestic, forgiving; 2.5× |
| **Eyelet/grommet** | Metal rings on a pole | Casual; deep even waves; poor light-block at the top |
| **Wave / ripplefold** | Cord-carrier track, continuous S-curve | Contemporary standard; 1.15–1.3× fullness; needs the right track |
| **Tab top** | Fabric loops | Casual, informal |
| **Roman blind** | Fabric panel with horizontal rod pockets, cords through rings, stacks in folds | Tailored; battens at 200–300 mm centres; needs a headrail; interlining transforms it |
| **Roller blind** | Single roll of fabric | Cheapest; blackout or solar-screen fabrics (specify openness factor, 1–10%) |
| **Venetian / timber blind** | Tilting slats | Controls direction of light, not just quantity |
| **Shutters (plantation)** | Hinged louvred panels | Best light and privacy control; expensive; permanent |
| **Sheers / voiles** | Light-filtering layer | Nearly always needed as the second layer; use 3× fullness |

**Linings.** *Unlined* — light and casual, no protection. *Lined* (cotton sateen) — standard; improves drape and protects from UV. *Blackout lining* — 3-pass coated; note that pinholes at needle penetrations always let some light through. *Interlined* (a soft bump/domette layer between face fabric and lining) — the single biggest upgrade available in curtain making: it gives weight, insulation, acoustic absorption and a luxurious drape, and roughly doubles the cost.

**Measuring.** Measure the track/pole, not the window. Standard drops: to sill (finish 10 mm above), below sill (finish 150 mm below), or to floor (finish 10–15 mm above finished floor, or "breaking" 20–40 mm on the floor for a soft look). Measure at three points across the width — floors are never level. Track height: as high as possible, ideally to the cornice, to gain apparent ceiling height; stack-back beyond the reveal so the curtain does not block glass.

## 9. Floor coverings

- **Solid timber** — 15–22 mm; needs stable humidity; sandable many times. Board width over 180 mm increases cupping risk.
- **Engineered timber** — plywood core with 3–6 mm wear layer; stable, suitable over underfloor heating; sandable once or twice.
- **Parquet patterns** — herringbone (single or double), chevron (mitred, more formal), Versailles/Chantilly panels, basketweave. Setting-out is critical: start from the room's centre-line, dry-lay first.
- **Stone and tile** — specify slip resistance (**R-rating** R9–R13, or PTV/pendulum). Wet-area floors need R11+ or a small-format tile with more grout. Confirm falls to gullies.
- **Carpet** — construction: cut pile (plush, saxony, twist/frisé), loop pile (level loop, berber), cut-and-loop. Weight (g/m²) and density matter more than fibre for wear. Wool/nylon 80/20 is the durable contract classic.
- **Rugs** — hand-knotted (knots per square inch, wool or silk), hand-tufted (glued backing, shorter life), flatweave/kilim/dhurrie, hand-loomed, machine-made.
- **Resilient** — vinyl (LVT, sheet), linoleum (linseed, genuinely bio-based), rubber, cork.
- **Poured** — microcement, resin, terrazzo.
- **[ZA] [NA]** Polished concrete and screed are cheap, locally achievable, thermally useful and forgiving in dusty conditions. Sisal is common and inexpensive but stains and is unsuitable in wet or high-traffic-with-spills areas.

## 10. Wall finishes

Paint (matt/flat, eggshell, satin, semi-gloss, gloss — increasing sheen means increasing washability and increasing revelation of surface defects); limewash and clay paint (mineral, matt, breathable, mottled); Venetian plaster/marmorino (polished lime, deep and reflective); tadelakt (waterproofed lime, seamless, for wet areas); microcement; wallpaper (machine print, hand-blocked, grasscloth, silk, vinyl for contract); fabric-on-wall (battened and stapled, or paperbacked — excellent acoustically); timber panelling (shaker, raised-and-fielded, board-and-batten, slatted acoustic); tile; and mirror. See file `09` for how each is actually applied.

**Surface preparation determines the result more than the product does.** For a flat sheen on a wall, the plaster must be flawless — matt paint hides nothing under raking light.

## 11. Scale rules for art and rugs

**Art.**
- Hang the **centre of the picture at 1450–1550 mm** above finished floor (eye level for a standing adult; use 1450 mm as the default and be consistent throughout a house).
- Over furniture, hang so the **bottom edge is 150–250 mm above** the sofa back or console top.
- A picture over a sofa should be roughly **two-thirds to three-quarters the width of the sofa**. Smaller reads as an accident.
- A **gallery wall** should be composed as a single rectangle with consistent gaps of **50–75 mm**; lay it out on the floor or on paper templates first, and hang the anchor piece at 1450 mm centre.
- Lighting: picture lights or narrow-beam spots at roughly **30° from vertical** to avoid reflections and canvas shadows.

**Rugs.**
- **Living room, all legs on** — the ideal: at least the front and back legs of every seating piece on the rug, with 300–450 mm of rug visible beyond.
- **Living room, front legs on** — the workable compromise; the rug must still reach under all the primary seating.
- **Never a small rug floating in the middle.** The commonest domestic error. A rug that touches nothing shrinks the room.
- **Dining** — the rug must extend **600–750 mm beyond the table on every side** so chairs stay on it when pulled out.
- **Bedroom** — either a rug extending **600–750 mm beyond the bed** on three sides, or runners at each side (700–900 mm wide).
- **Perimeter of floor visible** — aim for **200–450 mm** of exposed floor around a rug in a room; less looks like failed fitted carpet.
- Common sizes: 1700×2400, 2000×3000, 2400×3000, 2500×3500, 3000×4000 mm.
