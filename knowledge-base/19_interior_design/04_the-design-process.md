# The interior design process end to end

**Summary.** A professional interior project runs through seven gates: **brief and feasibility → concept → developed design → technical documentation → procurement → construction and installation → handover**. Each gate ends with a client sign-off and a payment. The commonest cause of project failure is not bad design; it is a gate crossed without sign-off, most often the leap from concept to ordering. This file sets out each stage, what is actually produced, a realistic programme, and the fee models — including an honest treatment of the ethics of each.

## Key facts

| Item | Typical value |
|---|---|
| Stages | 7 (feasibility, concept, developed design, technical, procurement, construction/install, handover) |
| Typical residential programme, whole-house renovation | 12–24 months from brief to handover |
| Typical restaurant/bar fit-out | 6–12 months |
| Design phase as share of total programme | Roughly 35–45% |
| Upholstery/bespoke furniture lead time | 8–16 weeks, often longer for imported |
| Stone and bespoke joinery lead time | 6–14 weeks after template/site measure |
| Imported goods to Southern Africa **[ZA] [NA]** | Add **8–16 weeks** sea freight plus clearing; air freight 1–3 weeks at 4–8× cost |
| Common design fee, percentage basis | 10–20% of construction + FF&E cost |
| Common procurement margin | 15–35% on trade price, or the full trade discount retained |
| Contingency to carry | 10% design contingency + 10% construction contingency, minimum |
| Snagging period | 2–4 weeks post-practical-completion; defects liability commonly 6–12 months |

> ⚠️ Never issue a purchase order before the client has signed the scheme *and* paid the deposit that funds it. Designers who pre-order to save programme routinely end up personally liable for goods a client has changed their mind about.

## Stage 0 — Qualification, before the brief

Before spending unpaid hours: establish that the client has a **budget**, a **decision-maker**, and a **timescale**. A useful screening triad —
1. "What number would make you say no to this project?" (budget, obtained without asking for a budget)
2. "Who else has to be happy with the result?" (finds the hidden second client)
3. "What is driving the date?" (a wedding, a birth, a lease expiry, a sale — this determines everything)

Then a paid **feasibility or discovery** stage, small and fixed-fee, so nobody works for free and both sides can walk away cheaply.

## Stage 1 — Brief and discovery

**The discovery interview.** Two hours minimum, on site, with everyone who lives or works there. Structure it around *life*, not style:
- **Daily pattern.** What time do you get up; where do you eat breakfast; where does the post land; where do shoes come off; who cooks; do you eat at a table; where does the television actually get watched?
- **Hosting.** How many, how often, seated or standing, do guests stay?
- **Possessions.** What must stay? Photograph and measure every inherited or loved object at this visit — the grandmother's chest determines a wall length later.
- **Irritations.** "What annoys you every single day in this house?" This produces more useful design information than any question about taste.
- **Precedent.** Ask for images they love *and* images they hate. The hate list is more diagnostic; it reveals the boundary.
- **Money and time.** Stated plainly, in writing, with a contingency named.

**Survey and existing-condition record.** Measure everything yourself or commission a measured survey. Record ceiling heights at multiple points, floor levels, window reveal depths, radiator and socket positions, structural walls, service runs, boiler and consumer-unit locations, and the direction and quality of daylight through the day. Photograph every wall.

**Deliverables:** written brief, existing plans and elevations, photographic record, a schedule of accommodation, a budget framework, a programme, and a signed appointment.

**Duration:** 2–5 weeks. **Fee:** fixed, typically 3–8% of the total design fee.

## Stage 2 — Concept

This is where the project's idea is set. Working outputs:
- **Concept statement** — one paragraph and, ideally, one sentence. If it cannot be said in a sentence it will not survive value engineering.
- **Mood boards** — not Pinterest collages. A working mood board carries **material samples at real scale**, a colour position, a light quality, and two or three precedent images, and it should imply a budget level. Make one per room or per zone, plus one for the whole project.
- **Sketch plans** — two or three genuinely different spatial options, not one option and two straw men.
- **Indicative budget** — allocated by room and by element, so the client sees where the money goes before falling in love with a scheme they cannot afford.

**Duration:** 3–6 weeks. **Gate:** client signs one spatial option and the concept direction.

## Stage 3 — Developed design (spatial planning and detail)

**Spatial planning and circulation.** The plan is settled here: partitions, openings, levels, ceiling zones, wet areas, joinery envelopes. Test it against real dimensions (file `06`): 900 mm minimum general circulation, 1200 mm for a main route, 1000 mm clear in front of a wardrobe, 1050–1200 mm between kitchen runs, 750 mm dining chair pull-back plus circulation behind. Walk the plan mentally at 6 a.m., at dinner, and with a suitcase.

**Furniture layouts to scale.** Every piece drawn at its actual dimensions, never as a symbol. This is where most amateur schemes fail: sofas are always bigger than people imagine and rugs are always smaller.

**Preliminary lighting design, materials palette, joinery concept, colour scheme.** Full-size samples where possible; large paint samples on board, moved around the room and viewed at three times of day.

**Duration:** 4–8 weeks. **Gate:** frozen plan. After this gate, plan changes cost money and time and must be issued as variations.

## Stage 4 — Technical documentation

The package a contractor can build from without asking questions. A competent set contains:

| Drawing | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Demolition plan** | What comes out, marked and hatched, with structural notes |
| **Setting-out plan** | New walls dimensioned from a fixed datum, not from each other — cumulative error is the enemy |
| **Floor finishes plan** | Material, direction of lay, threshold details, movement joints, setting-out point for tiling |
| **Reflected ceiling plan (RCP)** | Ceiling levels, bulkheads, light fitting positions and types, sprinklers, detectors, grilles, speakers, curtain tracks and pelmets — coordinated so nothing collides |
| **Electrical and small-power plan** | Sockets, data, TV, switching, dimming zones, with heights dimensioned |
| **Lighting layout and control schedule** | Circuit by circuit, scene by scene, with dimming protocol named |
| **Internal elevations** | Every wall of every room that has anything on it |
| **Joinery drawings** | Plan, elevation, section at 1:20 and details at 1:5 or 1:1; material, edge treatment, ironmongery, shadow gaps, tolerances |
| **Wet-area details** | Falls, waterproofing, tanking laps, tile setting-out, niche dimensions |
| **Finishes schedule** | Room by room, surface by surface: product, code, supplier, finish, quantity, application note |
| **Door and ironmongery schedule** | Size, type, fire rating, handing, hinges, locks, closers, handles |
| **Window treatment schedule** | Type, fabric, heading, lining, track, fixing, control side, dimensions from a re-measure |

**Coordination is the actual work.** The RCP is where the project is really designed: lights vs sprinklers vs beams vs curtain tracks vs ventilation. Do it before the ceiling is built, not after.

**Duration:** 6–12 weeks for a house; longer for hospitality. **Gate:** tender issue.

## Stage 5 — FF&E specification and procurement

**FF&E** = furniture, fixtures/fittings and equipment. Two sub-classes matter contractually: **FF&E** (loose, supplied by the designer or client) and **fitted/contractor-supplied** items (built in, in the building contract). Confuse them and something arrives twice or never.

**The specification.** Every item gets a code, a room, a product name, supplier, dimensions, finish, fabric with a rub-count and fire rating, lead time, unit price, quantity, and an image. Maintain it as one live schedule — this becomes the purchase-order log, the delivery checklist and the handover manual.

**Sourcing and lead times.** Order in lead-time sequence, not room sequence. Long-lead items first: bespoke joinery, stone, upholstery, bespoke rugs, imported lighting, sanitaryware from small European makers. Confirm lead times **in writing at order**, and again at 50% of elapsed lead time — suppliers slip quietly.

**[ZA] [NA] Southern African reality.** Sea freight from Europe or Asia adds 8–16 weeks plus clearing, duty and VAT; port congestion and customs holds routinely add more. Air freight costs 4–8× and is only rational for small, high-value, programme-critical items. Local making — upholstery, joinery, metalwork, ceramics, weaving — is frequently faster, cheaper and better than importing, and is the right default. Build the schedule around what can be made within a day's drive.

**Duration:** procurement overlaps construction and runs 12–30 weeks.

## Stage 6 — Construction, installation and styling

**Contractor and joiner coordination.** Weekly site meetings with a written record and a live action list. Attend the **setting-out** of anything tiled, and the **template** visit for stone. Approve joinery in the workshop before it is finished and delivered. Request **mock-ups** for anything you cannot fully imagine: a paint panel, a tile setting-out board, a joinery corner, a lighting mock-up at night.

**Sequence in the last six weeks** (this order matters):
1. Second-fix electrical and plumbing
2. Decoration — final coats
3. Floor finishes and protection
4. Joinery installation and scribing
5. Sanitaryware, ironmongery, light fittings
6. Deep clean
7. **Installation day** — carpets and rugs, then furniture, then curtains and blinds, then lighting fittings if not already, then art hanging
8. **Styling day** — books, objects, textiles, plants, flowers

**Installation day** is a logistics exercise: a delivery schedule by hour, a route into the building, protection down, a crew, tools, a steamer for curtains, touch-up paint, picture-hanging kit, and a plan for packaging removal. Never let the client arrive mid-install.

**Styling** is the last 5% that carries 50% of the perceived quality. Layer in odd numbers, vary heights, let some surfaces stay empty, and bring something living into every room.

## Stage 7 — Snagging, photography, handover

**Snagging.** Walk the job with the contractor and a fresh pair of eyes, at both daylight and night lighting. Record every defect with a photo, a location and a responsible party. Typical snag list on a house: 80–200 items. Agree a completion date for the list; hold retention until it is cleared. Defects liability commonly runs 6–12 months.

**Photography.** Shoot after snagging and styling, before the client moves fully in. Use a specialist interiors photographer. Agree in writing: usage rights for you, the photographer's credit, embargo, and who may publish first. Shoot plans and details as well as hero views — awards submissions need both (file `03`).

**Handover.** Deliver an **operations and maintenance file**: as-built drawings, finishes schedule with product codes and touch-up paint, fabric care instructions, appliance manuals, warranty documents, lighting control programming notes and scene descriptions, supplier and trade contact list, and a maintenance calendar. This document is also the single best generator of repeat work.

## A realistic programme — full house renovation

| Stage | Duration | Cumulative |
|---|---|---|
| Feasibility and appointment | 2–4 weeks | 4 |
| Brief, survey, discovery | 2–4 weeks | 8 |
| Concept | 3–6 weeks | 14 |
| Developed design | 4–8 weeks | 22 |
| Technical documentation | 6–12 weeks | 34 |
| Statutory approvals (if needed) | 6–16 weeks, often parallel | 34–44 |
| Tender and contractor appointment | 4–8 weeks | 42–52 |
| Construction | 20–40 weeks | 62–92 |
| FF&E procurement | 12–30 weeks (parallel) | — |
| Installation and styling | 1–3 weeks | 65–95 |
| Snagging, photography, handover | 3–6 weeks | **68–101 weeks (16–24 months)** |

Compress this and something breaks — usually joinery quality or the FF&E, because they are last. A single room refresh runs 8–16 weeks; a kitchen 16–28; a restaurant fit-out 26–52.

## Fee models, and the ethics of each

### 1. Percentage of cost
**10–20% of construction plus FF&E**, sometimes tiered (higher percentage on smaller budgets). Simple, scales with scope, and is the default in much of Europe and Southern Africa.
**Ethics:** the structural problem is obvious — the designer earns more when the project costs more. Mitigate by agreeing the percentage against a **budget cap** set at the outset, and by capping the fee once the budget is exceeded for reasons within your control. Disclose the conflict plainly rather than pretending it does not exist.

### 2. Hourly
**Rate bands by seniority** (principal / senior / designer / junior / admin), with a fee estimate and a not-to-exceed for each stage. The most transparent model and the fairest for exploratory work.
**Ethics:** clean, but requires honest timesheets and disciplined reporting. Bill monthly with a stage-by-stage burn-down. The risk is client anxiety — cap each stage.

### 3. Flat design fee
A fixed sum for a defined scope and a defined number of revisions. Best for clients who need certainty.
**Ethics:** clean, provided the scope and the revision count are written down. The failure mode is scope creep absorbed silently until the designer resents the client. Write a variation procedure and use it from the first change.

### 4. Cost-plus / procurement margin
The designer buys at **trade price** and sells to the client at trade plus a stated margin (commonly **15–35%**), or retains the trade discount as the fee.
**Ethics:** this is the model with the real problems, and it should be handled with explicit rules.
- **Disclose the model.** The client must know before signing whether you are buying on their behalf as an agent or reselling to them as a principal. These are different legal relationships with different duties.
- **Never take an undisclosed commission.** Accepting a supplier rebate the client does not know about is the single most damaging practice in the trade and breaches most professional codes of conduct, including the BIID's.
- **Never let the margin choose the product.** If a supplier pays you more, that is a reason for extra scrutiny, not for specification.
- **Never bill a percentage fee on top of a retained trade margin without saying so.** Double-dipping, disclosed, is a commercial decision; undisclosed, it is a fraud.
- **Handle client money properly.** Deposits for goods are the client's money until the goods ship. Where possible use a separate client account, and always issue a proper purchase order and a supplier invoice trail.

### 5. Hybrid — the practical default
Fixed fee for stages 1–4 (where the work is defined), hourly for construction-phase administration (where it is not), and a disclosed procurement margin on FF&E, with the client offered the alternative of buying direct at trade with a handling fee. Offering the alternative is what makes the margin honest.

### Payment structure
Typically: 10–20% on appointment, then a payment at each gate, with the final 5–10% held to handover. Bill in advance of each stage, not in arrears — it aligns the sign-off gate with the payment gate.
