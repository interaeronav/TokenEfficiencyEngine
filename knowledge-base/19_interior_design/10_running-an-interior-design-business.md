# Running an interior design business

**Summary.** Interior design is a service business with a product business bolted on, and most studios fail because they price the service like a product or the product like a service. This file covers positioning, portfolio, client acquisition, contracts and scope, the three real revenue streams (design fee, trade margin, licensing), trade accounts, purchase orders and deposits, insurance, the common disputes and how to avoid them, and how studios scale.

## Key facts

| Item | Typical value |
|---|---|
| Revenue streams | Design fee · procurement margin/trade discount · product royalties and licensing |
| Design fee, percentage basis | 10–20% of construction + FF&E |
| Procurement margin | 15–35% on trade, or the retained trade discount |
| Trade discount from suppliers | Commonly 20–50% off RRP, varies wildly by category |
| Typical deposit on FF&E | 50–100% of goods value at order |
| Utilisation target (billable hours) | 60–75% for designers; 30–50% for the principal |
| Studio headcount thresholds | 1 → 3 → 8 → 15 → 40; each needs a different structure |
| Peter Marino Architect | ~160 staff, three offices — the large-studio end point |
| Studio Ashby | Founded 2014; now principal + two partners |
| Professional indemnity | Essential; level set by contract value and jurisdiction `needs-verification` |

> ⚠️ **Undisclosed commission is the single most serious ethical failure in this business.** Taking a supplier rebate the client does not know about breaches professional codes of conduct (including the BIID's) and in many jurisdictions breaches an agent's fiduciary duty. Disclose the model in writing before appointment, every time.

## 1. Positioning and niche

The commonest strategic mistake is offering "interior design" to "anyone with a house". Positioning is what allows a fee to be above market.

**The three axes of positioning:**
1. **Sector.** Residential / hospitality / workplace / retail / healthcare / lodges and resorts / yacht and aviation. Sector determines everything downstream — technical requirements, sales cycle, fee model, awards and publications.
2. **Aesthetic.** A signature an editor can describe in one sentence. Dirand: strong lines, marble, monochrome. Mahdavi: colour. Vervoordt: patina. Hoppen: East-meets-West neutrals. Van Duysen: resolved restraint. A signature is a *constraint you accept in exchange for being findable*.
3. **Geography and client type.** Local high-end residential; international clients in one city; a specific diaspora or industry.

**Evidence that a niche is a commercial asset:** Kelly Wearstler's career pivoted on **one developer relationship** (Brad Korzen, 1996) that became the Avalon, Maison 140, Viceroy and Proper hotel groups. Peter Marino's became 17 Barneys stores from 1986–93 and then the entire luxury-fashion retail sector. Neither diversified early; both went deep.

**[NA] [ZA] regional positioning.** In a small market, the viable niches are: hospitality and lodge interiors (the region's strongest export-quality sector); high-end residential for a small, mostly-known client base; commercial and workplace fit-out; and craft-led product with international distribution (Mabeo in Botswana is the proof case). Positioning around **climate-appropriate, locally-made** work is defensible, differentiated and cheaper to deliver than an import-led model.

## 2. Portfolio and website

**The portfolio is the product.** Rules that hold across the field:
- **Ten projects beautifully photographed beats forty snapshots.** Show fewer, better.
- **Photograph everything**, including small jobs and single rooms. Budget a photoshoot per completed project as a fixed cost of doing business — it feeds the website, awards (file `03`), press and social simultaneously.
- **Show plans.** A plan next to a photograph signals professional competence and is what commercial clients look for.
- **Show details.** Close-ups of junctions, joinery and material meetings do more to win technical work than hero shots.
- **Credit everyone** — photographer, architect, contractor, joiner, maker. It is both correct and how you stay in other people's referral networks.
- **Website:** fast, image-led, mobile-first, with a clear statement of *what you do, for whom, and how you charge*. A contact form that asks for budget range and timescale pre-qualifies enquiries and saves hundreds of unpaid hours a year.
- **Rights:** get written usage rights from the photographer covering website, press, social and award entries, and written permission from the client to publish, agreed at appointment rather than at completion. Many high-net-worth clients will refuse — negotiate this **before** you take the job.

## 3. Client acquisition

Ranked by what actually produces work in this field:

1. **Referral from past clients.** The dominant channel in residential. Cultivate it deliberately: the handover file (file `04`), a follow-up at six months, and a genuinely useful relationship after the job.
2. **Referral from adjacent professionals.** Architects, estate agents, builders, private bankers, art advisers, property developers. Build five of these relationships properly rather than fifty superficially.
3. **Publication.** A published project generates enquiries for years. This is why awards and press matter commercially and not just for vanity (file `03`).
4. **Trade and supplier networks.** Showroom staff refer clients constantly. Be the designer they like dealing with.
5. **Social media.** Effective for reach and for product businesses; weak at generating high-value residential clients on its own. Bobby Berk and Nate Berkus built businesses on media reach — but both then monetised through **product and television**, not through design fees.
6. **Cold outreach and directories.** Low yield in residential; genuinely useful in commercial, where procurement is formalised and pre-qualification questionnaires exist.
7. **Speculative competition entries.** Only where the shortlist itself has value.

**Qualification discipline.** Every enquiry gets the three questions in file `04` §Stage 0 before any unpaid time is spent. A studio's profitability correlates more strongly with *what it declines* than with what it wins.

## 4. The contract and scope of services

**Never start without a signed appointment.** The minimum contents:

| Clause | Why |
|---|---|
| Parties, project address, and the definition of the property | Identifies who can instruct you |
| **Scope of services, stage by stage** | The single most important clause. Mirror the stages in file `04` |
| **Explicit exclusions** | Architecture, structural design, statutory applications, MEP design, cost consultancy, project management if not included — list them |
| Deliverables per stage | What the client actually receives |
| Fee basis, amounts, and the payment schedule | Tied to stage sign-offs |
| **Procurement model and margin disclosure** | Agent or principal; what margin, on what, disclosed |
| Additional services and variation procedure | Hourly rate, and how a change becomes an instruction |
| Expenses and disbursements | Travel, printing, samples, courier, mileage |
| Client obligations | Timely decisions, access, information, payment |
| Programme and the consequences of delay | Protects you from an indefinite project |
| Copyright and licence to use the design | You retain copyright; the client gets a licence on payment |
| **Publication and photography rights** | Negotiated up front |
| Confidentiality / NDA | Standard at the high end |
| Limitation of liability and PI insurance level | Cap liability at a defined sum |
| Suspension and termination, and fees on termination | The clause you will one day need |
| Dispute resolution and governing law | Mediation first, then arbitration or courts |

Use a professional body's standard appointment where one exists — the BIID publishes standard forms of agreement for UK use. `needs-verification` on current document names and availability.

## 5. Fee structures and how money is actually made

### Stream 1 — the design fee
The payment for thinking, drawing and coordinating. Models and their ethics are set out in file `04` §Fee models: **percentage of cost** (10–20%), **hourly** (rate bands by seniority, capped per stage), **flat fee** (defined scope, defined revisions), or a **hybrid** (fixed through documentation, hourly through construction).

**Costing a fixed fee honestly.** Estimate hours by stage and by grade; multiply by a **charge-out rate**, not a salary rate. A defensible charge-out rate is roughly:
`(annual salary + employer costs) ÷ billable hours per year × overhead multiplier`
with billable hours realistically **1,200–1,400 per person per year** (not 1,800) and an overhead multiplier of **2.5–3.5×**. Studios that use 1,800 billable hours and a 2× multiplier lose money on every fixed fee and do not know why.

### Stream 2 — procurement margin
The designer buys at trade and either resells at trade + margin or discloses the trade price and charges a procurement fee.
- **Trade discounts** vary enormously — commonly 20–50% off RRP for furniture and fabric, much less for appliances and sanitaryware, and effectively nil for some brands.
- **The margin must be disclosed.** Three honest structures: (a) resale at cost + stated margin; (b) client buys at trade and pays a stated procurement/handling fee; (c) designer retains the trade discount as the entire fee, disclosed and with no additional design fee.
- **Never let the margin choose the specification.** If a supplier's margin is unusually generous, apply *more* scrutiny to the product, not less.
- **Never double-dip undisclosed.** Charging a percentage design fee *and* silently retaining a trade margin on the same goods is the practice that generates the field's worst disputes and reputational damage.
- Procurement carries real work and real risk — sourcing, ordering, chasing, receiving, inspecting, storing, delivering, replacing damaged goods, and financing. A margin is legitimate payment for that. Say so, and price it as a service rather than hiding it.

### Stream 3 — product, licensing and media
Where mature studios make disproportionate money, because it scales without hours.
- **Own furniture, lighting, fabric or rug lines** — sold direct or through a manufacturer. **Pierre Yovanovitch** launched *Pierre Yovanovitch Mobilier* in 2021 and in March 2024 acquired the manufacturer d'Argentat and ECART International. **Kelly Wearstler**, **Kelly Hoppen** and **Bunny Williams** all run product lines.
- **Licensing and creative direction** — **Vincent Van Duysen** as creative director of Molteni&C; **Miles Redd** as creative director of Oscar de la Renta Home 2003–13; **India Mahdavi** after seven years as artistic director for Christian Liaigre.
- **Retail** — **Sister by Studio Ashby** (2021, flagship 2022); **Rose Uniacke's** three Pimlico Road shops; **Bobby Berk Home** from 2006.
- **Books, television and media** — Hoppen's thirteen books and *Dragons' Den*; Berkus's syndicated show; Bullard's *Million Dollar Decorators*; Berk's *Queer Eye*.
**The pattern:** the design practice builds the name; the name monetises through product and media. Almost no studio in file `02` got rich on design fees alone.

## 6. Trade accounts and supplier relationships

**Opening trade accounts.** Suppliers typically require a registered business, a VAT/tax number, a business address, proof of trading (a website, a portfolio), sometimes professional-body membership, and trade references. Terms are usually pro-forma at first, moving to 30 days after a trading history. Some brands require a showroom or a minimum annual spend.

**Managing suppliers.**
- Keep a **sample library** and return borrowed samples promptly — this is the main reason showrooms like or dislike a designer.
- Get **written lead times** at quotation and re-confirm at 50% of elapsed lead time.
- Read the **terms**: who owns the goods before delivery, who insures them in transit, what the returns and cancellation policy is (bespoke goods are almost never returnable), and what the tolerance is on colour and dimension.
- Build relationships with **makers** — a joiner, an upholsterer, a curtain workroom, a metalworker, a painter-decorator, a stone fabricator. A studio is only as good as its five best trades. **[ZA] [NA]** this is the decisive competitive advantage in the region: local making is faster and cheaper than importing.

**Purchase orders and deposits.**
- Issue a numbered **purchase order** for every item: PO number, project, item code, description, finish, quantity, price, delivery address, required date, and terms.
- Take a **client deposit before placing any order** — commonly 50–100% of goods value, plus the margin. Never fund a client's purchase from your own working capital.
- **Client money is client money.** Where possible hold FF&E deposits in a separate client account. Insolvency in interior design most often comes from spending client deposits on overheads.
- Track everything in one live FF&E schedule (file `04`) that doubles as the PO log and the delivery checklist.
- Arrange **receiving, inspection and storage** — a receiving warehouse for larger projects. Inspect on arrival; a damaged item discovered on installation day is a disaster, discovered in a warehouse it is an inconvenience.

## 7. Project management and studio operations

- **One live document per project**: the FF&E and PO schedule. Everything else derives from it.
- **Software.** Design: AutoCAD/Revit/ArchiCAD/Vectorworks, SketchUp, Adobe CC. Specification and procurement: Programa, Studio Designer, Ivy/Houzz Pro, DesignFiles, or a rigorously maintained spreadsheet. Project: Asana/Monday/Trello. Time: Harvest/Toggl — **track time even on fixed fees**, because it is the only way to learn what work actually costs.
- **Weekly rhythm:** a studio meeting on project status, a site meeting per active project, and a monthly financial review (WIP, invoiced, received, pipeline).
- **The three numbers to watch:** cash in the bank; work in progress not yet invoiced; and pipeline value weighted by probability. A profitable studio can still die of cash.

## 8. Insurance and legal

- **Professional indemnity (PI)** — covers negligent design advice. Essential. Claims-made, so maintain run-off cover after closing a practice.
- **Public liability** — injury or damage on site.
- **Employer's liability** — statutory where you employ people.
- **Goods in transit / stock** — if you take title to FF&E, you carry the risk.
- **Contents and equipment**, cyber, and business interruption.
- **Company structure:** limited company or its local equivalent, to separate personal and business liability. **[ZA]** a (Pty) Ltd; **[NA]** a (Pty) Ltd. Take local tax advice on VAT registration thresholds and on how procurement margin is treated for VAT — resale and agency have different VAT consequences and getting this wrong is expensive. `needs-verification`
- **Statutory compliance:** where the work is code-regulated, ensure a properly qualified person is the designer of record (file `00`).

## 9. The common disputes, and how to prevent each

| Dispute | Root cause | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| "This isn't what I approved" | Sign-off not documented | Signed drawings and schedules at each gate |
| "Why is it over budget?" | Budget set after the scheme, not before | Budget allocated by room at concept; a live cost tracker; 10 + 10% contingency |
| "You're making money off my sofa" | Undisclosed margin | Disclose the model in the contract, in writing, before appointment |
| "It's late" | Lead times not confirmed or communicated | Written lead times, re-confirmed; a programme the client can see |
| "The sofa doesn't fit" | Access not surveyed | Measure the delivery route — doors, stairs, lift, corners — before ordering |
| "The colour is wrong" | Approved from a small sample | Large samples, in the room, at three times of day, under the project's lamps |
| "The contractor says it can't be built" | Documentation gaps | A complete technical package; workshop drawings; mock-ups |
| "I'm not paying the final invoice" | Fee tied to satisfaction rather than to stages | Bill in advance per stage; hold only a small final retention |
| Scope creep | No variation procedure | Write it into the contract and use it from the first change |
| Non-payment | No credit control | Deposits, stage payments in advance, and a suspension clause you actually invoke |

**When a dispute happens:** stop, put everything in writing, re-read the contract, offer a meeting, and propose mediation before anything adversarial. Most interior design disputes are about **expectation management**, not about design.

## 10. Scaling from solo to studio

**Stage 1 — Solo (1 person).** You do everything. Revenue capped by your hours. **Priority:** charge properly, keep overheads near zero, build a maker network, get one project photographed. **Danger:** doing admin instead of design and never raising the rate.

**Stage 2 — Solo + support (2–3).** First hire is usually a **junior designer or a studio/procurement administrator** — and the administrator is almost always the higher-leverage hire, because procurement admin is what eats a principal's design time. **Priority:** documented processes, a specification system, timesheets. **Danger:** hiring a designer before there is enough work to keep them billable.

**Stage 3 — Small studio (4–8).** Roles separate: design, technical, FF&E/procurement, project administration. The principal is now half in business development. **Priority:** a second person who can front a client; a real financial model with charge-out rates and utilisation targets. **Danger:** the principal remaining the only salesperson and the only design authority — the classic ceiling.

**Stage 4 — Established studio (10–20).** Team leads, a studio manager, an in-house or retained finance function, and a real brand. **Studio Ashby's** structure is instructive: founded by Sophie Ashby in 2014 and now **led with two partners**, Fiona Blanchot and Nicola Koller — i.e. distributed design authority. **Priority:** a partner track, so senior people have a reason to stay. **Danger:** quality drift as the principal touches fewer projects.

**Stage 5 — Large practice (30+).** **Peter Marino Architect** reached ~160 people across three offices. At this scale the business is a professional services firm: sector teams, a business-development function, formal QA, contract administration, HR. **Priority:** systems and culture. **Danger:** becoming a delivery machine with no design position — the reason many large interiors firms are commercially successful and critically invisible.

**Two things that reliably raise studio value:** a **repeatable client type** (a developer, a hotel group, a corporate rollout) and a **product or licensing line** that earns while nobody is billing hours.

**And one honest caution.** Most of the celebrated names in file `02` deliberately stayed small for a long time, and several never grew at all. Growth is a choice, not a metric. Jean-Michel Frank, Sister Parish and Rose Uniacke built reputations of the first rank from very small operations. The question to answer before hiring is not "can I grow?" but "what do I want to be doing in five years, and does this hire move me toward it?"
