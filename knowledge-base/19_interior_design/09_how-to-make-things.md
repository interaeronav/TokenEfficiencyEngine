# How to make interior things — techniques, tools and references

**Summary.** An indexed guide to the practical making crafts that sit behind interior design: the core method, the essential tools, the standard reference books, and the realistic skill level for each. The argument for a designer learning to make is empirical: Syrie Maugham's apprenticeship taught her **furniture restoration, trompe-l'œil, curtain design and upholstery** before she opened a business, and Axel Vervoordt learned by restoring buildings. People who can make things specify better, brief makers more precisely, and cost work more accurately.

**Skill-level key:** **[1]** a weekend, **[2]** a few weeks of practice, **[3]** months, **[4]** years — commission a professional and learn enough to judge the result.

> ⚠️ Anything upholstered for a public, rented, hospitality or workplace interior must meet the **fire-safety regulations of its jurisdiction** (file `06`). Home-made upholstery is generally not compliant for contract use. Electrical work on lamps and lighting must be done or certified by a competent person.

## 1. Upholstery

### Traditional (sprung) upholstery — the full build sequence
1. **Frame.** Hardwood (beech, ash), mortise-and-tenon or dowelled, glued and screwed. Strip an old frame back and re-glue before anything else; a loose frame destroys new work.
2. **Webbing.** Jute or black-and-white English webbing, woven and interlaced, stretched with a **web stretcher** to high tension and tacked with 16 mm improved tacks, folded and double-tacked. Seat webbing goes on the **top** of the rail; back webbing on the inside face.
3. **Springs.** Double-cone hourglass springs (usually 9–12 gauge) stitched to the webbing at four points with a spring needle and twine, then **lashed** in eight directions with laid cord to set the crown and stop lateral movement.
4. **Hessian.** 12 oz hessian over the springs, tacked, then the springs stitched through to it.
5. **Bridle ties.** Loose twine loops across the hessian to hold the stuffing.
6. **First stuffing.** Coir fibre or horsehair, teased and packed under the bridles.
7. **Stuffing ties and stitched edge.** A scrim layer, blind-stitched then top-stitched with a **double-ended bayonet needle** to build the hard roll edge that gives a traditional seat its profile. This is the hardest single skill in the craft.
8. **Second stuffing.** Hair or wadding.
9. **Calico.** The under-cover that sets the final shape. **Everything is judged at calico stage** — if it is wrong here, the top cover cannot save it.
10. **Wadding.** Skin wadding or polyester to stop the hair working through.
11. **Top cover.** Cut on grain, pattern centred and matched, tensioned, tacked or stapled, corners pleated.
12. **Finishing.** Piping (a bias-cut strip over cord), double piping, gimp, braid, decorative nails at even centres, and the bottom cloth.

### Modern (foam) upholstery
Frame → webbing or a Pirelli rubber-webbing platform → foam (specify density in kg/m³ and hardness; **seat foam typically 35–40 kg/m³**, back foam softer) → polyester wrap or a feather-and-down wrap for a soft look → calico → top cover. Faster, cheaper, and the correct choice for most contemporary work; a feather wrap over foam is the standard high-end compromise.

**Buttoning.** Deep buttoning requires marking a diamond grid on both the foam/hair and the cover, allowing extra fabric for the pleat depth (typically **1.5× the flat dimension**), drilling or cutting button holes, and pulling buttons through with a long **buttoning needle** and twine tied off to a slip knot on the underside. Float buttoning is the shallow version.

**Essential tools.** Ripping chisel and mallet; magnetic tack hammer; web stretcher; upholstery staple gun (pneumatic or electric); regulator; spring needle; double-ended bayonet needle; circular needles; skewers; buttoning needle; laid cord and twine; upholstery scissors; staple remover.

**References.** The standard texts are David James's upholstery books (*Upholstery: A Complete Course*, *Upholstery Techniques Illustrated*), Alex Law and the classic **Wm. Frank Bast / Bill Tarrant** manuals, and *The Upholsterer's Step-by-Step Handbook*. **Titles, authors and editions `needs-verification`.**

**Skill:** modern foam work **[2]**; traditional sprung and stitched work **[4]**. Learn to do a drop-in dining seat first — it contains webbing, stuffing, calico and cover in two hours.

## 2. Curtain making

### Measuring
Measure the **track or pole**, not the window, and fit the track first. Width = track length; add returns (the bend back to the wall) and an overlap at the centre (typically 50–100 mm each). Drop = from the top of the heading (or the hook position) to the finish point. Take **three drop measurements** across the width. Finish points: 10 mm above sill; 150 mm below sill; 10–15 mm above finished floor; or "breaking" 20–40 mm onto the floor.

### Cutting and quantity
Number of widths = (track length × fullness factor + returns + overlap) ÷ usable fabric width. Round **up** to a whole width, then split between two curtains. Cut length = finished drop + heading allowance (100–150 mm) + hem allowance (150–200 mm for a double 100 mm hem) + one **pattern repeat** per drop. Always calculate the repeat; it is the commonest costing error.

### Headings
- **Pinch pleat (triple/French)** — buckram in the heading, pleats hand-formed and stab-stitched, 2.0–2.5× fullness.
- **Goblet** — pleats padded with wadding to hold a cylinder.
- **Pencil pleat** — gathered tape, 2.5×; easiest.
- **Wave/ripplefold** — needs a specific corded track and carrier spacing; 1.15–1.3× only.
- **Eyelet** — punched rings; casual.

### Linings and interlining
Lining is cut narrower than the face fabric and locked to it with **lock stitch** every 300–400 mm down each drop, so the two layers hang as one. **Interlining** (bump or domette) goes between face and lining, locked to the face fabric — it is the single largest quality upgrade available, giving weight, insulation, acoustic absorption and drape, and roughly doubling labour. Blackout lining is 3-pass coated; expect pinhole light at stitch lines.

### Roman blinds
Cut face fabric plus 50 mm side turnings and hems; interline if quality matters. Mark **rod pockets** on the lining, spacing so the folds stack evenly: for a blind of drop *D* with *n* folds, the bottom section is roughly half a fold deeper than the rest. Typical fold depth 200–300 mm. Sew rod pockets, insert flat fibreglass or timber rods, sew **rings** at 300–400 mm horizontal centres, thread cords up through the rings and across a headrail through screw eyes or a cord-lock. Weight bar in the bottom hem. Fix to a batten or headrail with hook-and-loop tape. **[2]–[3]**

**Tools.** Long cutting table (2.4 m minimum), long metal rule, rotary cutter and mat, tailor's chalk, weights, sewing machine with a walking foot, hand needles, pins, mitring gauge, steamer, ladder.

**References.** Merrick & Day's workroom manuals (*The Encyclopaedia of Curtains*, *Curtain Design Directory*) are the trade standard; Wendy Baker and Catherine Merrick titles are the UK workroom references. **Titles and editions `needs-verification`.**

**Skill:** unlined panels **[1]**; lined pinch-pleat **[2]**; interlined and hand-finished **[3]**.

## 3. Cushions and soft furnishings

- **Knife-edge cushion:** two panels, right sides together, zip in one seam, stitch, clip corners, turn. **[1]**
- **Piped cushion:** cut bias strips at 45° (width = cord circumference + 2× seam allowance), join on the bias, fold over cord, stitch with a zipper or piping foot, sandwich between panels. Piping is what separates homemade from professional. **[1]**
- **Boxed cushion (seat pad):** top, bottom and a gusset/boxing strip with a zip section along the back and 100 mm around each back corner so the cover can be removed. **[2]**
- **Fills:** feather-and-down for softness (buy the pad **50 mm larger** than the cover, always); foam with a feather wrap for seats; polyester for washable/allergy use.
- **Bolsters, headboards, ottomans, pelmets and valances** are all variations on the same skills plus a timber substrate.

## 4. Slipcovers (loose covers)

Made by **draping and pinning calico directly onto the piece**, marking the seam lines, cutting the calico apart to make the pattern, then cutting the real fabric with generous allowances. Details: piped seams, a skirt (gathered, box-pleated or kick-pleat), ties or a zip at the back leg, and pre-washing the fabric so the cover can be laundered. **[3]** — harder than upholstery in some ways because there is no staple to hide a mistake.

## 5. Lampshade making

**Hard (PVC-backed) shades:** self-adhesive PVC panel, fabric laminated on, rings (a top ring with a gimbal or duplex fitting, a bottom ring), rolled and glued around the rings, edges tucked and glued with a bone folder. Kits make this accessible. **[2]**
**Soft/traditional (tailored) shades:** a wire frame bound with tape, lining fitted and hand-stitched inside, outer fabric stretched and stitched on, then trim applied over the stitching. **[3]**
**Pleated shades:** knife or box pleats hand-set around the frame. **[3]**
**Key dimensions:** a shade should be roughly **two-thirds the height** of a table lamp base and wide enough that the bottom diameter exceeds the widest part of the base; the fitting must hide the bulb and the harp. For a diffuse warm glow, use a cream or gold-lined shade; white linings read cooler.
> ⚠️ Do not rewire lamps unless competent and legally permitted. Have every rewired lamp PAT-tested or equivalently certified.

## 6. Rug making and tufting

**Tufting-gun rugs (the accessible route).** Stretch **primary tufting cloth** on a heavy timber frame at high tension; draw the design on the back (mirrored); tuft with a **cut-pile or loop-pile electric tufting gun** using rug yarn (wool, acrylic or blends); apply latex/TufBond glue to the back; press on a **secondary backing** cloth; trim, shear and carve the pile with clippers and shears; bind or fold the edge. **[2]–[3]** Realistic budget for a starter setup: gun, frame, yarn, glue, clippers — the equipment is now inexpensive relative to the results.
**Hand-knotted rugs.** A vertical loom, warp threads, and individual knots (Turkish/Ghiordes symmetrical, or Persian/Senneh asymmetrical) tied row by row, beaten down, with weft picks between rows. Quality is measured in **knots per square inch/decimetre**. **[4]** — a workshop craft, not a hobby.
**Flatweave / kilim.** Weft-faced plain weave on a loom, slit-tapestry or interlocked joins for colour blocks. **[3]**
**Punch-needle and latch-hook.** Entry-level versions of the same idea. **[1]**
**Rag rugs, braided and hooked rugs.** Traditional, cheap, and appropriate to a reuse-led practice. **[1]–[2]**
**[ZA] [NA]** Grass, sisal, ilala palm and reed weaving traditions across Southern Africa produce mats and floor coverings of exceptional quality — commissioning from established weavers is usually better and cheaper than making, and supports the craft economy.

## 7. Cabinet and shelf building

**Cross-reference domain `06_joinery_and_woodwork` for the full treatment.** For interiors specifically:
- **Carcass construction:** 18 mm birch ply or MDF, butt-jointed with dominos/dowels/pocket screws, or housed (dado) joints for shelves. Back panel in a rebate — it squares the carcass and carries racking loads.
- **Shelf spans:** see file `06` — 800 mm max in 18 mm MDF, 900–1000 mm in 25 mm, longer with a front lipping (a 20 × 40 mm hardwood lipping on edge roughly doubles the usable span).
- **Fitted joinery into an existing room:** nothing is square. Build the carcass square, then **scribe** the filler panels to the wall with a compass and a block plane.
- **Doors and drawers:** 35 mm cup hinges with three-way adjustment; soft-close undermount runners; a consistent **3 mm shadow gap** reads far better than an attempted zero gap.
- **Finishing:** spray-lacquered MDF for colour; oiled or lacquered veneer for timber; always finish the inside as well or the panel will bow.
**Skill:** flat-pack assembly **[1]**; a wall of shelves **[2]**; fitted alcove joinery **[3]**; sprayed handleless kitchens **[4]**.

## 8. Painting and decorative finishes

### Preparation (the 80% that determines the result)
Fill, sand (120 → 180 → 240 grit), dust off, mist-coat new plaster (paint thinned 10–20%), prime bare timber and metal with the right primer, caulk internal angles, and mask properly. Under raking light, **matt paint hides nothing**.

### Application
Cut in with a 50–63 mm angled brush; roll with a medium-pile microfibre roller in overlapping "W" strokes and lay off in one direction; two coats minimum, three over a strong colour change. Keep a wet edge. For joinery, use a brush plus a foam or mohair mini-roller, or spray.

### The decorative finishes

| Finish | Method | Skill |
|---|---|---|
| **Colourwash** | Thinned glaze over a base coat, worked with a rag or brush for a soft mottle | [1] |
| **Limewash** | Slaked lime + pigment, applied in thin cross-hatched coats onto a porous mineral substrate; carbonates and lightens as it cures. Breathable, matt, alive. Not for gypsum plaster without a mineral primer | [2] |
| **Clay/mineral paint** | Like limewash but more forgiving | [1] |
| **Stencilling** | Cut or bought stencil, spray adhesive to fix, near-dry brush or roller, pounce not stroke, register marks for repeats | [1]–[2] |
| **Stippling / dragging / ragging** | Oil or acrylic glaze over eggshell, broken with a stippling brush, dragging brush or rag | [2] |
| **Marbling** | Base coat, glaze layers, veining with a feather or fine sable, softening with a badger brush, glazing back, then varnishing. Study real stone slabs, not other people's marbling | [4] |
| **Graining (faux bois)** | Base coat in the ground colour, glaze combed and rocked with a graining rocker/heart-grainer, overgrained, varnished | [4] |
| **Gilding (oil/mordant)** | Gold size to the correct tack, loose or transfer leaf laid, skewed off with a brush, sealed. Water gilding on gesso and bole, burnished with an agate, is a separate and much harder craft | [3] oil / [4] water |
| **Venetian plaster / marmorino** | Lime putty and marble dust applied in multiple thin coats with a stainless trowel, each burnished; final coat compressed to a polish; soaped or waxed | [4] |
| **Tadelakt** | Moroccan lime plaster applied, compressed with a smooth stone, then treated with **olive-oil soap** which saponifies the lime and makes it water-resistant. Suitable for showers and basins when correctly executed | [4] |
| **Microcement** | Primer, mesh/scrim, two to three thin polymer-modified cement coats trowelled, sanded between, then two to three sealer coats. Hairline cracking is inherent to the material — tell the client before, not after | [3] |
| **Trompe-l'œil** | Perspective-correct painted illusion. Syrie Maugham learned it in her apprenticeship; it is a fine-art skill | [4] |

**References.** Jocasta Innes's *Paint Magic* is the classic that started the modern revival; Kevin McCloud's *Decorating Book* and *Complete Book of Paint and Decorative Techniques*; Annie Sloan's colour and finish books; and for gilding and traditional finishes, the conservation literature. **Titles, authors and editions `needs-verification`.**

## 9. Tiling and mosaic

**Setting out is the whole job.** Find the room's centre lines, dry-lay to see where cuts fall, and shift the grid so that cuts are equal at both ends and no sliver-cut lands in a visible position. Full tiles go at the entrance and at the most-seen edge.
**Substrate.** Must be flat (±3 mm over 2 m), rigid and, in wet areas, **tanked** with a liquid membrane or board system with taped joints and correctly lapped corners. Falls to the gully must be formed in the screed, not made up with adhesive.
**Fixing.** Notched trowel, correct adhesive class for the tile and substrate (large-format and porcelain need a flexible, often S1/S2, adhesive and back-buttering), levelling clips for large formats, tile spacers sized for the grout joint. Cut with a wet saw for porcelain and stone; a manual score-and-snap for ceramic; a hole saw or grinder for penetrations.
**Grouting and sealing.** Grout 24 h after fixing; epoxy grout for showers and worktops; seal natural stone **before** grouting so the grout does not stain it. Movement joints in silicone at every internal corner and at perimeters — never grout an internal corner.
**Zellige and handmade tile** are deliberately irregular: expect variation in thickness and colour, use a wider joint (3–5 mm) or a tight butt joint by hand, order **20–25% extra** for breakage and shade variation.
**Mosaic.** Direct method (tesserae fixed face-up into adhesive) or indirect/reverse method (assembled face-down on paper, then set). Nippers, tesserae, thin-set, grout. **[2]** for sheeted mosaic; **[3]–[4]** for hand-cut pictorial work.
**Skill:** a splashback **[2]**; a floor **[3]**; a fully tanked wet room or a large-format book-matched slab wall **[4]**.

## 10. Framing

**Frame construction.** Mitre the moulding at 45° with a mitre saw or a mitre trimmer for a glass-smooth cut; join with a corner clamp and glue plus V-nails (underpinner) or wedges. **Mount/mat cutting** needs a bevel mat cutter and a straight edge; conservation board is acid-free. **Glazing:** float glass (cheap), low-reflective/museum glass (expensive, transformative for art on a lit wall), or acrylic for large or shipped pieces. **Backing:** acid-free board, sealed with framing tape; D-rings and picture wire, or a French cleat for heavy pieces.
**Conservation rules:** never dry-mount original work; hinge with Japanese paper and wheat starch; use spacers so the artwork does not touch the glazing; keep works on paper out of direct sun.
**Skill:** ready-made frames **[1]**; cutting mats **[1]**; making frames **[2]**.

## 11. Hanging

- Centre at **1450–1550 mm** above finished floor and be consistent (file `06`).
- **Fixings by substrate:** solid masonry → 6 mm plug and screw; timber stud → find the stud with a detector or by tapping and drill a pilot; plasterboard → spring toggle or metal self-drill anchor, never a plastic plug alone. Know the weight and double the rating.
- Two hooks are better than one — a picture hung on two points does not tilt.
- Use a **picture rail** where one exists; it is adjustable, reversible and historically correct in Georgian, Victorian and Edwardian rooms.
- **Gallery walls:** cut paper templates of every frame, tape them to the wall, and move them until the composition works. Mark the hook position through the paper.
- **Heavy mirrors and TVs:** into structure only, with a French cleat or a rated bracket. Get it wrong and it is a safety incident, not a snag.

## 12. Plants and interior landscaping

- **Light is the only variable that matters first.** Measure in lux or by shadow test: a sharp shadow = bright direct; a soft shadow = bright indirect; no shadow = low light. Most plant failures are light failures, then overwatering.
- **Reliable low-light:** *Zamioculcas zamiifolia* (ZZ), *Sansevieria/Dracaena trifasciata* (snake plant), *Aspidistra*, *Epipremnum* (pothos), *Zamioculcas*, *Aglaonema*.
- **Bright indirect:** *Ficus lyrata* (fiddle leaf fig — beautiful and unforgiving), *Monstera deliciosa*, *Strelitzia nicolai*, *Philodendron*, *Calathea* (humidity-demanding), palms (*Howea*, *Chamaedorea*).
- **[NA] [ZA] hot, dry, high-light interiors:** succulents and *Euphorbia*, *Sansevieria*, *Aloe*, *Crassula*, *Zamioculcas*, *Ficus* species, *Cycads* (check CITES and permit status — several are protected), and locally propagated indigenous species. Indigenous planting is both climatically sensible and legally simpler than importing.
- **Containers and drainage.** Never plant directly into a decorative pot without drainage — use a nursery pot inside a cachepot, on feet or gravel. Specify pot weight and the floor loading for large specimens.
- **Green walls and interior landscaping** are building services, not decoration: they need irrigation, drainage, a maintenance contract and, usually, supplementary grow lighting. Budget the maintenance contract in the FF&E schedule or it will fail within a year.
- **Design use.** Plants do the same work as sculpture: they break a corner, soften a hard junction, add a vertical, and give a room something living. Vary leaf scale — one large-leaved specimen beats five small pots.
