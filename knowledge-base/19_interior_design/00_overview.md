# Interior design domain overview

**Summary.** This domain covers the design of interior space as a discipline: what it is, how it is distinguished from decoration and from interior architecture, how one is trained and licensed, how leading practitioners were actually formed, how projects are run and priced, and what technical knowledge the work rests on. The single most important structural fact is that the field is **only partially regulated**: in most of the world anyone may call themselves an interior designer, while in a minority of North American jurisdictions the title or the right to submit certain drawings is restricted to NCIDQ-certified practitioners. Everything else in this domain — education, awards, fee models, business structure — follows from that half-regulated condition, which is why reputation, publication and portfolio do work that a licence does elsewhere.

> ⚠️ Anything that alters means of egress, fire compartmentation, structure, sanitary provision or accessibility is **building-code work**, not decoration. In most jurisdictions it must be signed off by a registered architect or engineer even where an interior designer produced the drawings. Never let a "decorating" scope quietly absorb code-regulated work.

## The three terms, honestly distinguished

The trade uses "interior designer", "interior decorator" and "interior architect" loosely and often as status markers. There are, however, real differences of scope, liability and training.

### Interior decoration — surface and furnishing
Concerned with what sits *on* and *in* the shell: colour, textiles, furniture, rugs, lighting fittings (not usually lighting *design*), art, window treatments, accessories and styling. The decorator's deliverables are schemes, sample boards, purchase orders and an installation. There is normally no building work, no statutory approval, no contractor other than painters, decorators, upholsterers and curtain makers. This is the tradition Elsie de Wolfe invented commercially in 1905, and it is a legitimate, difficult, highly skilled discipline — not a lesser one. Much of the canon in file `08` is decoration literature.

### Interior design — spatial, technical, sometimes regulated
Adds the manipulation of the space itself: partitions, openings, levels, ceilings, joinery, services coordination, wet areas, acoustics, lighting design, fire and accessibility compliance, and full technical documentation issued to contractors. It typically involves a building contract, a programme, statutory approvals and professional indemnity exposure. This is what CIDA accredits and what NCIDQ tests — and it is why NCIDQ's stated purpose is competency "to safeguard public health, safety, and welfare" rather than taste.

### Interior architecture — the shell rethought from within
An academic and European-professional term for design that reworks the envelope: adaptive reuse, structural intervention, new insertions into existing fabric. RISD frames its department precisely this way — it approaches "the design of buildings from inside out" and centres adaptive reuse. In much of continental Europe (and in South African practice) "interior architect" is the ordinary professional title; in the UK and US it is more often a positioning claim. Legally, in most places it does **not** confer the right to practise architecture.

### A practical test
Ask three questions of any brief:
1. **Does anything move that is fixed?** Walls, floors, ceilings, openings, services. If yes, you are past decoration.
2. **Does a statutory authority have to approve it?** If yes, you need a competent designer of record and a code path.
3. **Who carries the risk if a person is injured?** If the answer is "the designer", the job needs technical documentation, not mood boards.

## How the profession is actually structured

**By regulation.** Three regimes coexist:
- *Title/practice acts* — a minority of US states and Canadian provinces restrict the use of a title (e.g. "registered interior designer") or the right to seal certain interior construction documents. The gateway is NCIDQ certification. The exact list of jurisdictions changes and is `needs-verification` here; consult CIDQ's current jurisdiction map before advising anyone.
- *Voluntary chartered-style bodies* — BIID (UK), IIDA and ASID (US), IID **[ZA]**. Membership is a quality signal and a code-of-conduct commitment, not a licence.
- *Unregulated* — most of the world, including, as far as could be confirmed, Namibia **[NA]**. `needs-verification`

**By market segment.** Residential (private clients, high touch, procurement-heavy), contract/commercial (workplace, retail, healthcare, education — fee-for-service, contractor-delivered), hospitality (hotels, restaurants, bars — the most publication-hungry and awards-driven sector), and product/brand work (a designer's own furniture, lighting or fabric lines, which is where mature studios make margin).

**By business model.** Solo decorator with a maker network; boutique studio of 3–12; mid-size studio of 15–60 with technical, FF&E and project-management functions separated; and the architecture-adjacent practice that does both shell and interior. File `10` treats the economics.

**By the money.** Three distinct revenue mechanisms — design fee, trade discount retained as procurement margin, and product royalties/licensing — with materially different ethics and disclosure duties. This is the single most common source of client disputes in the field and is dealt with head-on in files `04` and `10`.

## The awkward truth about entry

File `02` tests a claim that circulates as folklore: that a strikingly high proportion of the most influential interior designers never studied interior design. The research in that file, based on documented biographies, largely supports it for the 20th-century decorating tradition and finds it weakening but still present today:

- Elsie de Wolfe was an actress; Syrie Maugham apprenticed in a decorating firm at around 40; Jean-Michel Frank had **no formal design training** at all and came to it through a Paris social circle; Billy Baldwin left Princeton architecture after two years; Sister Parish had no training and opened at 23 in the Depression; Andrée Putman trained as a *musician* at the Paris Conservatoire; Faye Toogood read art history and was a *World of Interiors* stylist; Ilse Crawford read history and was a magazine editor; Pierre Yovanovitch went to business school and spent eight years in menswear at Pierre Cardin; Martyn Lawrence Bullard trained as an actor; Bobby Berk has no formal training and came up through home-furnishing retail.
- Against that: Peter Marino (Cornell, 1971, then SOM / George Nelson / I. M. Pei), India Mahdavi (Beaux-Arts Paris then Parsons and SVA), Jacques Grange (École Boulle and École Camondo), Joseph Dirand (ENSA Paris-Belleville), Kelly Wearstler (Massachusetts College of Art), Sophie Ashby (art history at Leeds, then interior design at Parsons) are all conventionally trained.

The honest conclusion is not "school doesn't matter". It is that **the field has two doors** — a technical-professional door that requires accredited training and, in some places, certification; and a taste-and-network door that requires an eye, a first client, and publication. Contract, hospitality and code-regulated work overwhelmingly comes through the first door. High-end residential decoration still frequently comes through the second.
