# Namibian geology, soils, groundwater and what they mean for building

**Summary.** Namibia's geology divides into four periods driven by the movements of the **Congo and Kalahari cratons**: Rodinia and earlier (2 650–900 Ma), Gondwana's formation (900–550 Ma, the **Damara Orogen**), Gondwana (550–132 Ma, the **Nama** and **Karoo** sequences), and post-Gondwana (132 Ma to now, **Etendeka volcanics** and the **Kalahari Group** sands). About **one third of Namibia is covered in sand** deposited within the last few million years, and roughly **46 % of the country shows bedrock at surface** — the rest is buried under Kalahari and Namib sediment. Ohangwena sits over the **Owambo Basin**, under a thick Kalahari blanket of quartz sand (Arenosols) with calcrete at depth, saline/sodic soils in the *iishana*, and a layered groundwater system whose deep member — the **Ohangwena II aquifer** — is one of the most significant water resources in the country. For a builder in Okongo the practical consequences are: **no rock, no gravel, cohesionless sand foundations, calcrete as the only local hard material, and a water supply that is a borehole rather than a main.**

## 1. The geological framework

### 1.1 Basement complexes (Palaeoproterozoic–Mesoproterozoic)
The oldest rocks are the **Epupa Metamorphic Complex** (~2 100 Ma) at the Kunene River, the **Huab Complex** west of Outjo, and the **Grootfontein Metamorphic Complex** in the north-east — all attached to the **Congo Craton**. In the south and south-west the **Namaqua Metamorphic Complex** (Mesoproterozoic) carries metasediments derived from both cratons. Beneath Ohangwena, the basement is Congo Craton **granite and gneiss**, described as devolatilised during magmatic events over 2 200 Ma ago and therefore "exceptionally old and highly rigid".

### 1.2 The Damara Orogen (Neoproterozoic)
The **Damara Orogenic Belt** is the defining structure of central and northern Namibia — high-grade metamorphics with frequent granitic intrusions. Its mineralisation relates to successive phases of intracontinental rifting (copper, graphite) and passive margin formation. The Damara Sequence hosts the copper of the **Otavi Mountainland** and the **Matchless Amphibolite Belt**.

In the **Owambo Basin** the Damara Supergroup is a three-part package:
- **Nosib Group (900–750 Ma):** interbedded marine and continental sediment capped by volcanics — the first fill of the pull-apart basin.
- **Otavi Group (750–650 Ma):** entirely shallow marine — **limestone and dolomite** with oolitic shoals. This is the rock that later dissolved to form the **karst aquifers** of the Tsumeb–Otavi–Grootfontein belt.
- **Mulden Group (600–560 Ma):** deposited after uplift from the Kaoko and Damara Belt collisions (580–530 Ma), with a black-shale marker at its middle.

### 1.3 Nama Group (Ediacaran–Cambrian)
Overlying the Damaran rocks in the south, the **Nama Group** is shallow-shelf marine sediment famous for **Ediacaran to Early Cambrian fossils**. Clastic material came from the Damara Orogen; basal pelites and carbonates have an eastern provenance.

### 1.4 Karoo Supergroup (Permo-Triassic)
Begins with the glacial **Dwyka Group**, then continental deposits of the **Omingonde Formation** (Ecca and Stormberg Groups). Intruded by Mesozoic **dolerite sills and dyke swarms** associated with the break-up of Gondwana. Fossil highlights: therapsids from the Omingonde Formation, dinosaur trackways in the Early Jurassic **Etjo Sandstone**. In the Owambo Basin, the Karoo Group (280–130 Ma) is glacial till in valleys carved into the Mulden, then carbonate and organic-rich marine fill.

### 1.5 Post-Gondwana: Etendeka and the Kalahari Group
Gondwana began breaking up in the early Cretaceous (~184–132 Ma). South America drifted west; the **Etendeka Plateau** basalts and alkaline intrusions erupted; and the Owambo Basin was reactivated as a pull-apart with rotational wrench systems admitting sills, dykes and pegmatites — and the hydrothermal activity that produced the **Tsumeb** ore body.

From the mid-Cretaceous the basin sat near **30°S** and **desertified rapidly**. The resulting sandstone and unconsolidated sand deposition continues through the Quaternary as the **Kalahari Sequence**. This is the material directly beneath Okongo, and it is **hundreds of metres thick** in the basin centre.

### 1.6 Mineral resources
- **Diamonds** — alluvial and marine, along the southern coast north of the Orange mouth; **95 % gem quality**; Namibia leads in marine diamond mining.
- **Copper** — Tsumeb (closed), Kombat, Rosh Pinah. Tsumeb has **309 recorded mineral species with 72 type localities**; *arsentsumebite* and *tsumebite* are named for it.
- **Uranium** — in Damaran granites, Tertiary calcrete and Karoo sandstones. Namibia was the **third-largest producer globally, 7 333 t in 2023, 12.2 % of world production**.
- **Gold** — Karibib, Rehoboth, Omaruru, Kunene.
- **Industrial** — salt from coastal solar pans, fluorspar, wollastonite, semi-precious stones (tourmaline, aquamarine, garnet).
- **Cement, lime and aggregate** — the Otavi carbonates are the country's limestone resource.

> **Building implication.** Namibia's hard rock, aggregate and cement resources are concentrated in the **Damara belt and the Otavi Mountainland** — 300–500 km from Okongo. This is the geological reason that stone, aggregate and cement all have long haulage in Ohangwena and why **on-site block-making and calcrete winning** are so often the right answer.

## 2. Soils

The Atlas of Namibia identifies **eleven widespread soil types**, and is blunt about their quality: "Namibia's soils are generally poor... soils with inherently low fertility and poor physical properties place a major constraint on farming."

### 2.1 The types that matter in the north

**Arenosols — deep windblown quartz sands.** The dominant soil of the Kalahari, including the eastern and north-eastern sandveld where Okongo lies, and of the Namib Sand Sea and Kunene dune fields. Their defining engineering and agricultural property is a **low capacity to store water and nutrients**.

**Calcisols — lime-accumulating soils.** Widespread across central Namibia on alluvial, colluvial and aeolian deposits. Calcium carbonate concentrates through repeated cycles of rain-wetting and evaporation, cementing horizons at depth. **These are the parent of calcrete.**

**Solonchaks (saline) and Solonetz (sodic).** Concentrated in the **Cuvelai–Etosha drainage** — the closed depressions of the *iishana* and the Etosha pans. Solonetz are sodium-rich clayey soils with **slow drainage**.

**Fluvisols and Gleysols.** Restricted to riverbeds, floodplains and waterlogged areas (Liambezi–Chobe). Fluvisols show distinct bands of varying texture, colour and organic content; Gleysols show greyish, bluish and greenish colours in deeper layers — the visual marker of **permanent or seasonal saturation**.

### 2.2 Measured properties

| Property | Value | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Texture | "Great majority... sandy and/or loamy" | High clay only in NE alluvial soils |
| Cation exchange capacity, sandy soils | **<4 cmol/kg** | Extremely low — no nutrient or contaminant buffering |
| Organic carbon | "Most Namibian soils have little organic carbon" | Hot, arid, low biomass |
| pH | Most in **3–8**; sandy soils prone to acidification | Optimum 6–7 |
| Bulk density | **<1.5 t/m³** allows effective air and water movement | Above this indicates compaction |
| Water-holding capacity | Greatest in the **deep sands of the north-east**; lowest in south, west, central-north | Depth compensates for poor texture |
| Depth limits | Shallow bedrock, and **cemented calcrete or petrified gypsum hardpans** | Hardpans restrict roots — and dictate excavation |

### 2.3 Engineering behaviour of deep aeolian Kalahari sand

This is the material a builder in Okongo will actually meet. Its characteristics follow directly from being **wind-sorted, fine-to-medium, uniformly graded, quartz-dominant and essentially cohesionless**:

- **No trench stability.** A vertical face will not stand in dry aeolian sand. Excavations must batter back to the angle of repose (typically around 30–34° for loose dry sand) or be shored. This makes deep strip footings disproportionately expensive and dangerous.
- **Uniform grading = poor compactibility with water alone.** Single-sized sand has few finer particles to fill voids, so it reaches only a modest maximum dry density and needs **vibratory** compaction, not just watering and rolling.
- **Loose in-situ state.** Wind-deposited sand near the surface is often at low relative density. Left uncompacted under a slab or a strip footing it will settle under load and under wetting. **Compaction control is the single most important groundworks discipline on a Kalahari sand site.**
- **Free-draining but with low water retention.** Excellent for soakaways and for avoiding heave; terrible for holding compaction moisture and for curing.
- **Non-plastic, non-expansive.** The good news: Kalahari sand does **not** shrink and swell the way expansive clays do. Foundation problems here are settlement and erosion problems, not heave problems — unless the site is on Cuvelai clay or Solonetz, where they are heave and dispersion problems instead.
- **Erodible.** Fine sand is scoured by concentrated water flow. Roof water discharged onto bare sand will cut a hole and undermine.
- **Sulphate and chloride risk near the *iishana*.** Saline soils and shallow saline groundwater attack concrete and reinforcement. Test before assuming ordinary Portland cement is adequate.

> ⚠️ **Building implications for foundations.**
> - Prefer **shallow, wide footings** or a **stiffened raft** over deep strips. On uniform sand, a raft distributes load, tolerates local variation, avoids deep excavation, and gets the floor up out of the flood/termite zone in one operation.
> - **Strip and compact** the founding zone in controlled layers; specify a compaction standard and test it. Do not accept "we watered it".
> - **Blind and protect.** Cast onto a compacted, blinded surface; a sand blinding under a slab will simply mix with the fill unless a separating membrane is used.
> - **Keep water away from the founding sand.** Roof water piped clear, aprons falling away, no ponding against walls. The failure mode here is erosion and differential settlement, not clay movement.
> - **Termites.** Deep sandveld in northern Namibia is heavily termite-infested. Physical barriers, concrete ground beams, and avoidance of ground-contact timber matter more than in the south.
> - `needs-verification`: allowable bearing pressures for Ohangwena Kalahari sand. **Do not assume a value** — order a trial pit and, for anything beyond a small single storey, a DPSH/SPT probe.

## 3. Calcrete — Namibia's local construction material

**Calcrete** (caliche, *kalkkoppie*, "surface limestone") is a near-surface horizon of soil, sand or gravel cemented by secondary calcium carbonate precipitated from evaporating groundwater and soil moisture. It forms wherever evaporation dominates rainfall — which in Namibia is everywhere. The Atlas identifies **Calcisols** as one of the country's widespread soil groups and names **cemented calcrete layers** as the classic limit on soil depth.

Calcrete matters out of all proportion to its geological interest because in Namibia and Botswana it is:

1. **The standard road base and sub-base material.** Almost every unpaved and many paved roads in Namibia are built on calcrete won from borrow pits alongside the alignment. Its self-cementing behaviour — carbonate re-precipitating after compaction — gives it strength gains that a comparable silicate gravel would not achieve.
2. **The only locally-won hard material in sand country.** Where there is no rock and no river gravel, a calcrete borrow pit is the difference between a buildable site and a fully imported one.
3. **A source of aggregate and of lime.** Hardpan calcrete can be crushed for aggregate; calcrete has historically been burnt for lime.
4. **A uranium host.** Tertiary calcrete hosts some of Namibia's uranium (Langer Heinrich style deposits) — geologically interesting, operationally irrelevant to a house.

**Practical points for using calcrete:**
- Calcrete varies enormously — from soft powdery "calcrete sand" through nodular and honeycomb calcrete to a massive hardpan that needs ripping or blasting. **Test the specific pit**, not the material class.
- Because it is carbonate, **calcrete is not a durable exposed aggregate in acidic or aggressive conditions** and it is generally unsuitable for structural concrete where hard, sound, non-reactive stone is required. Its role is base course, fill, hardstanding, blinding and access roads.
- Compacted calcrete makes an excellent **construction platform and haul road** on a sand site — which on a remote job is worth as much as anything it does in the finished works.
- Winning calcrete requires a **permit** and rehabilitation of the borrow pit. Confirm with the Roads Authority / regional council before opening a pit.
- `needs-verification`: Namibian specification classes and CBR requirements for calcrete base and sub-base (Namibia follows the southern African TRH/COLTO tradition — see domain 03 and 17 for the actual standards).

**In eastern Ohangwena specifically:** calcrete is present in the Kalahari sequence but the sand cover is thick, so borrow sources are less reliably shallow than on the central plateau. **Verify locally.** The absence of nearby calcrete would substantially change the cost of any road, hardstanding or floor sub-base.

## 4. Groundwater and aquifers

### 4.1 The national picture
Groundwater is critical: **over 100 000 boreholes** have been drilled in Namibia, about half still in operation, and groundwater underpins water supply across nearly 80 % of the country's area. National safe yield is estimated at **660 Mm³/yr**, of which **groundwater is 300 Mm³/yr**.

Aquifers come in two forms: **porous** (water in pores between grains of sand, gravel, sandstone) and **fractured** (water in joints, dissolution voids and breaks in hard rock). Namibia's richest are the **karst** (Tsumeb–Otavi–Grootfontein), **Koichab**, **Kuiseb**, **Maltahöhe**, **Ohangwena II**, **Omdel**, **Stampriet** and **Windhoek** aquifers.

Groundwater moves slowly and picks up dissolved salts as it goes. The Atlas gives a striking calibration: water takes an estimated **15 000 years to travel 15 km** from the Auas Mountains to the hot spring in central Windhoek.

Borehole yields vary enormously and locally, especially in fractured aquifers. **1 m³/h** may be adequate for a small village; **15 m³/h or more** can irrigate large fields or supply a town.

### 4.2 The Ohangwena aquifer system

This is the most significant groundwater story in northern Namibia and directly relevant to Okongo.

The system is **vertically stacked**: aquifers separated by an aquiclude, with very different water in each. The Atlas explicitly names "the Ohangwena aquifer that straddles the border between Ohangwena and Angola" as an example of such stacking.

**Ohangwena II** was identified in **2012** by a Namibian–German programme (the **BGR**, Germany's Federal Institute for Geosciences and Natural Resources, working with the Namibian government and EU partners), under project manager **Martin Quinger**. Its published characteristics:

| Attribute | Value |
|---|---|
| Extent on the Namibian side | **~70 km × 40 km** |
| Depth | **~300 m** |
| Pressure state | **Artesian — under natural pressure**, so extraction is "easy and cheap" |
| Stored volume | **~5 billion m³** |
| Water age | **up to 10 000 years** |
| Supply capacity | Equal to **~400 years** of current consumption for the northern area, home to about **40 % of the national population** |
| Drought buffer | Could act as a natural buffer for **up to 15 years of drought** |
| Recharge | Receives inflow from **Angola in the north** — a transboundary resource |
| Overlying hazard | **A smaller saline aquifer sits directly above it** |
| Quality | Described as cleaner than many modern sources precisely because it was recharged before industrial pollution existed |

At the time of discovery the north depended for drinking water on a **40-year-old canal bringing water across the border from Angola**, serving about **800 000 people**.

> ⚠️ **The critical technical warning, in the project manager's own words:** "If people don't comply with our technical recommendations they might create a hydraulic shortcut between the two aquifers which might lead to the salty water from the upper one contaminating the deep one or vice versa." **Unauthorised or badly cased drilling is the principal threat to this resource.** Any borehole in Ohangwena that goes deep must be properly cased and grout-sealed through the saline horizon. This is not a nicety; it is the difference between a usable regional aquifer and a ruined one.

**Management guidance:** experts recommend that under normal climatic conditions abstraction be limited to **the inflow from the north**, treating the stored volume as a drought reserve rather than an income stream.

### 4.3 Why the Cuvelai has so few boreholes
The Atlas is explicit: the small number of boreholes in the Cuvelai is due to **salty deeper groundwater in that area, unsuitable for most uses**, which is exactly why the piped supply from the Kunene River was developed instead. **Shallow groundwater in the Cuvelai is generally brackish.** In Omusati, four small **solar-powered brackish-water desalination plants** were installed in 2010 under the German–Namibian **CuveWaters** project, each producing **0.5–3.3 m³/day** — enough for 10–66 people — at a desalination cost of about **€15/m³**, which the source describes as "very high".

## 5. Borehole practice in Ohangwena

**Sequence for a rural site:**
1. **Hydrogeological siting** — do not drill on guesswork. Get advice on target depth and expected water quality for the specific locality; the shallow/deep distinction matters more here than almost anywhere.
2. **Permitting** — abstraction and drilling are regulated under the **Water Resources Management Act, 2013 (No. 11 of 2013)**, administered by the responsible ministry (formerly Agriculture, Water and Forestry). Confirm current permitting requirements before drilling.
3. **Construction** — steel or uPVC casing, screened only in the target aquifer, with a **grout seal through the overlying saline horizon**. Sanitary seal at surface.
4. **Development and test pumping** — establish sustainable yield, not just the yield at the moment of drilling.
5. **Water quality analysis** — as a minimum: electrical conductivity/TDS, chloride, sulphate, nitrate, fluoride, pH, iron and manganese, and bacteriological.
6. **Pumping** — solar-powered submersible pumping is the default in this irradiance regime (see `08_infrastructure-and-services.md`); if the borehole taps the artesian deep aquifer, pumping energy may be minimal.

**Water quality issues to expect:**
- **Salinity/brackishness** in the shallow aquifer — the dominant problem.
- **Sulphate and chloride** attack on concrete and on steel reinforcement, pipework and pumps.
- **Fluoride and nitrate** are common problems in Namibian groundwater generally. `needs-verification` for Ohangwena specifically.
- **Hardness/scaling** from carbonate-rich water where the karst influence reaches.

> **Building implications of water quality.**
> - **Test borehole water before using it to mix concrete.** High chloride or sulphate water will compromise durability. If in doubt, use rainwater or potable supply for concrete.
> - Consider **sulphate-resisting cement** or a higher cement content and lower w/c ratio where saline groundwater or saline soil is present near the *iishana*.
> - Specify **plastic or lined pipework** rather than galvanised steel where water is saline.
> - **Rainwater harvesting is a quality strategy, not just a quantity strategy** in the Cuvelai: the Atlas notes that at the height of the rainy season *oshana* water is clear and of good quality, and the CuveWaters project pursued both floodwater and rooftop rainwater harvesting for exactly this reason.

## 6. What all this means for a building at Okongo — checklist

| Question | Answer for eastern Ohangwena |
|---|---|
| What will I dig into? | Deep, loose to medium-dense **aeolian quartz sand**; possible calcrete at depth |
| Will trenches stand? | **No.** Batter or shore |
| Is the soil expansive? | **No** on the sandveld; **yes, problematically sodic/dispersive** on Cuvelai Solonetz |
| Best foundation type? | **Stiffened raft** or wide shallow footings on compacted, tested fill |
| Is there stone on site? | **No.** Nearest hard rock is the Damara belt / Otavi, 300–500 km |
| Is there sand on site? | Yes, in unlimited quantity — but **uniform, fine, and generally unsuitable as concrete sand without blending**. `needs-verification` for local grading |
| Is there gravel on site? | Only **calcrete**, if a pit can be found and permitted |
| Where does water come from? | A **borehole**, or the village reticulation if on a serviced plot |
| Is the groundwater good? | **Deep = fresh and artesian; shallow = often saline.** Never mix the two |
| Flood risk? | Sheet inundation in the Cuvelai in ~45 % of years. Site high, raise the floor |
| Termites? | **Yes, severe.** Design for it |
| Corrosion risk? | Moderate to high near saline ground; low on the clean sandveld |
