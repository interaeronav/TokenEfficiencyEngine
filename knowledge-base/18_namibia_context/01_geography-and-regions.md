# Namibia's physical geography and regions

**Summary.** Namibia is a **824 292 km²** country of **3 022 401 people** (2023 census) — the second least densely populated on earth at about 2.7 persons/km² — organised by a west-to-east gradient from hyper-arid coast to semi-arid north-east. Five physiographic belts run roughly parallel to the Atlantic: the **Namib Desert**, the **Great Escarpment**, the **Central Plateau**, the **Kalahari**, and the northern/north-eastern **Bushveld**. Perennial rivers exist only on the borders; everything inland is ephemeral or underground. The north-central lowland — the **Cuvelai** drainage of shallow *iishana* channels — holds most of the country's people on some of its best soils, and it is where Okongo sits, on the sandy eastern edge of that system at about **1 150 m** elevation.

## 1. The five physiographic belts

### The Namib Desert
A continuous coastal desert running the full 1 572 km length of the country, between roughly 100 km and several hundred kilometres wide. It has endured arid or semi-arid conditions for **55–80 million years**, making it plausibly the world's oldest desert. Annual precipitation ranges from about **2 mm** in the driest core to about **200 mm** near the escarpment; the western Namib receives roughly 5 mm against the eastern Namib's 85 mm. The Namib Sand Sea contains some of the world's largest dunes, built from sand delivered by the Orange River and reworked by persistent south-westerly winds. Coastal temperatures are remarkably stable — generally **9–20 °C** — while inland summer days can exceed **45 °C** with freezing nights.

### The Great Escarpment
The land rises abruptly from the Namib to **over 2 000 m**. Summer winds are forced up and lose moisture here, so the escarpment is markedly more productive than the desert below it despite rocky, poorly developed soils. It is a belt of microhabitats and endemism, and — for the builder — a belt of **stone**: this is where hard rock, quarry aggregate and dimension stone actually exist in Namibia.

### The Central Plateau
Running north–south through the middle of the country at roughly 1 000–2 000 m, this holds Windhoek, most of the commercial farming, most of the industry and the bulk of the non-northern population. Arable land is only about **1 %** of Namibia, yet nearly a third of the population works in agriculture.

### The Kalahari
Shared with Botswana and South Africa, occupying the east and south-east. It is not a sand desert in the Namib sense but a vast **sand basin** — a Cenozoic blanket of aeolian quartz sand over older rock, with inselbergs breaking through. The southern Kalahari carries **linear dunes aligned NW–SE**, formed in two phases about **28 000 and 17 000 years ago**, over a dune field stretching **850 km north–south and up to 200 km east–west**.

### The northern Bushveld
North-eastern Namibia and the Zambezi Region receive around **400–650 mm/yr** and have cooler, more moderate temperatures, with woodland savanna rather than desert scrub. **Okongo lies at the transition** between the Cuvelai lowland and this wooded eastern sandveld — hence its name, from the Oshiwambo for a place or forest for hunting.

## 2. The coast and the Benguela Current

The **Benguela Current** is a cold, nutrient-rich, wind-driven upwelling system **200–300 km wide**, widening as it flows north. It is the engine of Namibia's coastal climate: it chills the onshore air, suppresses convection, and produces **fog on roughly one day in three along the coastal belt**. It also produces one of the world's richest fisheries. Crucially for the interior, the cold ocean feeds the **South Atlantic Anticyclone**, whose descending, drying air is a principal reason Namibia is dry at all.

## 3. Rivers: perennial, ephemeral, and absent

**Perennial rivers exist only on the national borders:** the Kunene and Okavango (Angola), the Zambezi and Kwando/Linyanti (Zambia/Botswana), and the Orange (South Africa). Namibia's actual abstractions as of 1999 were roughly 23 Mm³/yr from the Kunene at Ruacana, 49 Mm³ from the Orange at Noordoewer, 22 Mm³ from the Okavango at Rundu and 7 Mm³ from the Zambezi.

**Interior rivers are ephemeral** — the *omuramba* and *riviere* that flow for hours or days after a storm and are dry the rest of the year. FAO puts their 95 %-assured yield at about **96 Mm³/yr**, and only about half of the water impounded behind dams is usable because evaporation losses are so high.

**In the Cuvelai there are no rivers at all in the conventional sense** — only the *iishana* (singular *oshana*), shallow, grassy, interlinked channels that fill and drain seasonally.

For the whole country, the estimated total "safe yield" of water resources is about **660 Mm³/yr**: groundwater 300, ephemeral rivers 200, perennial rivers 150, and unconventional sources such as reclaimed wastewater 10. **Groundwater supports nearly 80 % of the country's area.**

## 4. The Cuvelai drainage system and the Owambo floodplain

The **Cuvelai** is a low-gradient inland delta straddling the Angola–Namibia border, draining south from the Angolan highlands into the **Owambo Basin** and ultimately into the **Etosha Pan**, from which nothing escapes. About **one third of the basin lies in Angola and the southern two thirds in Namibia**.

Its defining feature is a network of roughly **100 drainage channels** — the *iishana* — varying in width from **less than ten metres to over a kilometre**, which converge downstream into the **Omadhiya lakes**. Most channel beds are **impermeable clay or saline soil**, so infiltration is limited: water either evaporates or ponds. All water is contained within the Owambo Basin.

**Efundja / omafundja — the seasonal floods.** Over the 68 years from **1941 to 2021** the Atlas of Namibia records:

| Flow class | Years | Share |
|---|---|---|
| No significant flow | 23 | 34 % |
| Small flows | 14 | 21 % |
| Medium flows | 20 | 29 % |
| Major flows | 11 | 16 % |

Medium or major flows — *omafundja* — thus occurred **almost every second year on average**, with **major floods at an average interval of about six years**. Floods are driven as much by rain falling in Angola as by local rain, so a dry local season can still flood, and vice versa.

The Cuvelai holds most of Namibia's population because its soils, formed from wind-reworked alluvial silts, are **the most fertile in the region**. The Atlas describes exactly this mechanism at **17.49°S, 16.95°E** — squarely in the Ohangwena/Okongo latitude band: easterly winds scoured silts from ancient channels and pans and deposited them just west of the channels, where they mixed with sand; people settle and farm on those western margins, each smallholding visible from space as a pale clearing in the woodland.

> ⚠️ **Building implication.** Anywhere within or adjacent to an *oshana*, the risk is not river flooding but **shallow, wide, slow sheet inundation** that can persist for weeks, plus a locally raised water table and saline/sodic soils. Homesteads in the Cuvelai are traditionally sited on the slightly higher, sandy interfluves for precisely this reason. Follow that logic.

## 5. The Zambezi Region and the far north-east

The **Zambezi Region** (formerly Caprivi), **14 785 km²** and **142 373 people** (2023), is the wettest and greenest part of Namibia — the Caprivi Strip receives around **600 mm/yr**. It is a floodplain landscape between the Zambezi, Chobe, Kwando and Linyanti rivers, ecologically and architecturally distinct from the rest of the country. Its 2013–2023 population growth of **57.2 %** was the second highest of any region.

**Population distribution.** The four Cuvelai regions — Ohangwena, Omusati, Oshana and Oshikoto — together hold **1 142 503 people, about 37.8 % of Namibia's population, on roughly 10 % of its land area**. Add Kavango East and West and the northern belt approaches half the national population. Khomas (Windhoek) is the only genuinely urban concentration. The average household size in Ohangwena is **4.8 persons**, well above the national pattern and above Khomas's 3.6 — a fact with direct bearing on house planning.

## 7. Geographical profile of Ohangwena Region

- **Area** 10 706 km²; **population** 337 729 (2023); **density** 31.5/km² — by far the densest region outside Khomas and Oshana.
- **85.5 % rural, 14.5 % urban.** There is no large town: the capital **Eenhana** has 16 588 people, the border town **Helao Nafidi** (incorporating Oshikango) is the commercial gateway to Angola, and **Okongo** is a village of 3 564.
- **Borders Angola (Cunene Province, and Cubango Province in the far north-east) to the north**; Kavango West to the east, Oshikoto to the south, Oshana to the south-west, Omusati to the west.
- **Twelve constituencies:** Eenhana, Endola, Engela, Epembe, Ohangwena, Okongo, Omulonga (2003), Omundaungilo, Ondobe, Ongenga, Oshikango, Oshikunde (2013).
- **Settlements** are strung along the good tarred road from the Angolan border down to Ondangwa, where it meets the Oshakati–Tsumeb trunk road. The **eastern part of the region has good grazing but little water and poor communications**, which is why it remains thinly settled — and why Okongo, in the east, is a frontier village rather than a town.
- **Economy:** small-scale **mahangu** (pearl millet) cultivation and cattle keeping, rain-fed. Income sources at last detailed count: wages 24.9 %, pensions 23.8 %, farming 22.1 %, business 10.5 %. Oshiwambo is spoken in **97 %** of households; adult literacy is **84.6 %**. There are **234 schools with 90 703 pupils**.
- **Human Development Index (2017): 0.598**, ranking 11th of Namibia's 14 regions.

## 8. Geographical profile of Okongo

| Attribute | Value |
|---|---|
| Coordinates | **17°34′S, 17°13′E** |
| Elevation | **≈1 151 m** (PVGIS DEM); regionally the ground is a very flat sand plain around 1 100–1 200 m |
| Population | **3 564** (2023 census); 2 236 (2011) — 3.96 % annual growth |
| Status | Settlement area **1999**; proclaimed **village 2015**; five-seat village council |
| Role | District capital of **Okongo Constituency** |
| Access | On the **tarred road ~120 km east of Eenhana** continuing toward **Nkurenkuru** (Kavango West); **landing strip for small aircraft** |
| Services | Electricity, water and sanitation reticulated in the village; **Okongo District Hospital (62 beds)**; post office, supermarkets, banking, NaTIS office, community library (reopened January 2025 after a ~N$4 million refurbishment) |
| Schools | Two pre-primary, Okongo Primary School, Elia Weyulu Combined School, Oshela Senior Secondary School |
| Etymology | Oshiwambo for *a place or forest for hunting* |
| Notable | Birthplace of President **Netumbo Nandi-Ndaitwah** |

**Terrain and vegetation.** Okongo sits on the **deep Kalahari sandveld east of the main *oshana* network**. The terrain is exceptionally flat — regional relief across tens of kilometres is metres, not tens of metres — with subdued vegetated dune ridges and interdune flats inherited from earlier arid phases. Vegetation is **broadleaved tree-and-shrub savanna / dry woodland**, denser and taller than in the western Cuvelai: this is the "forest for hunting" of the name, and the reason the area retains commercial and household timber, firewood and thatching-grass resources that the treeless western Cuvelai has largely lost. Eenhana, 120 km west, is described as "situated in a subtropical forest".

**Settlement pattern.** Two overlaid patterns coexist:

1. **The dispersed homestead (*egumbo*) pattern** — individual family compounds scattered across the sandveld, each with its cleared *mahangu* field, sited on slightly higher ground, connected by sand tracks. This is the dominant rural form and it is what surrounds Okongo for tens of kilometres.
2. **The linear village core** — a strip of shops, offices, hospital, schools and serviced plots along the tarred road, which is where formal reticulated services stop.

The practical consequence is that **"in Okongo" can mean two entirely different infrastructure situations**: a serviced village plot with a NORED connection and a NamWater standpipe, or a homestead 12 km down a sand track with a borehole, a solar panel and no road maintenance. Establish which one applies before designing anything.

**Access and logistics.** Okongo is roughly **660 km from Windhoek** and about **1 000 km by road from Walvis Bay** — see domain 11 for the corridor detail. The tarred link to Eenhana and onward to Nkurenkuru means heavy delivery is feasible year-round to the village; the **last few kilometres of unsealed sand track to a rural homestead is the binding constraint**, and it is worst in February–April.

**History note.** The San settled the area in the 1900s, drawn by game and wild fruit; Bantu-speaking (Oshiwambo) migrants followed and displaced them. The eastern Ohangwena sandveld was a late frontier of Owambo settlement precisely because it had trees, game and sand but no *oshana* water.
