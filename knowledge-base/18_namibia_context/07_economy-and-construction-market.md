# The Namibian economy and construction market

**Summary.** Namibia is a **lower-middle-income, small, open, mineral-dependent economy** of about **US$14.69 bn nominal GDP (2025 IMF forecast)** and **US$4 413 GDP per capita**, with extreme inequality (**Gini 59.1**) and **36.9 % strict / 54.8 % broad unemployment (2023)**. The currency, the **Namibian dollar, is pegged 1:1 to the South African rand** and the rand is legal tender — so Namibia imports South African monetary policy and South African input costs with no exchange-rate buffer. **South Africa supplies 37.9 % of Namibia's imports**, and effectively all manufactured building materials. Construction is a small, volatile, government-capital-driven sector operating in a country with a housing deficit large enough that **more than 400 000 people live in informal housing**. For a builder in Ohangwena the practical translation is: **prices are rand prices plus freight; availability is a South African supply chain; and the local market is thin, informal and cash-based.**

## 1. The economy in brief

Namibia's economy is **undiversified and mineral-led on the export side, service-led on the domestic side**. Ores and minerals were **46.0 % of 2024 exports**, with metal ores and uranium alone **31.5 %**. The tertiary sector produces most of GDP (**55 % in 2025**) and the secondary sector — manufacturing, construction, utilities — only about **15 %**.

**Mining** contributed **14 % of GDP in 2025** and has averaged about **11 % since 1980**. Diamonds were the historic cornerstone but have been overtaken by metal ores (primarily gold) as diamond demand and prices softened in the 2020s. Uranium makes Namibia the **world's third-largest producer**.

**Manufacturing is structurally constrained**, and the reasons are given explicitly in the source: "a small domestic market, dependence on imported goods, limited supply of local capital, a widely dispersed population, a small skilled labour force and high relative wage rates, and subsidised competition from South Africa." **This is exactly why almost every manufactured building product used in Namibia is imported.**

**Tourism** is a major employer (18.2 % of employment on the 2008 WTTC estimate) and the main driver of high-quality construction outside the cities — lodges, camps and hospitality in remote locations. It is also the sector that has done most to develop a Namibian remote-site construction capability.

**Fishing** depends on the Benguela upwelling; catches have fallen from a **637 000 t peak in 2003 to about 373 000 t in 2023**. The sector was the subject of the **Fishrot** corruption scandal (2019), which cost two ministers their positions.

**Agriculture** occupies about half the population but only about **4.5 % of GDP** (crops 2.3 % + livestock 2.2 %) on **~1 % arable land**. Namibia is a net food importer. Livestock real value added fell **21.7 % in 2025** — drought and destocking are recurring.

## 2. The currency and the rand peg

The **Namibia dollar (NAD, N$)** has been the currency since **1993**, replacing the rand at par. It is **pegged 1:1 to the South African rand**, the **rand remains legal tender in Namibia**, and Namibia is part of the **Common Monetary Area**.

**Consequences for construction costs:**
1. **There is no exchange-rate buffer against South African price inflation.** When South African cement, steel or timber prices rise, Namibian prices rise the same day, in the same proportion.
2. **Namibian monetary policy shadows the South African Reserve Bank.** Interest rates — and therefore construction finance and mortgage costs — track South Africa.
3. **Rand weakness against the US dollar and euro raises the price of everything not made in the rand area** — which includes most machinery, electronics, ironmongery, solar equipment and specialist chemicals.
4. **Quotations may be in rand or Namibian dollars interchangeably.** Always confirm which, and always date the quote.
5. **Inflation** has run **6.07 % (2022), 5.89 % (2023), 4.25 % (2024), 3.5 % (2025)** — moderate, but enough that a **12-month-old rate is not a rate**. In a multi-year incremental build (see `06_building-culture-and-society.md`), material price escalation is a first-order planning variable, not a contingency line.

## 3. The construction sector

Construction sits inside the secondary sector, which was about **15–16 % of GDP** including manufacturing and utilities. It is characterised by:

- **Small absolute size.** In an economy of N$245 bn, the construction sector is a modest number of firms; the top tier is a handful of national contractors, mostly Windhoek-based, several with South African parentage or partnerships.
- **Extreme cyclicality driven by government capital budgets and mine construction.** A single uranium mine or a mass housing programme materially moves the sector. When the state capital budget is cut, the sector contracts hard.
- **A dominant informal tail.** The great majority of dwellings are built outside the formal contracting system entirely.
- **Skills concentrated in the centre and at the coast.** Ohangwena has artisans, not contractors.
- **High dependence on imports** for everything except aggregate, sand, block and labour.

> `needs-verification`: the construction sector's specific share of Namibian GDP, its employment numbers, and the number of registered contractors. The 2025 national accounts publish this but the figure was not extracted; the NSA Annual National Accounts 2025 PDF is the source to consult.

**Government capital expenditure** is the principal formal-sector demand driver: roads (Roads Authority / Roads Contractor Company), schools, clinics and hospitals, regional council offices, water schemes and rural electrification. In Ohangwena, essentially all formal construction is public. The **Okongo community library refurbishment at approximately N$4 million**, completed for a January 2025 reopening, is a representative example of the scale and type of publicly funded work in the constituency.

## 4. Housing programmes and the housing deficit

### The deficit
**More than 400 000 Namibians live in informal housing** — over 13 % of the population — and **41 % of the population is affected by multidimensional poverty**. The deficit is concentrated in the fast-growing urban periphery (Windhoek's Havana and Goreangab, Oshakati, Rundu, Walvis Bay) and in unserviced rural housing across the north.

Three structural causes:
1. **Land servicing is the bottleneck**, not construction. Un-serviced land cannot be legally built on and cannot be financed.
2. **Incomes do not reach mortgage thresholds.** With broad unemployment at 54.8 % and a Gini of 59.1, the great majority of households cannot service a conventional home loan.
3. **Communal tenure is not bankable** (see `06_building-culture-and-society.md`), which excludes most rural households from formal finance regardless of income.

### The National Housing Enterprise (NHE)
The state housing parastatal, responsible for developing and selling subsidised housing to lower- and middle-income households. `needs-verification`: NHE's current delivery numbers, income bands, product types and pricing were not retrievable.

### The Mass Housing Development Programme (MHDP)
Launched in the 2013 era as a large-scale national housing delivery programme with an ambitious unit target, implemented largely through NHE. It became one of Namibia's best-known public-sector failures: the programme was widely reported to have suffered **cost overruns, houses priced far above the target market's reach, quality defects, incomplete and abandoned units, and procurement irregularities**, and it was suspended and restructured.

> ⚠️ `needs-verification`: **the MHDP's target unit count, actual delivery, budget, unit costs and the specifics of its problems could not be confirmed from a primary source in this session.** The characterisation above reflects widely reported accounts. Obtain an NSA, Auditor-General or Ministry of Urban and Rural Development source before citing any figure.

**The design lesson from MHDP, which is safe to state:** a top-down, standardised, contractor-delivered housing model priced above what the target population can pay, and delivered without the land servicing to support it, does not solve a housing deficit. The alternatives that do work in Namibia are incremental and community-led.

### The Shack Dwellers Federation of Namibia (SDFN)
A federation of community savings groups, supported by the Namibia Housing Action Group, which pools members' savings, negotiates with local authorities for land, and builds houses collectively at a fraction of formal cost. Its method — **savings first, land second, services third, house fourth, all community-managed** — is the demonstrated low-cost delivery model in Namibia and has secured tenure for large numbers of households.

> `needs-verification`: SDFN's founding date, membership, savings group count, houses completed, land secured and typical house cost were not retrievable. These figures are published by SDFN and NHAG and should be obtained.

## 5. What this means for costs and availability in Ohangwena

### Cost structure
1. **Ex-works price is a South African price.** Cement, reinforcement, roof sheeting, timber, board products, sanitaryware, ironmongery, electrical and plumbing goods are almost all South African or imported through South Africa. Budget accordingly and track rand pricing.
2. **Add freight, and add it as a step function.** See domain 11. Okongo is roughly **660 km from Windhoek and ~1 000 km by road from Walvis Bay**. Freight is a large percentage on low-value-density materials (cement, block, aggregate) and negligible on high-value-density ones (ironmongery, electronics).
3. **Make the heavy things locally.** Block-making on site is often the correct answer in Ohangwena precisely because blocks are the worst thing to freight. Sand is free; cement and aggregate are the imported components.
4. **Labour is cheap and abundant; skill is not.** Broad unemployment of 54.8 % means casual labour is available at low cost; a genuinely competent bricklayer or carpenter is scarce and worth paying above the local rate to retain.
5. **Assume 5–8 % annual escalation** on a multi-year programme unless you have better data, based on the 2022–2025 inflation path, and note that construction material inflation typically runs above headline CPI.
6. **Cash is the operating currency.** Rural transactions are largely cash; formal credit terms with suppliers require a Windhoek or Oshakati trading relationship.

### Availability
- **Ondangwa, Oshakati and Ongwediva** form the northern commercial cluster and are the nearest real builders' merchants to Okongo — roughly 180–220 km west.
- **Eenhana** (~120 km) has hardware and general trade but a narrow range.
- **Helao Nafidi / Oshikango** is the Angolan border trade town and a significant commercial node.
- **Okongo itself** has supermarkets and banking but should not be assumed to stock construction materials beyond basics.
- **Lead times are long and variable.** The controlling risk is lead-time *variance*, not length (see domain 11).

### The market's structural risks
- **A construction downturn removes contractors from the market**, and remote-region work is the first to be dropped.
- **A mining boom absorbs artisans and plant**, and rural rates rise.
- **Public capital budget cuts** stop regional projects mid-stream; a half-built public facility is a common sight.
- **Rand volatility** transmits directly into material costs with no lag.
