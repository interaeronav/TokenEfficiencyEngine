# Namibian architecture — vernacular, colonial, modern and contemporary

**Summary.** Namibia's built environment carries four distinct architectural inheritances that barely speak to each other: **indigenous building traditions** of extraordinary climatic intelligence and almost no permanence; **German colonial architecture (1884–1915)** — Wilhelminian, neo-Romanesque and Jugendstil — imported wholesale and largely indifferent to climate; **South African–era modernism and pragmatism (1915–1990)**, including the mission stations and the military infrastructure of the north; and a **post-independence architecture** split between imported state monumentality and a small, serious current of climate-responsive, locally-grounded practice. For a builder in Ohangwena, the useful lesson is that **the indigenous traditions are the only ones that were actually designed for this climate**, and that almost everything they knew — shade before enclosure, ventilated roofs, compound rather than single-volume planning, cheap replaceable envelope over expensive permanent frame — remains correct.

## 1. Indigenous building traditions

### 1.1 The Owambo homestead (*egumbo*) — the tradition that matters at Okongo

The Owambo homestead is not a house. It is a **walled compound of many small single-purpose structures**, and understanding that distinction is the key to designing well in Ohangwena.

**Form.** A complex of huts **surrounded by a fence of large vertical poles linked by two horizontal poles** — a palisade. The internal layout forms a **maze, with two gates**. Each structure has a defined purpose: the ***ondjugo*** (a woman's hut), the ***epata*** (kitchen), grain stores, sleeping huts for different family members, cattle enclosures, a reception space near the entrance.

**Construction technique.**
- **The palisade** is the primary work: hundreds of straight timber poles (traditionally *omusati*/mopane and other hardwoods), set vertically in the sand, close-spaced, lashed to horizontal rails. It defines the property, controls livestock, screens the interior socially, provides security, and — critically — **breaks the wind and casts shade on the ground within**.
- **Huts** are round or oval, with a ring of timber posts, a woven or lashed roof frame, and a **thatch of grass**. Walls are timber, woven branches, or earth-plastered wattle.
- **Grain stores (*oshisha*/*eshisha*)** are raised woven baskets of thick construction on legs or a platform, capped with a removable thatch roof — a highly evolved piece of engineering for keeping mahangu dry and away from rodents in a climate with 130 mm in one month and none in the next six.
- **Materials are local, renewable and replaceable**: timber, grass, earth, cattle dung. Nothing is intended to last a century.

**Climate intelligence in this tradition:**
1. **Shade is made before enclosure is made.** The palisade and the trees create shaded outdoor space, and most living happens there. The huts are for sleeping, storage and shelter from rain — not for daytime occupation. This is the correct response to a climate with 2 275 kWh/m²/yr of irradiance.
2. **Dispersed small volumes beat one large volume.** Each structure is small, single-storey, thin in plan, easy to ventilate, and shaded by its neighbours and by the palisade.
3. **Separated functions manage heat.** The kitchen fire is in its own structure, not in the sleeping hut. In a climate where October afternoons hit 37 °C, keeping combustion out of the living space is elementary and was done as a matter of course.
4. **Lightweight, high-emissivity roofs.** Thatch is an excellent insulator, radiates heat well at night, and is cheap to replace.
5. **The maze plan is a wind and dust filter** as well as a social and defensive device: the offset entry blocks a straight line of driven sand from the easterly wind.
6. **Siting on the interfluve.** Homesteads sit on the slightly higher sandy ground with the *mahangu* field on the fertile margin — the flood logic described in `01_geography-and-regions.md`.

**What the tradition does not solve:** durability, security against modern threats, dust ingress into stored goods, and the fire risk of thatch. These are precisely the reasons households migrate to corrugated iron and concrete block — and lose the climatic performance in the process. **The modern Ohangwena house is often thermally worse than the homestead it replaced.** That is the central design problem in the region.

### 1.2 Herero and Nama *pontoks*

The **pontok** (from Khoekhoe, via Afrikaans) is the dome or beehive dwelling of the Nama and, in adapted form, of the Herero.

- **Nama** *|haru oms* / matjieshuis: a **hemispherical frame of bent saplings** lashed into a lattice, covered with **woven reed mats**. Its genius is that the reed mats **swell shut when wet** (making the dwelling rainproof) and **shrink apart when dry** (making it a ventilated screen in the hot season) — a self-regulating envelope. It is also **fully demountable and transportable**, which suited a pastoral life.
- **Herero** dwellings adopted the dome form with a frame of saplings, plastered with **earth and cattle dung** and often thatched. The **holy fire (*okuruuo*)** occupies the centre of the homestead, with the chief's house and the fire on a defined axis toward the cattle kraal. The Herero social plan is thus **cosmological before it is functional** — a lesson in the fact that vernacular plans encode meaning, not just performance.

**Climate lesson:** the Nama mat house is one of the world's most elegant answers to a hot arid climate with a short wet season — a **variable-permeability envelope**. Contemporary equivalents are operable screens, adjustable louvres and shutter systems.

### 1.3 Himba dwellings

Himba live in an ***onganda*** — a homestead described as "a small family-village, consisting of a circular hamlet of huts and work shelters that surround an *okuruwo* (sacred ancestral fire) and a kraal for the sacred livestock". Both the fire and the livestock are tied to veneration of ancestors.

**Construction:** a frame of **mopane wood** plastered by the women and girls with a traditional mixture of **red clay soil and cow manure** as a binding agent. Conical or dome-roofed, small, low-doored.

**Climate lesson:** the earth-and-dung plaster gives **thermal mass and a sealed, dust-proof, insect-resistant surface** from materials that cost nothing but labour, and it is renewed annually. The **circular plan around a central focus** creates a shaded, wind-sheltered communal court — the same logic as the Owambo palisade at a different scale.

### 1.4 San shelters

San hunter-gatherer shelters are the minimum case: a **hemispherical or lean-to frame of bent branches thatched with grass**, built in an hour or two, used for days or weeks, abandoned without loss. Camps are organised as a ring of shelters with entrances facing a common central space and hearths outside the entrances.

**Climate lesson:** the shelter is understood as **shade and wind-break first, rain shelter second**. Almost all activity is outdoors, in shade. In a climate with six rainless months this is not primitive — it is correct.

## 2. German colonial architecture, 1884–1915

Thirty-one years produced a remarkably complete set of European building types, executed at a level of finish that still dominates the tourist image of Namibia.

**Windhoek.** The **Alte Feste** — designed by Captain **Curt von François**, foundation laid **18 October 1890**, redesigned repeatedly and finally completed in **1915** — is the oldest building in the capital: a whitewashed fort with corner towers, built to house the Schutztruppe and to sit as a buffer between Nama and Herero territory. It never saw military action; it became a school hostel in 1935, a National Monument in 1957, and later the National Museum.

The **Christuskirche** (1907–1910), by **Gottlieb Redecker**, is the set piece: a **mixture of neo-Romanesque, Art Nouveau and Gothic Revival**, built of **quartz sandstone quarried near the Avis Dam**, with a **24 m spire**, a **Carrara marble portico** imported from Italy, a clock and part of the roof shipped from Germany, and **three bronze bells cast by Franz Schilling** inscribed *Ehre sei Gott in der Höhe*, *Friede auf Erden* and *Den Menschen ein Wohlgefallen*. Kaiser Wilhelm II gave the stained glass. It was originally called the *Church of Peace* — built as a symbol of peace immediately after the genocide, which tells you a great deal about the colonial imagination.

A footnote with a design moral: in the late 1990s a tourist noticed that **all the stained-glass windows had been installed with their sun-protection shields on the inside**. Over two years every window element was restored and refitted with the shield facing outward. **Shading works on the outside of the glass; the entire history of colonial building in Namibia is summarised by getting this backwards for ninety years.**

**Swakopmund.** Founded by von François in **1892** as the German port — Walvis Bay being British — the town is now the country's densest concentration of **19th- and early-20th-century German colonial architecture**. The **Woermannhaus (1906)** with its prominent **"Damara tower"** (now the public library) and the **Altes Gefängnis (1909)** by **Heinrich Bause** are the landmarks. The first settlers in 1893 had to **dig caves in the sand** for shelter; Swakopmund got municipal status in **1909**. It also housed a **concentration camp for Herero, where approximately 2 000 people died**.

**Lüderitz and Kolmanskop.** Lüderitz carries the country's best **Jugendstil / art nouveau** domestic architecture, built on the diamond wealth after 1908. **Kolmanskop**, 10 km inland, was built from 1908 "in the architectural style of a German town" with a hospital, ballroom, power station, school, skittle alley, theatre and sports hall, casino, ice factory, **the first X-ray station in the southern hemisphere**, and a tram — served by a 1.5 MW power station in Lüderitz then thought to be the largest in Africa. Diamond depletion and the 1928 discovery of far richer beach terraces 270 km south emptied it; it was **abandoned in 1956** and is now the country's most photographed ruin, its Edwardian interiors preserved by the arid climate and invaded by dune sand.

**Climate assessment of the colonial legacy.** Thick masonry walls and high ceilings gave real thermal mass and worked reasonably in Windhoek's moderate climate. But the **steeply pitched northern-European roofs**, the **small windows in the wrong orientations**, the **absence of shading**, and the **absence of verandas** in many buildings all show that the climate was being resisted rather than used. The one genuinely climate-derived colonial element — the deep **veranda/stoep**, borrowed from Cape and broader colonial practice — is the element most worth keeping.

## 3. The South African era, 1915–1990

Three parallel building cultures:

1. **Institutional modernism.** From the 1930s and especially the 1950s–70s, South African administrative, educational and commercial modernism: reinforced concrete frames, brise-soleil, ribbon windows, flat or low-pitched roofs, face brick. Windhoek's CBD and the government building stock are largely this. At its best it was genuinely climate-aware — concrete sunbreakers, deep reveals, cross-ventilated single-banked plans. At its worst it introduced the **unshaded curtain wall** to a country with 2 300 kWh/m²/yr.

2. **The mission and government station in the north.** Fired brick or concrete block, corrugated iron roofs, wide verandas, detached pavilions in a compound. Churches, schools, clinics, teachers' houses. This is the **first permanent architecture of Ohangwena** and it established the local model of "a proper building": rectangular, iron-roofed, rendered, painted. The Finnish mission stations at Ondangwa (1890), Olukonda, Oniipa and Eenhana (1936) are its origin points.

3. **Military and strategic infrastructure.** The war built the north's hard infrastructure: the **Ondangwa air base and staging area**, the tarred road pushed from Otjiwarongo through Ondangwa to Oshakati during the 1970s, the base at **Eenhana** (a military centre "without public infrastructure" until independence), and the airstrips and fortified posts along the Angolan border. This is why a village of 3 500 people like Okongo has a tarred road and a landing strip.

Also of this era: **Katutura** (1959–), the forced-removal township at Windhoek, and the standardised low-cost township house — a two- or four-room, iron-roofed, uninsulated block box that is the direct ancestor of most contemporary Namibian low-cost housing, and thermally the worst building type in the country.

## 4. Post-independence architecture, 1990–

**State monumentality.** The most visible post-1990 buildings in Windhoek were designed and built by **Mansudae Overseas Projects** of North Korea — the **Independence Memorial Museum** (inaugurated 20 March 2014, the 24th anniversary of independence), **Heroes' Acre**, the **Okahandja Military Museum** and a new **State House**. The Museum is a five-storey triangular glass structure, planned with **four equal 40 m walls reaching at least 40 m**, with a glass-fronted lift on its face, flanked by a bronze statue of Sam Nujoma and a Genocide Memorial inscribed "Their Blood Waters Our Freedom". It stands between the **Christuskirche** and the **Alte Feste**, and — as the historian Reinhard Kössler put it — has "broken up the ensemble of German monuments in Windhoek". Its socialist-realist idiom was read as symbolic of "the modernist, post-colonial state". Namibians call it the *potjie*, the coffee pot or the molar tooth.

A fully glazed tower in Windhoek is, thermally, an odd choice; it is the clearest example in the country of architecture as political statement over environmental performance.

**The Supreme Court of Namibia** (built **1994–1996**) is the counter-example. It was designed "in a north African style **in order to avoid resemblance of European colonial buildings**" and is described as **Windhoek's only post-independence building erected in an African architectural style** — courtyards, thick walls, small deep openings, arcades, an introverted plan. Whatever one thinks of the borrowing from north Africa rather than from southern African precedent, it is a serious attempt to answer both the political and the climatic question with the same move.

**Institutional and financial architecture.** The **Bank of Namibia**, the campuses of the **University of Namibia (UNAM)** and the **Namibia University of Science and Technology (NUST)**, and the regional council and hospital building programmes are the bulk of post-independence public construction. UNAM and NUST have expanded to satellite campuses including in the northern regions — **Eenhana now hosts university satellite campuses**, which is a significant fact for the availability of technical skills in Ohangwena.

**Housing.** The dominant post-independence building activity by volume is housing — National Housing Enterprise, the Mass Housing Development Programme, Shack Dwellers Federation self-build, and the vast informal sector housing more than 400 000 people. See `07_economy-and-construction-market.md`.

## 5. Contemporary Namibian practice and its concerns

The professional community is small — a few dozen practices, concentrated in Windhoek and Swakopmund, regulated through the Namibian Council for Architects and Quantity Surveyors and represented by the Namibia Institute of Architects. Its recurring concerns are:

- **Climate response** as the defining local question — shading, roof design, thermal mass, ventilation.
- **Water** — rainwater harvesting, greywater, water-efficient fittings in the driest country in sub-Saharan Africa.
- **Off-grid and solar** — in a country with world-class irradiance and a large unelectrified rural population.
- **Local material and local labour** — reducing imported content in a market where nearly everything manufactured is South African.
- **Appropriate technology and self-build** — designing for incremental construction by owner-builders rather than for a general contractor.
- **Decolonising the discipline** — what a Namibian architecture that is neither German pastiche nor imported modernism actually looks like.

### Vernacular-informed contemporary work

The best-known practitioner of a low-impact, materially inventive, climate-driven Namibian architecture is **Nina Maritz** (Nina Maritz Architects, Windhoek). Her work is associated with recycled and reclaimed materials, passive and off-grid environmental systems, lightweight structures, and buildings for conservation and community clients in remote settings — including, by widespread attribution, the **Habitat Research and Development Centre in Katutura, Windhoek**, a demonstration building for appropriate technology and sustainable construction.

## 6. What each tradition teaches for building at Okongo

| Tradition | The transferable lesson |
|---|---|
| **Owambo homestead** | Build a **compound, not a house.** Shade the ground first. Separate hot functions. Use the palisade/screen as a wind, dust, privacy and shade device. Site on high ground. Plan for incremental addition |
| **Owambo grain store** | Raise stored goods off the ground; ventilate under; cap with a removable, replaceable roof |
| **Nama pontok** | A **variable-permeability envelope** — open in the dry season, closed in the wet — outperforms a fixed one. Operable shutters and screens, not sealed glazing |
| **Herero homestead** | The plan carries meaning. A house here has a social geometry (entrance, reception, seniority, hearth) that is not negotiable for the sake of a tidy layout |
| **Himba onganda** | Earth-and-dung plaster gives mass, sealing and dust resistance at zero material cost. Renewable annual maintenance is a legitimate strategy |
| **San shelter** | Shade before enclosure. Life happens outdoors, in the shade, in this climate |
| **German colonial** | Mass and high ceilings help; **imported roof forms and unshaded glass do not.** Put the shading on the outside |
| **Colonial veranda / stoep** | The single best borrowed element — a deep, shaded, ventilated outdoor room on the sun-facing side |
| **Mission station** | The pavilion-in-a-compound plan with wide verandas is the right permanent-building type for the north |
| **Township house** | The cautionary example. Uninsulated block walls under an unventilated iron roof is the region's default and its worst-performing building |
| **Supreme Court** | Courtyards, thick walls, small deep openings and an introverted plan are a defensible modern institutional response |
| **Independence Memorial Museum** | Large-scale glazing in this climate is a political gesture, not an environmental one |
