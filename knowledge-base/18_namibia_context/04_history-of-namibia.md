# A history of Namibia, with the Owambo and Ohangwena story

**Summary.** Namibia's history is unusually compressed and unusually violent. Human occupation runs back at least 25 000 years; Bantu-speaking agropastoralists arrived over the last two millennia; European contact began in **1485** and colonisation only in **1884**. In the thirty-one years of German rule the colonial state carried out the **Herero and Nama genocide (1904–1908)**, killing perhaps **24 000–65 000 Herero (50–70 % of the people)** and **about 10 000 Nama (about 50 %)**. South Africa occupied the territory in **1915**, administered it under a League of Nations **Class C mandate** from **1920**, imposed apartheid and the **Odendaal Plan** homelands from the 1960s, and fought a **twenty-three-year war (1966–1989)** against SWAPO's PLAN — a war fought overwhelmingly in and from **Owamboland**. Independence came on **21 March 1990**. The current president, **Netumbo Nandi-Ndaitwah**, sworn in on **21 March 2025**, is from **Okongo**.

## 1. Precolonial peoples and polities

**San.** The oldest continuous population, hunter-gatherers across the whole subcontinent. The Huns Mountains settlement dates to about 25 000 BC and the Brandberg paintings to about 2000 BC. San communities were the **first settlers of the Okongo area in the 1900s**, drawn by game and wild fruit, and were subsequently displaced by Bantu-speaking migrants — a very late instance of a process that had run for two millennia elsewhere.

**Nama** (Khoekhoe-speaking pastoralists) settled southern Africa and Namibia from around the first century BC, occupying the south and centre with cattle and sheep. **Damara** (also Khoekhoe-speaking, but with a distinct history) occupied the central highlands and escarpment. **Herero**, Bantu-speaking cattle pastoralists, migrated into Namibia in the **17th century** from the east African lakes region; the **Himba** of the Kaokoveld are a Herero-speaking group who retained a transhumant pastoral life. **Kavango** peoples settled the Okavango River corridor with riverine agriculture and fishing.

### The Owambo kingdoms
The **Ovambo** (Owambo, Aawambo) are today about **half of Namibia's population** — roughly 2.2 million people including southern Angola. They are not one polity but a family of related Bantu-speaking groups, each historically a hereditary kingdom with its own dialect:

| Kingdom | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Ondonga** (Aandonga) | Capital **Ondangwa**, palace at Onambango. Speaks Oshindonga. Matrilineal succession alternating between two lines (Epale and Onethika): the oldest surviving brother of the king, then his sisters' sons. King Immanuel Kauluma Elifas died March 2019; **Fillemon Shuumbwa Nangolo** recognised as successor |
| **Oukwanyama / Uukwanyama** (Ovakwanyama) | The **largest** group; territory split in 1884 between Portuguese and German spheres. Defeated the Portuguese at the **Battle of the Cunene, 1904**. Capital Oihole; Oukwanyama Kingdom and King Mandume Museum at **Omhedi**. **Ohangwena Region is predominantly Kwanyama** |
| **Uukwambi** (Aakwambi) | Capital **Elim**. Last king **Iipumbu yaTshilongo**, deposed by the South African authorities in **1932**; the senior office is now *elenga enene* (head chief). **Oshakati** lies within its traditional borders. Sam Nujoma was the son of a Kwambi princess |
| Ongandjera, Uukwaluudhi, Uukolonkadhi, Ombalantu, Eunda, Ombandja | Smaller western and north-western kingdoms |

**Social structure.** Descent is **matrilineal**: chieftainship passes through the daughters' children, not the sons'. Polygyny was accepted, first wives holding senior status. The economy combined **mahangu** (pearl millet), sorghum (*iilyavala*) and beans (*omakunde*) with cattle, goats and sheep kept largely for milk, supplemented by hunting, fishing and gathering. Religion centred on a supreme being, **Kalunga**, with sacred fire ritual; today about **97 % identify as Lutheran**, with syncretic retention of the ritual fire and Kalunga.

**Why this matters for building:** the Traditional Authorities descended from these kingdoms are **still the bodies that allocate communal land** (see `06_building-culture-and-society.md`). In Ohangwena that means the **Oukwanyama Traditional Authority** for most of the region, and the relevant authority for the Okongo area specifically must be confirmed on the ground.

## 2. Early trade and mission contact

Diogo Cão reached the Skeleton Coast in **1485** and Bartolomeu Dias followed, but the coast offered no water and no harbour and Europeans did not penetrate. Walvis Bay passed to Dutch control in **1793** and to Britain, which annexed it to the Cape, in **1878** — a fact that shaped German colonial planning a decade later, since it meant Germany had to build **Swakopmund** as an artificial substitute port.

Missions arrived before administrations. The **London Missionary Society** from 1805, the **Rhenish Mission Society** from the 1840s, and — decisively for the north — the **Finnish Missionary Society from the 1870s**. **Martti Rautanen** developed the Oshindonga literary language; the Finns established Lutheranism as the dominant faith of the Owambo, culminating in the **Ovambo-Kavango Evangelical Lutheran Church in 1954**. The Finns' first stations were in Ondonga because the other kingdoms rebuffed them; **August Pettinen founded the Ondangwa station in 1890**, about 15 km north-west of Olukonda.

The mission legacy is architectural as well as religious: mission stations introduced fired brick, lime, corrugated iron, rectilinear planning, the church, the school and the clinic to the north — and they remain the oldest permanent buildings in most Owambo settlements.

## 3. German colonisation, 1884–1915

Bismarck declared **German South West Africa** in 1884 after Lüderitz's purchase. Governor **Theodor Leutwein** (from 1894) pursued a policy of treaties and division; **Hendrik Witbooi** signed a protection treaty the same year. Settler numbers rose from **3 700 in 1903 to 13 000 by 1910**.

Crucially, **German control never extended effectively into Ovamboland.** The far north was governed indirectly, through the kingdoms, and the German colonial state's violence fell on the centre and south.

### The Herero and Nama genocide, 1904–1908
In January 1904 Herero forces attacked remote farms and killed about **150 Germans**. Leutwein was replaced by **General Lothar von Trotha**, who crushed the Herero at the **Battle of Waterberg** and drove the survivors into the waterless **Omaheke**, sealing the waterholes. A subsequent extermination order and a system of concentration camps followed; the Nama rose in late 1904 and were destroyed in turn.

**Death toll:** **24 000–65 000 Herero — 50–70 % of the population** — and **about 10 000 Nama, roughly 50 %**. Camps operated at Swakopmund, where approximately **2 000 Herero died** in forced labour, and on **Shark Island** at Lüderitz.

Descendants of von Trotha apologised to Herero chiefs on **7 October 2007**. In **May 2021** Germany agreed to pay Namibia **€1.1 billion** in recognition of the genocide — a settlement that remains politically contested in Namibia.

**Diamonds** were found at Kolmanskop in **1908** by railway worker **Zacharias Lewala**, who showed the stone to inspector **August Stauch**; the German Empire immediately declared the *Sperrgebiet*.

## 4. South African occupation, mandate and the conquest of the north, 1915–1960

South African forces took the colony in **1915**. In Ovamboland the British/South African administration arrived the same year, chose **Ondangwa** as its seat, and **raised the Union Jack there on Christmas Day 1915**. Major **C. N. Manning** was the first Resident Commissioner; Lieutenant **C. H. L. Hahn** — grandson of the missionary Carl Hugo Hahn — then ran Ovamboland as its senior administrator **for thirty years**, and shaped the relationship between the Finnish missions and the colonial state. The government bought the Ondangwa station from the Finns in **1921**.

**The conquest of Oukwanyama.** **Mandume ya Ndemufayo** (1894–1917) took the Kwanyama throne in **1911**, moved the royal residence to **Ondjiva** (now Angola), expelled Portuguese traders over price inflation, banned the picking of unripe fruit as a drought measure, restricted the use of firearms, imposed harsh penalties for rape and **allowed women to own cattle** for the first time. He resisted both Portuguese and South African encroachment, and on **6 February 1917** he died under South African attack — by suicide or machine-gun fire, accounts differ. He is a national hero in both Namibia and Angola. His defeat ended independent Owambo political authority; **Uukwambi's Iipumbu yaTshilongo was deposed in 1932**, completing the process.

**League of Nations mandate.** From **1920** South Africa administered South West Africa under a **Class C mandate**. When the League dissolved in 1946 South Africa refused to place the territory under UN trusteeship, and the resulting legal struggle ran for four decades.

## 5. Apartheid, the Odendaal Plan and the homelands

The killing of **eleven protesters in Windhoek in 1959** — during the forced removal of residents from the Old Location to Katutura — is conventionally taken as the birth of organised Namibian nationalism.

The **Odendaal Commission** (established 1962) recommended partition of the territory into ethnic homelands, with the balance — including most of the agriculturally viable land — reserved for whites. **Ten bantustans** were created.

**Ovamboland**, set up in **1968**, was "the first fully functional Bantustan in South West Africa", granted nominal "self-government" in **1973**. Its labour regime was the mechanism that shaped modern northern Namibian society:

- **Men could leave only on a labour contract, typically of 16 months.**
- **Women faced complete restriction on movement out of the homeland.**
- **Ondangwa** was the recruitment and transit point for contract workers going to and from the mines — for example to **Tsumeb, 270 km south-east**.

From **1980 to 1989** Ovamboland was converted into a non-geographic ethnic "Representative Authority", and the homeland was **abolished in May 1989**. Today more than half of Namibia's population lives in that former homeland area — **about 6 % of the national territory**.

> This is the direct historical source of two enduring features of building in Ohangwena: **the migrant-remittance economy** that funds most rural house construction, and **the very high rural population density** on a small area of communal land.

## 6. The liberation struggle and the border war, 1966–1989

**SWAPO** grew out of the Ovamboland People's Organisation and the contract labour grievance. On **26 August 1966** its military wing **PLAN** (People's Liberation Army of Namibia) began guerrilla operations; the same year the **UN General Assembly revoked South Africa's mandate (Resolution 2145)**.

The **1971–72 general strike** by **13 000 contract workers — a quarter of the entire working population** — was a decisive demonstration that the contract system was politically unsustainable.

South Africa responded with the **Turnhalle Constitutional Conference (1975)**, excluding SWAPO, and with an intensifying military campaign. The **Western Contact Group** (Canada, France, West Germany, UK, US) formed in **1977**, and **UN Security Council Resolution 435** was presented in **1978** — the settlement plan that would eventually deliver independence, twelve years later. South Africa held unilateral elections in 1978, boycotted by SWAPO.

**The war was fought in the north.** Ovamboland, Kavango and the Caprivi were the operational theatre; PLAN infiltrated from Angola and South African forces mounted cross-border operations into Angola. This militarisation is written directly into the physical fabric of Ohangwena:

- **Ondangwa's airport** made it a major **SADF staging area**; road and rail were upgraded specifically to move military vehicles.
- **Before 1970 the highway was unpaved beyond Otjiwarongo**; by the end of that decade the tarmac ran through Ondangwa to Oshakati. The northern tarred network is a war artefact.
- **Eenhana was an SADF military base "without public infrastructure"** before independence — it was proclaimed a settlement only in **1992** and a town in **1999**.
- The **airstrip at Okongo** and the tarred road east from Eenhana belong to the same logic.

The 1988 Reagan–Gorbachev process linked Cuban withdrawal from Angola to South African withdrawal from Namibia; the **Tripartite Accord** was signed in New York on **22 December 1988**. Resolution 435 implementation began on **1 April 1989** with **UNTAG** under **Martti Ahtisaari** — and immediately went wrong, with roughly **375 PLAN fighters killed** in fighting during the transition. **1 600 Koevoet members** were demobilised in October 1989.

**November 1989 elections:** 98 % registration; **SWAPO 57 %**, DTA **29 %**. The Constitution was adopted on **9 February 1990** and **independence came on 21 March 1990**, with Sam Nujoma sworn in and 147 countries represented.

## 7. Post-independence, 1990 to the present

Namibia inherited an extraordinarily unequal country. At independence **3 500 farmers, almost entirely white and about 0.2 % of the population, owned roughly 50 % of the agricultural land**. Land reform has proceeded on a "willing buyer, willing seller" basis and slowly: from 1990 to 2007 an independent report found approximately **800 black farmers resettled** on about 12 % of commercial farmland — less than 1 % per year. In the **2023 census, 53 773 Namibians identified as white — 1.8 % of the population.**

**Political continuity.** Nujoma won **76.34 % (1994)** and, after a constitutional amendment in 1998 permitting a third term, **76.82 % (1999)**. **Hifikepunye Pohamba** succeeded in 2005; **Hage Geingob** won **87 % (2014)** and **56.3 % (2019)**, died on **4 February 2024**, and was succeeded by **Nangolo Mbumba**. **Netumbo Nandi-Ndaitwah** was sworn in on **21 March 2025** with **58 %** — the first female president, **and a native of Okongo**.

**Development record.** Namibia is a lower-middle-income country with GDP per capita around **US$4 413 (2025)** but a **Gini coefficient of 59.1** — among the world's highest. **41 % of the population is affected by multidimensional poverty** and **more than 400 000 people live in informal housing**. Unemployment was **36.9 % (strict) / 54.8 % (broad) in 2023**.

Infrastructure has expanded substantially — the Trans-Caprivi and Trans-Kalahari corridors, the Walvis Bay port expansion, rural electrification and water schemes — but service coverage in Ohangwena remains among the thinnest in the country.

## 8. The Ohangwena and Okongo story specifically

Ohangwena's history is the eastern Kwanyama history, and it runs like this:

1. **Kwanyama kingdom**, straddling what is now the Angolan border, split arbitrarily by the **1884 Berlin Conference** into Portuguese and German spheres. The border still cuts through a single people; **97 % of Ohangwena households speak Oshiwambo**, and family, trade and church networks run across it.
2. **Mission expansion eastwards.** **Eenhana** was founded around **New Year's Day 1930** by the Reverend **Paulus Hamutenya** — one of the first seven Ovambo ordained as pastors, at Oniipa in 1925 — who moved east from crowded **Edundja** to found a new settlement for the Kwanyama in the woods **60 km east of Engela**. He built a church there in **1932** and died the same year. The mission station proper was founded by the nurse **Linda Helenius in 1936**, and Eenhana was the centre of medical care for eastern Oukwanyama for decades. The name means *calves*, after the animals that watered at the pan.
3. **The eastern frontier.** The sandveld east of the *oshana* network — Okongo's country — was settled late because it had trees, game and sand but no surface water. The **San lived there in the 1900s**, drawn by wildlife and fruit; Oshiwambo-speaking migrants followed and displaced them. The name **Okongo** means *a place or forest for hunting*.
4. **Militarisation, 1966–1989.** The Angolan border made Ohangwena a front line. Eenhana was a base; the tarred roads and airstrips date from this era; and the region's people bore the direct costs of the war.
5. **Post-independence local government.** Eenhana: settlement 1992, town 1999. **Okongo: settlement area 1999, proclaimed village 2015**, with a five-seat council. Its population grew from **2 236 (2011) to 3 564 (2023)** — nearly 4 % a year. The **Okongo District Hospital (62 beds)**, the schools, the NaTIS office and the community library (reopened January 2025 after a roughly **N$4 million** refurbishment) are all products of this thirty-five-year period of state building.
6. **National prominence.** President **Netumbo Nandi-Ndaitwah** is from Okongo — which, in Namibian political practice, is not an irrelevant fact for public investment in the constituency.
