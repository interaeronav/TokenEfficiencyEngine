# Lessons and mental models — what is transferable, and what was luck

**Summary.** The distilled content: the models these operators repeatedly cite and what each actually does, the failure modes that recur, the case for temperament over intellect, and — the section that matters most — an honest accounting of which parts of these records are replicable and which were luck or a one-time structural opportunity that has closed.

> ⚠️ **This file makes inferences.** The facts in `02`–`04` are sourced. The models below are well-attested in the primary writing of the people concerned, but the claim that they *caused* the returns is not testable, and I do not assert it.

---

## Part I — The models

### 1. Opportunity cost
Every decision is measured against the best alternative use of the same capital, time and attention, not against zero. Munger's formulation: *the correct hurdle rate is your next best available idea*, which means a "good enough" investment is a bad investment if you already own a better one. This is why concentrated managers hold few positions — the 21st idea has to beat the 20th, and it usually does not. It is also the argument against diversification for its own sake: every marginal position dilutes the average quality of the portfolio unless it is genuinely better than what it displaces.

**The transferable version, outside markets:** the cost of a project is not its price but the best thing you did not do instead.

### 2. Incentives
Munger: *"Show me the incentive and I will show you the outcome."* Applied in three directions, all of which appear in `06` and `08`:
- **At the company you are analysing.** What management is paid on predicts what management will do, better than any strategy document. Read the proxy first.
- **At the manager you are allocating to.** A 2% management fee makes asset gathering rational. A performance-only fee with a high-water mark makes performance necessary. Schloss's zero-management-fee/25%-of-profits structure, Nomad's hurdle-plus-clawback, and Orbis's refundable performance fee are three different attempts to fix the same asymmetry — and each of those managers had an unusual record.
- **At yourself.** Your own compensation structure, career risk and capital terms determine what you are able to do, regardless of what you believe.

### 3. Inversion
Jacobi: *invert, always invert.* Instead of asking how to succeed, ask what would guarantee failure, and avoid that. In practice: permanent capital loss comes from overpaying, from owning a deteriorating business, and from leverage. Avoiding those three is most of the job. The pre-mortem in `06` is inversion made procedural.

### 4. Second-order thinking
Howard Marks's central idea and the one he has written about most. First-level thinking: *this is a good company, so I should buy the stock.* Second-level: *this is a good company, everyone knows it, the price reflects it, and therefore the stock may be a bad investment.* Second-level thinking requires holding a view about the consensus as well as about the fact, and requires being non-consensus **and right** — being non-consensus and wrong is merely expensive, and being consensus and right earns you the index.

### 5. Circle of competence
Know the boundary of what you understand, and — the part usually omitted — **the size of the circle matters far less than knowing where its edge is.** Schloss's circle was statistically cheap securities readable from published statements; he never met a management team. Simons's circle was pattern extraction from data; he refused to hire people with market opinions. Both were narrow and both were profitable. The failure mode is not a small circle but an unmarked one.

### 6. Margin of safety
Graham's Chapter 20, and the load-bearing concept of the whole value tradition. Buy at enough of a discount to your estimate of value that being wrong about the estimate still leaves you whole. Its real function is epistemic humility made quantitative: it is an admission that your valuation is an estimate with an error bar, and a way of surviving the error. Note that it applies to *the estimate*, not to the price move — a stock can fall 50% below a value estimate that was itself 50% too high.

### 7. Expectancy over accuracy
The insight from Thorp, from poker, and from every market maker: **it does not matter how often you are right; it matters how much you make when you are right versus how much you lose when you are wrong.** A strategy with a 30% hit rate and a 5:1 payoff ratio is excellent. Soros: *"It's not whether you're right or wrong that's important, but how much money you make when you're right and how much you lose when you're wrong."* This is also the correct frame for evaluating your own record — Annie Duke's "resulting" error is judging a good decision by a bad outcome, and it is the most common error in self-assessment.

Its formal expression is the Kelly criterion (`06`) and, on the high-breadth side, the fundamental law of active management: information ratio ≈ information coefficient × √breadth. You can be slightly right about very many things (Renaissance, Schloss) or very right about few (Nomad, Akre, Hohn). Both are coherent. Being slightly right about few things is not.

### 8. A good business versus a good investment
The most commonly confused distinction in the entire field. A good business earns high returns on capital and can defend them. A good investment is one bought at a price that leaves room for the buyer. **These are independent.** The great businesses of 1972 — the Nifty Fifty — were correctly identified as great and were disastrous investments at 50× earnings. Conversely, a mediocre business bought at a third of liquidation value can be an excellent investment, which is the entire Schloss method.

Corollary: **growth is not automatically good.** Growth adds value only when the return on the incremental capital exceeds its cost. A company growing at 15% while earning 6% on capital against an 9% cost of capital is destroying value enthusiastically. This is the McKinsey value-driver point in `06` and it is the single most useful piece of corporate finance in this domain.

### 9. Time arbitrage and the structural advantage of patient capital
Most market participants have a horizon of months because their capital, their compensation or their job security has a horizon of months. A participant whose capital cannot be withdrawn can therefore harvest a *structural* premium simply by holding through what others must sell through. **This is arguably the largest and most durable inefficiency remaining in public markets, and it is available only to those who engineer the right capital.**

It is not a soft point. It is the common thread of the profiles in `02`: Swensen's perpetual endowment; Gayner's and Watsa's insurance float; Lindsell Train's closed-end investment trust; Nomad's lock-ups and selected investors; Schloss's 92 hand-picked partners and no management fee; Hohn's and Vinall's long-duration institutional capital. The analysis was in most cases replicable. The capital structure was the moat.

### 10. Scale economics shared
Nick Sleep and Qais Zakaria's contribution (see `02`). Most companies that gain scale advantages keep them as margin. A rare few — Costco, Amazon, GEICO, Nebraska Furniture Mart — pass them to customers as lower prices, which drives volume, which deepens the scale advantage, which funds further price cuts. The loop compounds. Markets systematically under-price it because it looks like margin destruction in any single year and only reveals itself over a decade, and because the DCF assumptions required to capture it feel absurd at the time.

The general form: **look for businesses whose competitive advantage strengthens as a mechanical consequence of their own growth**, and whose management understands this well enough to accept lower near-term margins deliberately.

### 11. The capital cycle
Marathon Asset Management's framework (*Capital Returns*, 2015). High returns attract capital → capital expands supply → supply crushes returns → capital withdraws → supply contracts → returns recover. The practical instruction is to **watch capital expenditure, capacity and industry consolidation rather than earnings**, and to be contrarian on the *supply* side rather than the demand side, because supply is far more forecastable than demand. Most reliable in shipping, semiconductors, mining, energy, airlines, homebuilding and, historically, memory chips.

It also explains why quality investing works when it does: businesses that can absorb capital at high returns without inviting supply are rare, and that rarity is the moat.

### 12. Reflexivity
Soros's contribution, and the one genuinely anti-efficient-markets idea in this list. Prices do not merely reflect fundamentals; they change them. A rising share price lowers a company's cost of capital, permitting acquisitions and investment that improve the fundamentals that justified the rise. A falling price can trigger covenant breaches, rating downgrades, customer defection and a funding crisis that make the fall correct. This is why banks, insurers, leveraged rollups and anything dependent on continuous access to capital markets are qualitatively different from businesses that are not. LTCM in 1998 is reflexivity in its destructive form: forced liquidation moved the very prices the liquidation was measured against.

### 13. Base rates and the outside view
Before the specific story, the class statistic: what proportion of large acquisitions create value? What proportion of turnarounds turn? How many companies sustain 20% growth for a decade? (Vanishingly few.) The inside view — the compelling, detailed, specific narrative — reliably overrides the outside view, and the correction is procedural: **write the base rate down before you write the thesis.**

### 14. Skin in the game
Taleb's framing of an old idea. Weight the opinion of someone who bears the consequences above someone who does not. Applied: a manager with most of their net worth in their own fund; a CEO who purchased shares rather than received them; an analyst who has to publish a dated, falsifiable thesis. Its inverse is the most reliable red flag in finance — advice from someone who is paid whether or not it is right.

## Part II — The recurring failure modes

1. **Confusing a good business with a good investment** (see model 8). The dominant error of the quality-investing era, and the plausible explanation for Lindsell Train's post-2020 experience: the businesses did not deteriorate as much as the prices had assumed they would improve.
2. **Style drift under pressure.** Abandoning the process precisely when it is underperforming — which is, mechanically, when it is cheapest. Jean-Marie Eveillard's late-1990s experience is the canonical case: a value manager who refused to own technology stocks in 1997–1999, lost a large fraction of his assets to redemptions, and was vindicated afterwards. Most managers in that position change the process instead.
3. **Leverage.** Converts a temporary drawdown into a permanent loss by forcing the sale. It is the mechanism by which correct theses still bankrupt their holders. Almost none of the discretionary managers in `02` used meaningful leverage; the ones who used it (Thorp, the quants) used it against genuinely hedged books and sized it with explicit mathematics.
4. **Over-betting a real edge.** The Kelly result in `06`: betting 2× Kelly gives an expected growth rate of zero even with a genuine edge. Being right and sized wrong is a complete way to fail.
5. **Anchoring on purchase price.** The price you paid is information about your past self, not about the security. Refusing to sell at a loss and refusing to buy more above your entry price are the same error.
6. **Commitment and consistency bias after publishing a thesis.** The cost of the written-thesis discipline in `06` is that publication creates a commitment. The correction is the pre-written sell criteria, decided before ownership.
7. **Confusing volatility with risk, or risk with volatility.** Both directions are errors. An investor with permanent capital who fears volatility gives up a structural advantage; a pod PM with a 10% stop-out who ignores volatility loses the job.
8. **Mistaking a factor exposure for skill.** A large share of historic "alpha" was value, size, quality or momentum exposure before those were named and priced. Any record from before roughly 1993 should be read with this in mind.
9. **Over-fitting** — in quantitative research literally (see `07`), and in discretionary research through the accumulation of confirming detail. Both feel like diligence.
10. **Career-risk-driven herding.** Documented in `08`. It is not a moral failure; it is the rational response to being paid on relative performance and fired on relative underperformance.
11. **The one great trade problem.** Michael Burry's subprime trade was correct, enormously profitable, and generated furious investor opposition while it was being carried. A single correct call also tells you almost nothing about a process — Burry's subsequent record has been mixed, and Bill Miller beat the S&P 500 for fifteen consecutive years (1991–2005) and then lost more than $100m on Bear Stearns in 2008. **Streaks end, and the end is usually as informative as the streak.**

## Part III — Temperament over intellect

The single most consistent claim made by the people in `02`, and the one for which the evidence is strongest, is that **intelligence past a threshold contributes almost nothing, and temperament contributes almost everything.**

The evidence for the threshold claim is the composition of the group itself: it includes a man with no tertiary education (Schloss), an English literature graduate (Akre), a history graduate (Smith), a physician (Burry), a philosophy dropout (Miller) and half a dozen doctorates in mathematics and computer science. If raw intellect were the driver, that distribution would not exist.

The temperamental traits that recur:

- **The ability to do nothing.** Terry Smith's third instruction, literally. Most damage is done by action.
- **Tolerance for being wrong in public for years.** Eveillard 1997–1999. Lindsell Train from 2020. Gray's entire firm markets on this pattern. The tolerance must be structural as well as personal — see time arbitrage.
- **Genuine independence, not contrarianism.** The distinction matters: contrarianism is a reaction to the consensus and is therefore still determined by it. Independence is indifference to it. Schloss did not read the newspaper's opinion pages.
- **Emotional stability around money.** Losing 40% without changing the process; gaining 40% without changing the position sizing.
- **Curiosity sustained over decades.** All of these people read constantly, and about things adjacent to rather than inside finance.
- **Willingness to be told you are wrong quickly.** Explicit in market-making interviews (`08`), and the operative trait in a trading seat.
- **Honesty about one's own record.** Rochon's annual mistake review; Swensen's *Unconventional Success*, in which a man who beat the market for three decades told individual investors to index.

The uncomfortable corollary: **temperament is much harder to acquire than knowledge, and the industry selects for the opposite of it.** The recruiting funnel in `08` selects for competitiveness, speed, and relative performance under observation. Nothing in that funnel selects for patience or for equanimity in the face of public error.

## Part IV — The honest accounting: what was replicable, and what was not

This is the section every reading list of this kind omits.

### What was probably a one-time structural opportunity

| Record | The condition that made it possible | Status in 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| **Thorp, Princeton/Newport (1969–1989)** | Convertible and warrant mispricings computable by one mathematician before Black–Scholes was published (1973) and before anyone else was doing it | Closed. This is now an automated, competitive, thin-margin business. |
| **Simons, Medallion (from 1988)** | Statistical structure in futures and equity data that essentially nobody was mining, combined with an ability to hire scientists nobody else wanted | Substantially closed; note Renaissance's own external funds have not replicated it, and Medallion is capped and employee-only. |
| **Swensen, Yale (from 1985)** | Endowments held 60/40 portfolios; private equity, venture and timber carried an unharvested illiquidity premium; Yale's early access to top-quartile VC | Closed. Every endowment now runs the model; the premium it exploited has been competed away. |
| **Schloss (1955–2000)** | Statistically cheap securities were abundant, screening was manual and therefore scarce, and Value Line was an edge | Largely closed. Screening is free; the deep-value opportunity set in developed markets is small and structurally challenged. |
| **Gray (from 1973)** | A world-class method learned at Fidelity, applied in an under-analysed, capital-controlled South African market | Partly closed in South Africa; the *pattern* — frontier-market arbitrage of method — is still live. |
| **Greenblatt, Gotham (1985–1994)** | Special situations too small and complex for institutions, in a market with far less coverage | Partly closed for large caps; still live in genuinely small and complex situations. |
| **A.W. Jones (from 1949)** | Being the first person to combine long/short, leverage and a 20% incentive fee | Closed the moment Carol Loomis wrote about it in 1966. |

### What is genuinely replicable

1. **Structuring your capital to match your strategy.** The most consistently under-copied element of every record here. Insurance float, endowments, closed-end trusts, lock-ups, clawback fee structures, investor selection by temperament. Nothing about this depends on a market condition.
2. **A fee structure aligned with the outcome.** Schloss's, Nomad's and Orbis's are three different working designs, all available to anyone starting a vehicle today.
3. **Writing dated, falsifiable theses and scoring them.** Free, immediately available, and the practice most reliably shared by the group.
4. **The accounting scepticism.** The devices in Terry Smith's 1992 book have changed; the mentality has not, and the underlying incentive for management to flatter earnings is permanent.
5. **The capital-cycle discipline.** Supply is more forecastable than demand. That is a structural feature of industrial economics, not a market anomaly, and it has worked repeatedly across cycles.
6. **Temperamental practices**: pre-mortems, base rates, sell criteria written before purchase, decision journals, annual public error reviews.
7. **The import-a-method strategy.** Simons imported hidden Markov models; Thorp imported information theory; Gray imported Fidelity's process to Cape Town. **[NA]** The general pattern — take a mature method into a market where it is scarce — is the most promising strategic template in this file for someone starting from a small market.

### What was luck

Some of it. There is no way to determine how much, and the profiles cannot tell you, because failed practitioners of the same methods do not write memoirs.

The disciplined statement of the problem: 91.99% of US large-cap active funds underperformed the S&P 500 over the twenty years to end-2024, and only 32.95% of the funds that existed at the start of that period still existed at the end. Any set of long-run winners is drawn from a distribution whose left tail has been deleted. Buffett's coin-flipping rebuttal in *The Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville* (17 May 1984) — that the winners cluster in one intellectual village, which chance would not produce — is a real argument and has real force. But it is a *clustering* argument, and it establishes that the method is not worthless; it does not establish that a given practitioner of it will beat the market, and Buffett selected his nine after the outcomes were known.

### The most defensible conclusion

**The returns are not replicable. The practices largely are, and they have positive expected value in domains other than markets.**

Reading widely and continuously; writing down what you believe with a date attached and scoring it later; establishing base rates before constructing a story; inverting to find what would guarantee failure; matching the duration of your commitments to the duration of your resources; understanding what the people around you are paid on; sizing bets so that being wrong is survivable; and being able to sit still — these are what the fifteen profiles in `02` actually have in common, and none of them requires a market, a degree, or a particular decade.

## Open questions

- The attributions of specific aphorisms to Munger, Soros, Keynes and Jacobi are from general knowledge; none was verified against a primary text in this session.
- Eveillard's 1997–1999 asset outflows are widely reported but were **not** confirmed by the Wikipedia article fetched, which does not mention them. `needs-verification`.
- Marathon's *Capital Returns* framework is described from general knowledge; the book was not fetched.
- The claim that a majority of pre-1993 "alpha" was factor exposure is an inference, not a measured result; a proper test needs holdings-level factor attribution.
- No attempt was made to quantify the luck/skill split for any manager. Doing so properly would require the full population of managers using each method, which does not exist.
