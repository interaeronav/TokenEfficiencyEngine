# Southern African and emerging-market figures, and the Namibian picture

**Summary.** Southern Africa produced at least one genuinely world-class investor — Allan Gray — and a distinctive institutional ecosystem: an owner-manager investment-holding-company tradition (PSG), a Cape Town boutique cluster (Allan Gray, Coronation, and their many offshoots), a home-grown investment bank that became a dual-listed international (Investec), and, in Namibia, a small but well-regulated financial sector dominated by one enormous pension fund. This file covers the verifiable figures, flags clearly what could not be verified, and then treats the Namibian sector in detail as the practical context for a finance career started from Windhoek.

> ⚠️ **Verification warning.** Southern African and African financial-sector sources are markedly harder to fetch than US/UK ones: several firm websites are robots-disallowed or serve JavaScript shells. A larger share of this file is marked `needs-verification` than in the rest of the domain. Track records quoted here for private managers are firm self-reports.

## 1. Allan Gray (1938–2019) — the most important investor southern Africa produced

**Education.** Selborne College in East London; studied **accounting** at Rhodes University; MBA from **Harvard Business School, 1965**. He was dyslexic. His family had emigrated from Aberdeen to Butterworth in the 1890s; his grandmother, an Aberdeen graduate, became the first female mayor in South Africa — a family history he credited for his emphasis on education.

**Career sequence.** After Harvard he spent eight years at **Fidelity Management and Research in Boston**, where he established himself as a contrarian, having formed his views around the 1962 market decline. This is the essential fact of his career: the philosophy was learned inside the most competitive fund-management culture in the world, and then transplanted. He returned to South Africa in **1973** and founded Allan Gray Investment Counsel in Cape Town, initially advising individuals and later institutions. In **1989** he founded **Orbis Investment Management** in London to invest internationally; it moved to Bermuda in 1991. His son William later served as Orbis's president.

**The philosophy.** The firm states it explicitly and has not varied it: *"We buy shares we think are undervalued and sell them when we think they have reached their worth, regardless of popular opinion."* Independent-minded, "often contrarian," valuation-driven, with the courage "to fly in the face of popular opinion or conventional wisdom." In practice this has meant long periods of severe relative underperformance followed by sharp recovery — the firm's marketing materials themselves emphasise this pattern, which is unusual honesty.

Orbis's other distinctive feature is its **fee structure**: a refundable performance fee that is paid into a reserve and clawed back when performance reverses, so that the manager shares the downside as well as the upside. Alongside Nomad's clawback (see `02`) this is one of the few genuine attempts in the industry to solve the option-like asymmetry of a standard performance fee.

**Scale and philanthropy.** By 2015 Orbis managed over $30bn and Allan Gray had become South Africa's largest privately held investment firm at about $35bn, with Gray's personal wealth assessed at around $2bn. He founded the Allan and Gill Gray Charitable Trust in 1979, the **Allan Gray Orbis Foundation** in 2007 with a $130m endowment, and in **2016 donated his entire business stake to charitable purposes** — meaning the firm is now effectively owned by a philanthropic structure. The Foundation is funded on an ongoing basis by 5% of Allan Gray's pre-tax profits and runs entrepreneurship-focused scholarships and fellowships across Southern Africa; it reports 975 fellows in its alumni community, 56% female, with 200+ having created businesses employing 800+ people. **[NA]** The Foundation's programmes have historically included Namibia and Botswana; the current country coverage is `needs-verification` from the Foundation's own material.

Allan Gray's ownership structure in South Africa is also notable: E² (Excellence in Entrepreneurship) holds 17.8% and a staff empowerment scheme reserves 14% for black staff ownership, giving roughly 32% black ownership.

**Transferable lesson.** Train at the frontier, then apply the method where it is scarce. Gray learned contrarian value investing at Fidelity in Boston and deployed it in an under-analysed market 12,000 km away. That arbitrage — world-class method, less-competed market — is the single most relevant strategic insight in this file for anyone building a career from Namibia or another small market.

## 2. Coronation and the Cape Town cluster

**Coronation Fund Managers** was founded in Cape Town in **1993** as an independent asset manager deliberately without tied distribution channels — a direct challenge to the life-assurance-owned model that then dominated South African savings. It opened Dublin (1998) and London (1999) offices, listed on the JSE in **June 2003** (ticker CML), and became the first South African asset manager to achieve GIPS compliance in the same year. It launched Africa portfolios in 2008, established the Imvula Trust for black ownership in 2005, and completed a B-BBEE transaction in 2024 taking black ownership to 52%. AUM was **R761 billion at 30 September 2025**.

Its founding team is not named on the Wikipedia article and I could not fetch the firm's people pages (`needs-verification`). The firm's long-standing CIO is **Karl Leinberger** (CA(SA) and CFA, `needs-verification`), with Anton Pillay as CEO, Kirshni Totaram heading institutional business and Pieter Koekemoer heading personal investments.

**Why the cluster matters.** Cape Town's independent-manager cluster — Allan Gray, Coronation, and the many boutiques founded by their alumni — is a genuine example of the "tree of protégés" pattern seen at Yale and Tweedy Browne. People trained inside a strong process leave and reproduce it. The named individuals below are part of that lineage but could not be verified from fetched sources:

- **Piet Viljoen** — founder of RECM (Regarding Capital Management, founded c. 2003), later Merchant West Investments; a deep-value, contrarian manager and prolific public writer; previously at Investec Asset Management. `needs-verification` on all details including education (reported CA(SA)).
- **Kokkie Kooyman** — global financial-sector specialist, founder of what became Denker Capital (formerly Sanlam Global Investment Solutions); widely regarded as one of the best bank analysts globally; repeatedly ranked by international fund-rating bodies. `needs-verification`; the Denker Capital site is robots-disallowed.
- **Adrian Saville** — founder of Cannon Asset Managers, later Genera Capital; professor at the Gordon Institute of Business Science (GIBS), University of Pretoria; PhD in economics (`needs-verification`). Notable as the clearest example in southern Africa of the academic-practitioner hybrid.

## 3. Jannie Mouton and PSG — the owner-operator holding company

**Education.** Born 1946 in Carnarvon, Northern Cape. **BCom (Hons), University of Stellenbosch**; articles at Coopers Brothers (now PwC); qualified as a **Chartered Accountant in 1973**.

**Career sequence.** Accountant at Federale Volksbeleggings from 1973. Co-founded the stockbroking firm **Senekal, Mouton & Kitshoff** in 1982 and became managing director in 1987. In **1995, at 48, he was dismissed by his own partners** — the founding trauma of the story and the title of his 2011 book, *And then they fired me*. He and Chris Otto then acquired control of PAG Limited, a small listed shell, which became **PSG Group**.

**The model.** PSG is an investment holding company operated by owner-managers: buy or found businesses, back entrepreneurial management with equity, hold for very long periods, and unbundle to shareholders when the subsidiary is large enough to stand alone. Its most celebrated creation is **Capitec Bank**, built to serve the mass market that the incumbent banks priced out — by late 2025 Capitec reported 24.1 million customers (about 38% of South Africa's population), 880 branches, ~9,000 ATMs and the second-highest market capitalisation among the big five South African banks with the highest return on equity. Other holdings and creations included Curro (private schools), PSG Konsult, Zeder (agriculture) and Stadio (tertiary education). From around 2022 PSG unbundled most of its major interests to shareholders. PSG Financial Services reported net profit of R1.38bn and AUM of R470.7bn in February 2025.

In August 2025 the Jannie Mouton Stigting announced it would repurpose R7.2bn to acquire Curro shares, directed at quality education for disadvantaged children.

**Track record (claimed) — `needs-verification`.** PSG's share price compound annual growth from 1995 is routinely quoted in the region of 40%+ per annum over the first two decades. The figure originates in PSG's own annual reports and depends heavily on the starting point (a distressed shell in 1995). I could not fetch a source; treat as unverified.

**Transferable lesson.** Being fired at 48 was the enabling event. Also: the holding-company structure is another answer to the permanent-capital problem — shareholders can sell their PSG shares, but the underlying capital never leaves the vehicle.

## 4. Investec — a South African investment bank that went global

Founded in **1974** in Johannesburg as a small leasing and finance company by **Larry Nestadt, Errol Grolman and Ian Kantor**. Listed on the JSE in 1986 after merging with the trust company Metboard, and in **July 2002** implemented a dual-listed company structure with a London listing — the first South African company listed on both the London and Johannesburg exchanges. As at 31 March 2026 it employed approximately 8,200 people across South Africa, the UK, Ireland, Switzerland, Mauritius, Guernsey, Jersey, India and the US.

The long-serving leadership pair, **Stephen Koseff** (CEO) and **Bernard Kantor** (MD), are not covered in the Wikipedia article I fetched (`needs-verification` for their backgrounds; both are reported to be CA(SA)/accounting-trained South Africans who joined in the early 1980s). Investec Asset Management, founded within the group in 1991 and led by **Hendrik du Toit**, was demerged in 2020 and renamed **Ninety One** — one of the largest active managers headquartered in an emerging market (`needs-verification` on the demerger date and AUM).

**Career relevance.** Investec and Ninety One, together with Standard Bank, Rand Merchant Bank (part of FirstRand), Absa and Nedbank, are the principal sell-side and buy-side employers for southern African graduates, and are the most realistic first employers for a Namibian entering the industry.

**Honest caveat on African track records.** Public, long-horizon, independently verified track records for African managers are much scarcer than for US/UK managers, and the currency-translation problem is severe: a fund reporting strong local-currency returns in Nigeria or Egypt over 2015–2025 may have delivered materially negative dollar returns. Always ask which currency, and whether the figure is before or after devaluation.

## 6. **[NA]** The Namibian financial sector, in detail

### The macro frame
Namibia is a lower-middle-income economy with GDP per capita of $4,413 (2025) and nominal GDP of N$245.1bn in 2024 (3.71% real growth). Income inequality is extreme (Gini 59.1, 2015, second-highest in the world). The tertiary sector is 55% of output, primary 21%, secondary 15% (2025). Only about a quarter of the working population has medical aid and about half a pension fund.

The **Namibian dollar is pegged 1:1 to the South African rand** within the **Common Monetary Area** (with South Africa, Lesotho and Eswatini), and Namibia is in SACU. The practical consequence for a finance career is enormous: **Namibian monetary policy is effectively imported from Pretoria**, Namibian interest rates track the SARB repo rate closely, and Namibian savings flow naturally into South African assets. Domestic asset requirements exist precisely to counteract that gravity.

### The institutions

- **Bank of Namibia** — central bank, founded 1990 under the Bank of Namibia Act 8 of 1990 and Article 128 of the Constitution; sole issuer of the Namibian dollar; supervises banking institutions; a member of the Alliance for Financial Inclusion with Maya Declaration commitments from March 2012.
- **NAMFISA** — the Namibia Financial Institutions Supervisory Authority, an independent body established under **Act No. 3 of 2001**, regulating all non-banking financial institutions: capital markets, insurance and medical aid funds, friendly societies, microlending and retirement funds. It also enforces compliance with the Financial Intelligence Act, 2012. It publishes **Quarterly Statistical Bulletins** (most recent fetched reference: Q1 2026, published 26 June 2026), **Annual Reports** (2025 edition, 25 September 2025) and **Financial Stability Reports** (2026 edition, 5 May 2026). These bulletins are the authoritative source for Namibian pension-fund, unit-trust and insurance asset totals — I did not fetch the underlying numbers, so **all sector asset figures here are `needs-verification`**.
- **NSX (Namibian Stock Exchange / Namibia Securities Exchange)** — launched **30 September 1992**, licensed by NAMFISA under the Stock Exchanges Control Act. At January 2025 it had **59 listed companies: 47 dual-listed (39 of them also on the JSE) and 12 local-only listings**. The exchange's own site cites a total market capitalisation of **US$145bn at end-2014**, "the second biggest exchange in Africa in those terms" — a figure that is almost entirely the market value of dual-listed South African giants and therefore says almost nothing about the domestic market. The distinction between the **NSX Overall Index** and the **NSX Local Index** exists precisely because of this. Governance follows the **NamCode**, co-authored by the IoDSA and the NSX, which replaced King II.
- **GIPF (Government Institutions Pension Fund)** — by a wide margin the dominant pool of domestic capital and the gravitational centre of Namibian finance. It is the defined-benefit fund for Namibian public servants. It holds, for example, **26% of Capricorn Investment Holdings / Bank Windhoek**. Its total assets, membership numbers and funding level are `needs-verification` — the GIPF website's annual-report section is robots-disallowed and its About page carries no figures. **Anyone building on this file should fetch the GIPF annual report directly.** GIPF's unlisted-investment mandate, established under NAMFISA regulation, requires a proportion of the fund to be placed with registered unlisted investment managers in Namibian private assets — the single most important structural fact for private-capital careers in Namibia.
- **Regulation 13 / Regulation 28-equivalent domestic asset requirements** — Namibian retirement funds are required to hold a minimum proportion of assets in Namibia. The specific percentage and the current regulation number are `needs-verification`; historically the requirement has been increased in steps, and it is the reason a domestic asset-management industry exists at all.

### The firms

- **Capricorn Group** (NSX: CGP) — the largest Namibian-owned financial group, operating in Namibia, Botswana and Zambia, with **Bank Windhoek**, **Bank Gaborone**, **Capricorn Asset Management** and **Capricorn Private Wealth**. Bank Windhoek was founded in **1982** when entrepreneurs led by Koos Brandt took over eight Volkskas branches, and is described as the only systemically important bank in Namibia that is not foreign-owned; it generates more than 95% of Capricorn Group's total income and had 51 branches and agencies, 149 ATMs and 253 Cash Express machines as at June 2022.
- **Other banks** — First National Bank Namibia and Standard Bank Namibia (South African-owned), Nedbank Namibia, Letshego Bank Namibia, Bank BIC Namibia.
- **Asset managers** — Capricorn Asset Management, Old Mutual Investment Group Namibia, Sanlam Investment Management Namibia, Allan Gray Namibia, Ninety One Namibia, Prescient, M&G, and Namibian-owned boutiques.
- **Brokers, research and advisory** — **IJG** is the most prominent Namibian-owned house, describing itself as "the leading Namibian financial services provider," with five divisions: Advisory (valuations, business broking, capital raising, transaction advisory), Investment Management (a 100%-Namibian-owned asset manager serving pension, corporate and retail clients), IJG Capital (private equity, equity and debt funding with a renewable-energy emphasis), IJG Securities (equity and fixed-income trading, NSX Sponsor services, described as Namibia's leading stockbroker, operating on both the NSX and JSE), and Wealth Management. **Cirrus Capital** and **Simonis Storm Securities** are the other well-known local research and advisory houses (`needs-verification` on their details).
- **Old Mutual** — founded 1845 in Cape Town, demutualised and listed in 1999 on the London, Johannesburg, Zimbabwe **and Namibian** exchanges; announced a Namibian BEE initiative in 2006; completed a managed separation in 2018.

### What a finance career actually looks like from Namibia

The honest structural facts:

1. **The domestic market is small and the capital pool is concentrated.** GIPF plus a handful of corporate funds and the insurers are the client base. Winning a GIPF mandate is transformative for a local manager; there are not many mandates.
2. **The natural first employers** are the banks (Bank Windhoek/Capricorn, FNB Namibia, Standard Bank Namibia), the asset managers' Namibian arms, IJG/Cirrus/Simonis Storm, the Big Four audit firms (Deloitte, PwC, KPMG, EY all have Windhoek offices), NAMFISA, the Bank of Namibia and GIPF itself. The regulator and the central bank are unusually good training grounds in a small market because they see the whole system.
3. **The most common credential path is accounting.** CA(NAM)/CA(SA) through articles at a Big Four firm, then a move into corporate finance, banking or asset management. This mirrors the South African pattern (Mouton, and most of the Investec generation) and is the most reliable local route.
4. **The CFA is disproportionately valuable in a small market** — precisely because the signalling problem is worse when there is no target-school pipeline and no large peer group. See `08`.
5. **The regional escalator** runs Windhoek → Johannesburg/Cape Town → London. The CMA and the JSE dual-listing relationship make South Africa a low-friction next step; the University of Cape Town, Stellenbosch and Wits are the usual academic bridges; the Allan Gray Orbis Foundation scholarships and fellowships are an explicit vehicle for it.
6. **Remote work has genuinely changed the calculus.** The autodidact route in `08` — a public written track record, a small managed vehicle, an international client base — is materially more available from Windhoek in 2026 than it was in 2006. Robert Vinall runs a global fund from a Swiss village with a co-manager and a website; the constraint is regulatory domicile and custody, not geography.
7. **The unlisted-investment regulation is the local structural opportunity.** A regulated requirement to place pension money in Namibian private assets, in a market with very few qualified managers, is exactly the kind of structural mispricing that the profiles in `02` were built on. It is also where the governance risk is highest.
