# Breaking in and building a career

**Summary.** The practical route: how recruiting actually works and how early it now happens, what each track's interview process tests, which certifications are worth the time, how to build a public track record, the emerging-market and remote route, how to move between tracks, how compensation progresses, the distinctive career risks of the industry, and what starting your own vehicle actually involves.

> ⚠️ **All compensation figures in this file are `needs-verification`** unless attributed to a company disclosure. No compensation survey was fetched. Treat every number as an order-of-magnitude indication with a stated year, not as a quote.

## 1. The target-school and non-target reality

**The uncomfortable truth.** Investment banking, private equity, and to a lesser extent the elite market makers recruit disproportionately from a short list of universities, because the screening problem is expensive and a university's admissions process is a free pre-filter. In the US this means the Ivies plus Stanford, MIT, Chicago, Duke, Michigan, Berkeley, NYU Stern, and a handful of others; in the UK, Oxford, Cambridge, LSE, Imperial and Warwick; in South Africa, UCT, Stellenbosch, Wits and to a degree Pretoria and Rhodes.

**What actually varies by school** is not the ceiling but the *default*. At a target school the pipeline finds you: on-campus presentations, alumni networks, a societies infrastructure, and recruiters who have hired from there before. At a non-target you must construct all of that yourself, and you must start earlier.

**The non-target playbook, in order of effectiveness:**
1. **Networking with specificity.** Not "can I have advice," but a concise, informed message referencing something the person actually did, with one clear ask. Volume matters — a 5–10% response rate is normal.
2. **A public written track record** (section 5 below). This is the only tool that scales and the only one available to someone with no network at all.
3. **Regional and second-tier entry, then lateral.** A middle-market bank, a Big Four transaction-services group, an audit articles contract, a regional asset manager, or a regulator — then move. Two years of real work beats a target-school CV with none.
4. **Competitions and demonstrable artefacts.** CFA Institute Research Challenge, university investment funds, competitive programming for quant roles, a well-documented open-source project, a public model.
5. **Certifications** as a signal where none other exists (section 4).

**The elite market makers are the genuine meritocracy exception.** Jane Street, Optiver, SIG, IMC and Jump interview on measurable performance — mental arithmetic, probability, games, code — and will hire a mathematics student from an unfashionable university who solves the problems faster. If you are numerate and from a non-target background, **this is the highest-expected-value application you can make.**

## 2. Internships and the recruiting calendar

The single most consequential structural fact for a student: **the calendar has moved dramatically earlier and it is the binding constraint.**

| Track | When it happens | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| US investment banking summer analyst | Applications open ~12–18 months before the internship; some banks have recruited in students' *first* or second undergraduate year | Conversion rate from summer to full-time is high (commonly quoted 70–90%, `needs-verification`) |
| US private equity "on-cycle" | Historically within months of analysts starting their banking jobs; has repeatedly moved earlier and occasionally been pushed back | `needs-verification` for the current cycle |
| Market makers | Rolling and earlier each year; some run first-year "discovery" programmes | Process is fast — days to weeks |
| UK | Spring weeks in first year, summer internships in penultimate year | Spring weeks are the real gate |
| Hedge funds | Almost entirely experienced-hire; very few graduate programmes | Come from banking, research, consulting or quant |
| **[ZA]/[NA]** Southern Africa | Vacation work and articles applications typically in penultimate year; CA articles contracts are the dominant graduate structure | Much less compressed than the US |

**The practical implication.** If you are at a target school and want banking or PE, the decisions that matter are made in your first two years. If you missed that window, the route is lateral entry via a related job, and it is entirely viable — it just takes two or three years longer.

## 3. Interview processes by track

### Investment banking — technicals
A closed, well-documented body of knowledge. Expect:
- **Accounting.** Walk me through the three statements. If depreciation increases by $10, what happens to all three? (The canonical question; the answer requires a tax rate.) What is working capital and why does it move cash?
- **Valuation.** DCF mechanics; WACC; unlevered versus levered free cash flow; terminal value by perpetuity growth versus exit multiple; why EV/EBITDA rather than P/E; what a control premium is.
- **M&A and LBO.** Accretion/dilution; the sources and uses table; how an LBO creates return (debt paydown, multiple expansion, EBITDA growth) and roughly in what proportions; a paper LBO done mentally in ten minutes.
- **Behavioural.** Why banking, why this bank, walk me through your CV, a time you failed. These are scored more heavily than candidates expect.

Preparation is a solved problem: the standard interview guides plus 100+ practice questions. **Fluency and speed, not novelty, is what is tested.**

### Market making and quant trading — mental maths, probability, games
- **Timed arithmetic.** The archetype is Optiver's 80-question test: eight minutes, mixed operations including decimals and percentages. Passing requires drilling, not talent. Free trainers exist online.
- **Estimation.** Fermi problems — how many piano tuners, how much does the Empire State Building weigh, what is the annual revenue of a coffee shop. Structure the estimate out loud; the process is scored, not the answer.
- **Probability puzzles.** Expected value of dice and coin games, conditional probability, Bayes, the Monty Hall family, optimal stopping, gambler's ruin, martingale arguments. *Heard on the Street* (Crack) and *A Practical Guide to Quantitative Finance Interviews* (Zhou) cover ~80% of what is asked.
- **Market-making games.** "Make me a market on X" — quote a two-sided price, get hit or lifted, update. This tests adverse selection directly: if someone always trades against your quote, your quote is wrong. Being told you are wrong and adjusting without ego is the trait being measured.
- **Coding**, for research and dev roles.

### Hedge fund — the stock pitch
The interview *is* a pitch, and it is judged like an investment committee.
- **Prepare two or three pitches, one of them a short.** A well-constructed short is the fastest way to separate yourself, because most candidates cannot do one.
- **Structure:** what the business does and how it makes money; what the market believes (state the consensus explicitly); why the consensus is wrong and *what mechanism* causes the mispricing; the variant perception and how you would know if you were wrong; the reverse DCF or the specific numbers implied by the current price; the catalyst and timeframe; position size and why.
- **Know the numbers cold** — revenue, margins, ROIC, net debt, share count, current multiple, and how they have moved over five years.
- **The killer follow-ups:** who is on the other side and what do they believe? What would make you sell? What is the base rate for what you are forecasting? What did you get wrong on your last pitch?

### Private equity — the modelling test
A timed LBO, typically 60 minutes to 3 hours from a CIM or a short case, sometimes followed by an investment-committee memo and a verbal recommendation. Tested: speed and accuracy in Excel without a template, sensible assumptions, a defensible returns bridge, and the ability to say what you would diligence next. Formatting and clarity are scored — the output is judged as a work product.

### Equity research and long-only asset management
A written report, a pitch, and a discussion of process. Long-only interviews probe philosophy and consistency far more than hedge fund ones: they want to know that you will still be doing the same thing in ten years.

## 4. Certifications and their actual value

| Credential | Cost / time | Actually worth it for | Not worth it for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CFA** | Three exams; average **four years**; 4,000 hours of experience over ≥3 years; pass rates 22–59% (2014–2024); ≥200,000 charterholders (Dec 2024) | Institutional long-only asset management, equity research, wealth management, credit analysis; **strongly** for non-target, career-changer and small-market candidates | Investment banking, PE, market making, quant research — nobody will ask |
| **CAIA** | Two levels, ~12–18 months | Allocators, fund-of-funds, alternatives operations and due diligence, private-markets roles | Anyone in a front-office alternatives seat already |
| **FRM** | Two parts | Risk management, model validation, bank treasury, regulatory roles | Front-office investing |
| **Actuarial (FIA/FSA/FASSA)** | 6–10 years; the hardest examination path in finance | Insurance, reinsurance, pensions, ALM; among the most reliably employable qualifications in existence | Anyone who wants to pick stocks |
| **CA(SA) / CA(NAM) / CPA / ACA** | 3-year articles plus exams | **[ZA]/[NA]** The dominant credential in southern African finance — Mouton, Gray (accounting), Cundill and much of the SA investment establishment came through it. Excellent preparation for security analysis because the raw material *is* accounting | US quant and market-making roles |
| **Master's in Financial Engineering / MFE** | 1–2 years, expensive | Quant roles at banks and some funds; a legitimate route for non-PhDs | Competing with PhDs for research seats at top systematic funds |
| **MBA** | 2 years, very expensive | **Changing track, country or class** — see `04`. Gray, Watsa, Hohn and Spier all used it this way | Learning to invest, or advancing on a track you are already on |

**The honest framing:** every one of these is a *signal* plus a *body of knowledge*, and their value is inversely proportional to how strong your other signals are. **[NA]** For someone building a career from Namibia, where there is no target-school pipeline and the peer group is small, the CFA and the CA(NAM)/CA(SA) are worth disproportionately more than they would be for a Princeton graduate — this is the single most actionable point in this file for that context.

## 5. Building a public track record and writing

This is the most under-used lever available, and the cost is now zero.

**Why it works.** It solves the employer's actual problem, which is not "is this person clever" but "can this person think about a business and communicate it." A written thesis is direct evidence. Michael Burry is the documented case: a neurology resident writing about value investing on the Silicon Investor message board from 1996, who attracted the attention of professional firms including Vanguard, and founded Scion Capital in 2000. Robert Vinall raises institutional capital, including from major endowments, substantially on the strength of an annual letter and an annual gathering in Engelberg.

**How to do it properly:**
1. **Write full theses, not opinions.** Use the ten-point structure in `06`. 2,000–4,000 words each.
2. **Date everything and never edit retroactively.** The value is in the timestamp. A public record you can be embarrassed by is worth ten times a curated one.
3. **Publish the mistakes.** François Rochon reviews his errors annually in the Giverny letters; this is the practice that most distinguishes serious writers from promotional ones.
4. **Track the outcomes explicitly.** Maintain a table of every idea, the date, the price, the thesis, and the result — including the ones you got wrong.
5. **Where.** Your own site (owned, permanent), Substack, Value Investors Club (Greenblatt and John Petry's forum, capped at 250 members with a guest tier; getting in is itself a credential), Seeking Alpha, or a research blog. **[NA]** For a Namibian, writing about southern African listed companies that nobody else covers is a genuine informational niche and a differentiator that a New York candidate cannot replicate.
6. **A managed portfolio with a verifiable record.** Even a small personal account with a broker statement and a monthly published NAV, run for three years, is a real artefact.

**The realistic timeline:** two to three years of consistent public writing before it produces opportunities. Most people stop at four months.

## 6. The emerging-market and remote route **[NA]**

What has genuinely changed and what has not.

**Changed.** Research is remote-first — filings, transcripts, data and expert networks are all available from anywhere. Publishing costs nothing. Investor communication is video-native. Compliance and fund administration can be outsourced. A single-manager fund can be run from a small town, as Vinall demonstrates from Kilchberg.

**Not changed.** Capital formation is still relationship-driven and still concentrated in a few cities. Regulatory domicile, custody and audit still require an established jurisdiction. Time zones still constrain live trading roles. And the first job — the apprenticeship where you learn what good looks like — still overwhelmingly happens in person.

**The realistic sequences from Namibia:**

1. **The regional escalator.** Windhoek → Big Four articles or a bank/asset-manager graduate role → Johannesburg or Cape Town (Investec, Ninety One, Coronation, Allan Gray, Standard Bank, RMB) → London or New York if desired. The CMA currency arrangement, the NSX/JSE dual-listing relationship and the shared professional qualifications make each step low-friction. Allan Gray's own path — Rhodes, then HBS, then eight years at Fidelity in Boston, *then* back to Cape Town in 1973 — is the template, and the return leg is the point.
2. **The regulator/central bank route.** NAMFISA (established under Act No. 3 of 2001, regulating capital markets, insurance, retirement funds, microlending) and the Bank of Namibia give an unusually complete view of a small financial system very early in a career. In a market this size, that systemic view is a genuine and rare asset.
3. **The domestic private-capital route.** Namibia's pension-fund unlisted-investment requirement creates regulated demand for local private-asset managers in a market with very few qualified ones. This is the clearest structural opportunity in the country (see `03`), and the governance risk is correspondingly high.
4. **The remote-specialist route.** Build a public written track record on southern African and frontier equities that nobody else covers, work remotely for an international firm that wants that coverage, and let geography be the differentiator rather than the handicap.
5. **The quant route.** The most geography-independent of all. Market-maker and systematic-fund hiring is process-driven and merit-tested; relocation is expected and sponsored.

## 7. Moving between tracks

| From → To | Difficulty | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Banking → PE | Standard | The designed path; on-cycle recruiting exists for it |
| Banking → hedge fund | Standard | Usually after 2–4 years; pitch quality decides |
| Consulting → PE / growth equity | Moderate | Commercial diligence is the bridge |
| Equity research → buy-side | Standard | The classic route; sector expertise transfers directly |
| Audit / CA → corporate finance / research | Moderate | **[ZA]/[NA]** The dominant southern African route |
| Risk / middle office → front office | Hard | Requires an internal advocate and usually a formal move |
| Long-only → hedge fund | Moderate | The reverse is easier |
| Hedge fund → long-only | Easy | Often a lifestyle decision |
| PE → hedge fund | Moderate | Public-markets pace is the adjustment |
| Anything → quant research | Hard without the mathematics | The technical bar is real and not bluffable |
| Quant → discretionary | Hard | Different skill; rarely done well |
| Finance → founder/operator | Common | The most frequent exit, and usually a pay cut first |

**The general rule:** moves toward higher technical specificity are hard; moves toward higher commercial generality are easy. And **the first two years determine the option set more than any later decision** — which is an argument for optimising early jobs for skill acquisition and optionality rather than for pay.

## 9. The career risks

**Career risk itself is the biggest distortion.** Keynes's observation that it is better for reputation to fail conventionally than to succeed unconventionally describes the central pathology of professional investing. A manager who underperforms while holding the same stocks as everyone else keeps the job; one who underperforms holding different stocks does not. The observable consequences: closet indexing, benchmark-hugging, herding into consensus positions, and a systematic aversion to the concentrated, contrarian positions that produced every record in `02`. **The people profiled in this domain almost all escaped this by controlling their own capital structure** — a partnership with lock-ups, an investment trust, insurance float, an endowment, or simply owning the firm. That is not incidental; it is the mechanism.

**The pod-shop drawdown-stopout model.** At the large multi-strategy platforms, a PM operates under a tight drawdown limit (commonly reported around 5% for a warning and 10% terminal, `needs-verification`) enforced automatically. This has three effects: extremely short effective time horizons, an inability to hold a position through the drawdown that a correct-but-early thesis produces, and very high turnover of people. Millennium ran ~280 investment teams in 2018; the arithmetic of annual PM turnover in a population that size is unforgiving. The compensation is real and so is the survival probability. Go in with the expected value calculated, not the upside.

**Burnout.** Investment banking analyst hours of 80–100 per week are real, and the industry's periodic reform efforts have not changed the fundamentals. The 2013 death of a Bank of America intern in London and subsequent industry reviews produced protected-weekend policies of varying enforcement. Sell-side and pod-shop roles both have measurable attrition-by-exhaustion.

**Redundancy and cyclicality.** Sell-side headcount is procyclical and cuts are severe in downturns. The 2008–2009, 2015–2016 and 2022–2023 cycles each removed large fractions of some desks. Build savings and portable skills accordingly.

**Skill obsolescence.** Bank prop trading effectively ended after Volcker; research headcount fell sharply after MiFID II unbundling in 2018; discretionary macro shrank and then partly returned. The specific seat you train for may not exist in fifteen years. What transfers is accounting, probability, writing and judgement — not a desk.

**Regulatory and reputational risk.** Personal-account dealing rules, MNPI, expert-network compliance, and the fact that a single enforcement action is career-ending in a way it is not in most industries. Read the compliance manual properly, once, at the start.

## 10. Starting your own vehicle

The end state most of the people in `02` reached, and the least glamorous in practice.

**What it actually requires:**
1. **A track record.** Ideally attributable and verifiable — your own audited account, or a documented sleeve at a previous employer with permission to reference it.
2. **Seed capital.** The realistic sources are friends and family, a former employer, a seeding platform, or a family office. The uncomfortable arithmetic: at 1-and-20 you need roughly $30–50m of AUM to cover a two-person operation's costs before anyone is paid properly, which is why almost every fund starts under-capitalised and runs on the founder's savings for two to three years.
3. **Structure and domicile.** Fund vehicle, management company, prime broker or custodian, administrator, auditor, and legal counsel. Regulatory registration — SEC or state registration (Form ADV, public on EDGAR), FCA authorisation, **[ZA]** FSCA licensing, **[NA]** NAMFISA licensing under the relevant Act. Non-trivial fixed cost in both money and time.
4. **Capital that matches the strategy.** The most important decision and the one most often got wrong. A concentrated, long-horizon strategy funded with monthly-liquidity capital will be destroyed by its own investors at the worst moment. **The structural choices in `02` are the answer to this problem**: Nomad's hurdle-plus-clawback fee and selected investor base; Orbis's refundable performance fee; Schloss's zero management fee and 25%-of-profits-above-high-water-mark; Lindsell Train's closed-end trust; Markel's and Fairfax's insurance float; Yale's endowment.
5. **The willingness to earn nothing for years** while doing operations, compliance and fundraising rather than investing.

**The fee design is a strategy decision, not an administrative one.** A high management fee makes asset gathering rational and performance optional. A performance-only fee with a high-water mark makes the manager's income depend on the client's outcome, caps the incentive to grow, and — as Schloss and Nomad both demonstrated — is compatible with running a small fund very well for decades. Whichever you choose, you are choosing what you will optimise for over the next twenty years.
