# The industry map — what finance actually contains

**Summary.** "Finance" is not one industry but roughly a dozen, with different economics, different daily work, different entry gates and different failure modes. The organising distinction is **sell-side** (banks, brokers, market makers: manufacture, distribute and price risk; earn fees and spreads) versus **buy-side** (asset managers, hedge funds, pensions, insurers, PE/VC: own risk; earn returns on capital). This file walks each area, describing what the work actually is, how people get in, how they are paid, and how careers progress.

> ⚠️ **Compensation caveat.** Compensation figures in this file are drawn from widely reported industry ranges and, where marked, from company financial disclosures. Figures without a named source are marked `needs-verification` and should be treated as order-of-magnitude, not precise. Pay in this industry is dominated by discretionary bonus, varies by 3–5× across firms at the same title, and moves materially with the cycle. Currency is USD unless stated.

## 1. Investment banking (advisory and capital markets)

### M&A / advisory
**The work.** Building and maintaining operating models and merger models; drafting pitchbooks, information memoranda and board materials; running due-diligence processes and data rooms; comparable-company and precedent-transaction analysis; accretion/dilution; managing lawyers, accountants and the client's internal team through a process that runs three to nine months. Junior work is 70% document production and process management, 20% modelling, 10% analysis. Seniority is a sales job: originating mandates through relationships.

**Entry.** Overwhelmingly through the summer analyst programme in the penultimate year of an undergraduate degree, converting to a full-time analyst offer. Lateral entry at associate level from a top MBA, or from Big Four transaction services / audit, or from a boutique. Timing is the binding constraint — see `08`.

**Progression.** Analyst (2–3 yrs) → Associate (3 yrs) → VP (3 yrs) → Director/SVP → Managing Director. Attrition is brutal and intentional: most analysts leave for private equity, corporates or hedge funds after two years.

**Pay** `needs-verification` — bulge-bracket US first-year analyst base of roughly $100k–$110k with a bonus of $40k–$80k has been the widely reported range in 2023–2025; associate all-in commonly $250k–$400k; VP $450k–$700k; MD compensation is heavily origination-linked and ranges from around $1m to many multiples of that. Elite boutiques (Centerview, Evercore, PJT, Moelis) pay materially above bulge brackets at every level.

### ECM (equity capital markets) and DCM (debt capital markets)
**The work.** ECM structures and executes IPOs, follow-ons, convertibles and blocks: it sits between the corporate coverage bankers and the sales-and-trading desk, running bookbuilds, advising on timing, pricing and allocation. DCM does the same for bonds, and is far more calendar-driven and less deal-idiosyncratic. Both are more market-facing and less model-heavy than M&A, with better hours and generally somewhat lower pay at senior levels.

### Leveraged finance
**The work.** Underwriting the debt for LBOs and other levered transactions: credit analysis, capital-structure design, covenant negotiation, syndication. LevFin sits at the junction of banking and credit investing and is one of the strongest launchpads into private credit and distressed investing, because the skill (understanding what a capital structure will do under stress) transfers directly.

## 2. Sales and trading

**Trading.** On the sell-side, a "trader" is usually a market maker in a bank's inventory: quoting prices to clients, managing the resulting risk, hedging. Post-Volcker and post-Basel III, proprietary risk-taking inside banks has shrunk drastically and much of it has migrated to the independent market makers (section 8) and hedge funds. Bank trading now emphasises flow, client franchise and balance-sheet efficiency.

**Sales.** Covering institutional clients: distributing research, ideas, new issues and axes; the job is relationship and information intermediation, and it is compensated on the commission and spread revenue attributable to the coverage.

**Structuring.** Designing bespoke derivative and structured products for client needs — the most quantitative sell-side seat outside of strats/quant.

**Entry.** Analyst programmes, with rotations across desks. Increasingly quantitative even on flow desks. **Pay** `needs-verification`: broadly comparable to banking at analyst/associate level, with much greater dispersion at senior levels — a senior trader on a profitable book can out-earn an MD banker, or be cut in a bad year.

## 3. Equity research

**The work.** Sell-side analysts cover 10–20 companies in a sector: build and maintain earnings models, publish initiation reports and notes, forecast quarters, host management meetings and conferences, and rate stocks. The genuine product is not the rating (which is nearly worthless as a signal) but the sector expertise, channel checks, model granularity and corporate access.

**Why it matters for this domain.** Research is the classic training ground for the buy-side, and several profiled investors passed through it — Terry Smith was a top-rated bank analyst at BZW and then head of UK company research at UBS Phillips & Drew; Howard Marks was an equity research analyst and then director of research at Citicorp from 1969.

**Structural decline.** MiFID II (in force January 2018) unbundled research payments from trading commissions in Europe, which compressed research budgets and headcount materially. Buy-side internal research has partly absorbed the function.

**Pay** `needs-verification`: associate $100k–$200k; publishing analyst $250k–$600k; a highly ranked senior analyst can exceed $1m.

## 4. Asset management (long-only)

**The work.** Managing pooled capital against a benchmark for institutional and retail clients: mutual funds, UCITS, unit trusts, segregated mandates. Roles split into portfolio manager, analyst, product/client-facing, and operations. The daily reality is bounded by the benchmark and the tracking-error budget — a fact that shapes behaviour far more than any stated philosophy.

**The three sub-worlds.**
- **Active fundamental.** Stock-picking against a benchmark. Under structural pressure: 84.34% of US large-cap active funds underperformed the S&P 500 over the ten years to end-2024, and only 32.95% of those present twenty years earlier still existed (SPIVA).
- **Index / passive.** Enormous scale, thin margins, operationally sophisticated. The work is tracking-error minimisation, securities lending, corporate-action handling and cost management — a genuinely different and underrated craft.
- **Factor / systematic ("smart beta").** Rules-based exposure to value, quality, momentum, size, low-volatility. Sits between the two above and has substantially cannibalised the alpha that discretionary value managers once earned.

**Entry.** Graduate schemes, research analyst roles, transfers from sell-side research, CFA-supported career changers. **Pay** `needs-verification`: analyst $80k–$180k; PM at a large house $300k–$1m+, generally lower and much less volatile than hedge funds.

## 5. Hedge funds by strategy

The label is a legal-structure description (private fund, performance fee, limited redemption) not a strategy description. A.W. Jones's 1949 template — long/short equity, leverage, 20% of profits — remains the archetype.

### Long/short equity
Fundamental stock selection with a short book. Sub-styles: market-neutral (beta ≈ 0), variable-bias, sector-specialist (TMT, healthcare, financials, energy). This is the largest and most crowded strategy. Entry from banking, research or consulting; the interview is a stock pitch (see `08`).

### Global macro
Directional positions in rates, FX, sovereign credit, commodities and index-level equity, driven by views on policy, growth and inflation. Discretionary macro is the least systematisable seat in the industry and has the widest outcome dispersion.

### Event-driven / merger arbitrage
Trading announced deals, spin-offs, restructurings, index events and special situations. The analytical unit is *the probability and timing of a defined corporate event*, which makes it explicitly a base-rate discipline. Joel Greenblatt's *You Can Be a Stock Market Genius* (1997) is the best public description of the spin-off/special-situations end of it.

### Distressed and credit
Buying debt of stressed or defaulted issuers, often with the intention of controlling the reorganisation and converting to equity. Requires bankruptcy-law literacy alongside credit analysis. Howard Marks and Bruce Karsh co-organised what Wikipedia describes as the first distressed-debt fund at a major financial institution in 1988 (at TCW) and founded Oaktree in 1995.

### Quant / statistical arbitrage
Systematic, model-driven, high-breadth, typically market-neutral. Renaissance Technologies is the extreme case: founded 1978 as Monemetrics by Jim Simons and Howard Morgan, renamed 1982, Medallion launched 1988, and famously staffed by scientists — roughly 150 researchers and programmers, about half with PhDs, with Wall Street experience explicitly disfavoured. See `07`.

### CTA / managed futures
Systematic trend-following across futures markets. Long-horizon momentum, high capacity, low correlation to equities, long stretches of dull or negative returns punctuated by crisis-period gains. Intellectually the simplest well-documented systematic strategy.

### Multi-strategy "pod shops"
Millennium (founded 1989 by Israel Englander and Ronald Shear with $35m; ~280 investment teams by 2018; >$87bn AUM by May 2026), Citadel (founded 1990 by Ken Griffin; $67bn AUM Jan 2026; $90.4bn cumulative net gains to Dec 2025), Point72, Balyasny, ExodusPoint, Schonfeld.

**How the model works.** Capital is allocated to many semi-autonomous teams ("pods"), each running a tightly risk-constrained book, with centralised risk management, financing and technology. Correlations between pods are actively managed toward zero, and the platform levers the aggregate. Returns are consistent because they are engineered to be, not because any individual pod is exceptional.

**The stop-out.** The defining career fact of the pod world: a PM who breaches a drawdown limit — commonly in the region of 5% at a warning level and 10% terminal, though limits vary by firm and are not publicly standardised (`needs-verification`) — has the book cut and is often terminated. This produces very high turnover, short effective time horizons, and a distinctive psychological profile.

**Pay.** Pass-through expense structures and formulaic payouts (a share of the pod's P&L, commonly quoted in the 10–20% range, `needs-verification`) mean successful pod PMs are among the highest-paid people in the industry, with guarantees for senior hires reportedly reaching eight figures. The other side of the distribution is unemployment within eighteen months.

**Fees.** The classic 2-and-20 has been eroded at the low end (1-and-15, or 1-and-10 with hurdles) and re-inflated at the top (pass-through structures at the large multi-strats; Medallion historically at 5-and-44, `needs-verification` for the exact numbers, which are consistently reported but not disclosed in a filing I fetched).

## 6. Private equity, growth equity, venture capital

### Buyout / private equity
Acquiring control of companies using debt, improving them, and exiting in 3–7 years. Work: sourcing, LBO modelling, diligence, financing, board work, exit. **Entry** is overwhelmingly from investment-banking analyst programmes, via an intensely compressed "on-cycle" recruiting process that in the US has repeatedly moved earlier — in some years starting within months of analysts beginning their banking jobs.

**Economics.** Management fee (traditionally 2% of committed capital) plus carried interest (traditionally 20% of profits above a preferred return, commonly 8%). Carry is the wealth engine and vests over years; junior staff receive little or none.

**Progression.** Associate (2–3 yrs) → Senior associate → VP → Principal → Partner. **Pay** `needs-verification`: associate all-in $250k–$400k at large funds; VP $400k–$700k; principal/partner dominated by carry.

### Growth equity
Minority stakes in fast-growing, usually already-revenue-generating companies. Less leverage, more commercial diligence, blends PE process discipline with VC judgement.

### Venture capital
Minority stakes in early-stage companies where the return distribution is a power law: a small number of investments return the fund. The job is deal flow, judgement about founders and markets, and post-investment support. **Entry** is the least systematised in finance — operators, engineers, founders and product people enter directly; there is no reliable pipeline. Compensation is modest until carry lands, and carry may never land.

## 7. Private credit, real estate, infrastructure

**Private credit** — direct lending to mid-market companies, replacing what banks did before post-2008 capital rules made it unattractive. One of the fastest-growing segments of the last decade. Skills: credit analysis, covenant design, workout capability. Downside risk is asymmetric and the historic default experience is untested through a severe, prolonged default cycle.

**Real estate** — equity and debt across development, value-add and core strategies. Highly local, relationship-driven, capital-structure-intensive. Analytical unit is the property-level cash-flow model plus the financing stack.

**Infrastructure** — long-duration, often regulated or contracted assets (toll roads, utilities, fibre, renewables). The closest thing in finance to an actuarial discipline: the returns depend on concession terms, regulatory regimes and inflation linkage rather than on growth. **[NA]** Namibian renewable-energy projects have been financed partly through this channel, including via GIPF's unlisted-investment mandate (see `03`).

## 8. Market making and high-frequency trading

**Firms.** Jane Street (founded 1999 by Tim Reynolds, Robert Granieri, Marc Gerstein and Michael Jenkins — three of them from Susquehanna), Citadel Securities, Optiver (founded 9 April 1986 in Amsterdam by Johann Kaemingk, Ruud Vlek and Chris Oomen), IMC, SIG (founded May 1987 by eight partners including Jeff Yass and Arthur Dantchik, who had met playing cards at SUNY Binghamton), Jump Trading, DRW, Hudson River Trading, Tower Research, Flow Traders.

**The work.** Continuously quoting two-sided markets and earning the spread while managing inventory risk; index and ETF arbitrage; options market making with delta/gamma/vega hedging; latency-sensitive execution. The intellectual core is probability, expected value under uncertainty, and speed of both computation and decision.

**Culture and structure.** Flat, technical, and compensation-egalitarian relative to banks — Jane Street notably has no CEO, is led informally by 30–40 senior people, and pays all employees on firm-wide profit rather than personal trading P&L. This is the deliberate opposite of the pod-shop model, and the two structures produce very different behaviour.

**Entry.** Direct from undergraduate or PhD, via mental-arithmetic, probability, game and estimation interviews (see `08`). Target degrees: mathematics, physics, computer science, statistics, and increasingly competitive-programming or olympiad backgrounds. SIG's cultural association with poker is well documented in the firm's public profile, though the existence of a formal poker training programme is `needs-verification`.

**Pay.** The highest entry-level compensation in finance. Reported first-year packages at the top firms in 2024–2025 in the $300k–$450k range are consistent with the revenue-per-head arithmetic above, but the specific figures are `needs-verification`.

**Technology.** Jane Street's use of OCaml across the firm is unusual and well documented; most peers run C++ with Python research layers.

## 9. Insurance and reinsurance

**Why it belongs here.** Insurance generates *float* — premiums held before claims are paid — which is investable capital that cannot be redeemed at a bad moment. This is the purest form of permanent capital available outside an endowment, and it underlies two of the profiles in this domain: Markel under Tom Gayner and Fairfax under Prem Watsa (book value per share compounded at 18.7% a year since 1985, per the company's own reporting; share price 19.2% including dividends, from CDN$3.25 in 1985 to over CDN$2,000 at year-end 2024).

**Careers.** Actuarial (a formal, examination-gated profession), underwriting, claims, reinsurance broking, catastrophe modelling, and insurance-company investment management. Actuarial qualification is the most rigorously credentialed path in finance and among the most reliably employable.

---

## 10. Fintech, corporate finance and treasury, risk

**Fintech** — payments, lending, neobanks, wealthtech, infrastructure. Blends product and engineering with financial-domain knowledge; pay is equity-weighted and highly variable.

**Corporate finance and treasury** — inside a non-financial company: capital structure, cash and liquidity management, FX and commodity hedging, banking relationships, investor relations, FP&A. Lower pay, far better hours, and the closest daily contact with how businesses actually generate cash — which is why it is an underrated preparation for investing.

**Risk** — market risk, credit risk, liquidity risk, model validation, and the whole regulatory apparatus (Basel III/IV, IFRS 9, stress testing). Quantitative, secure, systematically paid below the front office, and the most reliable route into a bank for someone with a quantitative master's and no target-school pedigree.

---

## 11. Endowments, pensions and sovereign funds (the allocator seat)

Distinct from all of the above: the allocator does not pick securities but picks managers and sets asset allocation. David Swensen's Yale office is the canonical example, and it produced an unusual number of people who went on to run other endowments (see `02`). The seat requires manager due diligence, fee negotiation, liquidity and spending-rule management, and governance. Pay is far below the funds being allocated to; the compensation is intellectual breadth and access. **[NA]** In Namibia this seat is occupied principally by the GIPF and the larger corporate retirement funds (see `03`).
