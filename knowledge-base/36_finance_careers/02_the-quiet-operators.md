# The quiet operators — fifteen profiles

**Summary.** Fifteen people (and one collective) whose records are documented and whose names are known mainly inside the industry. Each profile separates four things with different evidential status: education and career sequence (usually verifiable), stated approach (verifiable as a statement, not as an explanation of returns), track record (a claim, with a claimant and a period), and the transferable lesson (my inference).

> ⚠️ **Track record caveat, applying to every profile below.** Every performance figure in this file is a *claim*, attributed where possible to whoever made it and bounded by the period stated. None was verified against an audited fund document. All of these people are selected because they succeeded; the far larger population who used similar methods and failed is invisible. Read `00_overview.md` before drawing conclusions.

---

## 1. Jim Simons (1938–2024) — the mathematician who refused to hire financiers

**Education.** BS Mathematics, MIT, 1958 (completed in three years). PhD Mathematics, UC Berkeley, 1961, at 23, under Bertram Kostant; dissertation *On the transitivity of holonomy systems* (1962).

**Career sequence.** Taught at MIT, then Harvard. 1964–1968: code-breaker at the Institute for Defense Analyses, Communications Research Division, working for the NSA — his first exposure to pattern extraction from noisy signal, and where he first attempted a trading venture (iStar, with Richard Leibler). Left over his public opposition to the Vietnam War. 1968–1978: chaired the mathematics department at Stony Brook for a decade, building it from obscurity. With Shiing-Shen Chern he produced the Chern–Simons form, a piece of differential geometry that later became foundational to topological quantum field theory and string theory — genuine first-rank mathematics, recognised by the AMS Oswald Veblen Prize in Geometry in 1976. Founded Monemetrics in 1978 (with Howard Morgan), renamed Renaissance Technologies in 1982; launched the Medallion Fund in 1988. Retired as CEO in late 2009, remained non-executive chairman until 2021, died 10 May 2024 aged 86 with a Forbes-assessed net worth of $31.4bn.

**The approach.** Simons's central organisational decision was to hire scientists rather than financiers. Renaissance employs roughly 150 researchers and programmers, about half with PhDs, and Wall Street experience is explicitly disfavoured. The logic is precise: a person trained in markets arrives with priors — narratives about why prices move — and those priors contaminate a purely statistical search. A physicist or a computational linguist arrives with a method for extracting weak signals from noisy data and no theory of why the data behaves as it does. Simons wanted the second. The recruitment of Robert Mercer and Peter Brown from IBM's speech-recognition group in 1993 is the clearest expression of this: speech recognition is the problem of inferring hidden states from noisy sequential observations, which is the same shape of problem as short-horizon price prediction.

**Track record (claimed).** Medallion is reported as having returned an average of 66.1% gross / 39.1% net annually from 1988 to 2018 — the enormous gap is the fee structure. It gained 98.2% in 2008 while the S&P 500 fell 38.5%, and 76% in 2020. It has been closed to outside money since 1993 and is now employee-only. The honest counterweight: Renaissance's *external* funds have not replicated this. RIEF and RIDA have historically lagged Medallion by roughly 17–19 percentage points (as of April 2020), and in 2020 the long-biased fund fell ~20%, the market-neutral fund ~27% and the global equities fund ~25% — while Medallion rose 76%. Capacity, not insight, is the constraint, and the strategy that produced the record is not available to buy.

**What he read.** Mathematics, not finance. The intellectual lineage is Kolmogorov, hidden Markov models (Leonard Baum, his early collaborator, co-invented the Baum–Welch algorithm), and information theory.

**Transferable lesson.** Import a method from a field where it is mature into a field where it is not, and hire for the method rather than the domain. Also: the fee structure and the capacity limit *are* the strategy. Medallion's returns are inseparable from its refusal to grow.

## 2. The Renaissance scientists — Baum, Ax, Berlekamp, Laufer, Mercer, Brown

Treating them collectively makes a point that individual profiles obscure: the record belongs to a research organisation, not a person.

**Leonard Baum** — IDA cryptanalyst, co-inventor of the Baum–Welch algorithm for hidden Markov models; built Renaissance's first models. **James Ax** — algebraist (Cole Prize winner), extended Baum's models, ran the fund until departing in 1989 after roughly 30% peak-to-trough losses by April of that year.

**Elwyn Berlekamp** (1940–2019) — BS and MS in electrical engineering, MIT, 1962; PhD EE, MIT, 1964, advised by Robert Gallager, Peter Elias, **Claude Shannon** and John Wozencraft. Professor at Berkeley; co-inventor of the Berlekamp–Massey algorithm used in Reed–Solomon error correction; a founder of combinatorial game theory (*Winning Ways for your Mathematical Plays*, with Conway and Guy, 1982). With Sandor Straus he revamped the Medallion system after the 1989 crisis; 1990 produced a reported 55.9% gain. Note the pedigree: the man who fixed Medallion was a coding theorist trained by Shannon, whose other career was the mathematics of games.

**Henry Laufer** — consultant, then long-term chief scientist, central to the 1989–1990 overhaul. **Robert Mercer** — BSc physics and mathematics, University of New Mexico; PhD computer science, University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign, 1972; IBM Research from autumn 1972, working under Frederick Jelinek and with Lalit Bahl on speech recognition and statistical machine translation (Brown clustering, the IBM alignment models); ACL Lifetime Achievement Award, June 2014. Joined Renaissance 1993, recruited by Nick Patterson; co-CEO 2009/2010 with Peter Brown; stepped down November 2017. **Peter Fitzhugh Brown** — BA mathematics, Harvard; PhD computer science, Carnegie Mellon, under **Geoffrey Hinton**; IBM speech group from 1984; lead author on the IBM alignment models; joined Renaissance 1993 after Simons offered to double his IBM salary; partner 1995; EVP 2003; co-CEO 2010; sole CEO from November 2017.

**Transferable lesson.** Every one of these people was already excellent at something hard and unrelated before they touched a market. None of them retrained. The organisation converted existing expertise rather than manufacturing new expertise — and the two 1989–1990 rescues show that the record was not a smooth inevitability but survived a near-death event.

## 3. Ed Thorp (b. 1932) — the first quant

**Education.** Began at UC Berkeley, transferred to UCLA after a year; BA in physics; PhD in mathematics, UCLA, 1958.

**Career sequence.** MIT 1959–1961 (where he worked with Claude Shannon); professor of mathematics at New Mexico State 1961–1965; UC Irvine 1965–1977, then professor of mathematics *and finance* 1977–1982. *Beat the Dealer* (1962, substantially revised 1966) proved that blackjack was beatable by card counting — the first mathematically rigorous demonstration that a casino game offered positive expectation to a disciplined player. With Shannon he built what is generally regarded as the first wearable computer, for roulette, in 1960–61. *Beat the Market* (1967, with Sheen Kassouf) applied the same logic to warrants and convertibles, describing delta-hedged convertible arbitrage — before Black–Scholes was published in 1973.

Princeton/Newport Partners ran 1969–1989 as a market-neutral derivatives hedging fund. Ridgeline Partners ran August 1994 – September 2002 doing statistical arbitrage. As of May 1998 he reported an annualised ~20% rate of return averaged over 28.5 years. His autobiography, *A Man for All Markets*, appeared in 2017.

**The approach.** Thorp's method is a single idea applied in four domains: find a situation with a computable edge, size the bet by the Kelly criterion (which he learned from Kelly's 1956 paper), and repeat with enough independence that the law of large numbers does the work. He was explicit about the modification that matters in practice — fractional Kelly, because full Kelly's drawdowns are intolerable and the edge estimate is itself uncertain.

**Track record (claimed).** The ~20% over 28.5 years is Thorp's own statement as of 1998. Princeton/Newport did not have a losing year over its life, a claim frequently repeated but which I could not verify from a filing (`needs-verification`).

**What he read.** Kelly's *A New Interpretation of Information Rate* (1956); Shannon; the academic finance literature, which he read and then outpaced. He famously identified an early error in Black–Scholes-style pricing before the formula was published.

**Transferable lesson.** The edge and the sizing are separate problems, and most people solve only the first. Also: Thorp left blackjack when the casinos adapted and left convertible arbitrage when it became crowded. Knowing when an edge has decayed is a distinct skill from finding it.

## 4. David Swensen (1954–2021) — the allocator who rebuilt an asset class

**Education.** BA and BS, University of Wisconsin–River Falls, 1975 (his father and grandfather were chemistry professors there). PhD in economics, Yale, under **James Tobin** (Nobel laureate) and William Brainard; dissertation *A Model for the Valuation of Corporate Bonds*.

**Career sequence.** Salomon Brothers 1980–1981 in corporate finance, where he worked on the first currency swap — the 1981 IBM/World Bank transaction that created the swap market. Lehman Brothers 1981–1985, senior vice-president, helping build the swap business. Became Yale's Chief Investment Officer on 1 April 1985 at 31, taking an enormous pay cut, and held the job until his death on 5 May 2021.

**The approach — the "endowment model."** Swensen's insight was that a perpetual institution with a modest annual spending rate has an unusual asset: it can bear illiquidity. Conventional endowment portfolios in 1985 were 60/40 domestic stocks and bonds. Swensen argued that equity-like assets should dominate, that diversification should be across genuinely different return drivers rather than across managers of the same asset, and that the illiquidity premium in private equity, venture capital, real estate, timber and absolute-return strategies was systematically underexploited. He paired this with a distinctive manager-selection philosophy: back small, hungry, aligned, owner-operated managers rather than large institutions, and hold them for decades.

**Track record (claimed).** Yale's endowment grew from $1.3bn in 1985 to $31.2bn in 2021 after disbursements, with average annualised returns of 12.4% over 1990–2020 (Yale's own reporting). Two honest caveats: much of the return came from access to top-quartile venture and private-equity managers that Yale obtained partly through being early and prestigious — an advantage not available to a later imitator — and the model's mass adoption arguably eliminated the illiquidity premium it exploited. The 2008–09 liquidity crisis exposed institutions that had copied the allocation without copying the governance.

**The tree of protégés.** Swensen's office produced an unusual number of CIOs at other institutions — among them Andrew Golden (Princeton), Seth Alexander (MIT), Paula Volent (Bowdoin, later Rockefeller University), Ellen Shuman (Carnegie Corporation), Randy Kim, Donna Dean and Lauren Meserve. The exact roster and dates are `needs-verification`; Wikipedia confirms only that former staff went on to manage endowments at MIT, Stanford and Penn. The mechanism is worth noting: he taught a course, ran a small office, and treated training successors as part of the job.

**What he wrote and read.** *Pioneering Portfolio Management* (2000, rev. 2009) for institutions; *Unconventional Success* (2005) for individuals — the second is a blunt argument that individuals should index, from the man who beat the market for three decades. That combination is the most intellectually honest act in this file.

**Transferable lesson.** Structural advantage beats cleverness. Swensen's edge was the *shape of his capital* — perpetual, patient, with a governance structure that let him hold through drawdowns — and he engineered the portfolio to exploit it. Ask what your capital can bear that others' cannot.

## 5. Nick Sleep and Qais Zakaria — Nomad Investment Partnership

**Education.** Sleep: geography, University of Edinburgh (`needs-verification` — not confirmed from a source I fetched). Zakaria: `needs-verification`.

**Career sequence.** Both worked at Marathon Asset Management in London, the firm whose capital-cycle framework (published as *Capital Returns*, ed. Edward Chancellor, 2015) shaped their thinking. They launched the Nomad Investment Partnership in September 2001, closed it to new capital in 2004, and wound it up in 2014, returning capital to investors while both were still in their forties. `needs-verification` on all dates — I was unable to fetch a primary source for Nomad, and there is no Wikipedia article for Sleep, Zakaria or Nomad.

**The approach.** Three ideas, all set out in the partnership letters:

*Scale economics shared.* Most businesses that achieve scale advantages keep the benefit as margin. A rare few — Costco, Amazon, GEICO, Nebraska Furniture Mart — give the benefit back to customers as lower prices, which grows volume, which deepens the scale advantage. This creates a self-reinforcing loop that the market systematically undervalues because it looks like margin destruction in the near term and only compounds over a decade.

*Destination analysis.* Rather than forecasting next year's earnings, ask what the business looks like at maturity, and whether the current management is genuinely optimising for that destination.

*Deliberate structural design.* The fee arrangement was unusual and consciously chosen: no management fee sufficient to enrich the managers, a performance fee above a 6% hurdle with a high-water mark, and — the distinctive part — a clawback mechanism so that fees earned in good years could be returned if later years disappointed. Sleep and Zakaria also selected investors for temperament, and later committed the great majority of their personal proceeds to charity.

**Track record (claimed) — `needs-verification`.** The figure repeated everywhere is a cumulative return of roughly 921% before fees (around 720% after fees) from September 2001 to the end of 2013, against roughly 117% for the MSCI World Index — implying an annualised figure in the low-to-mid 20s. **I could not fetch a primary source for this.** It originates in the partnership's own final letters, which circulate in PDF form; treat it as the managers' self-report over a period they selected, which happens to begin close to the post-dot-com bottom.

**What they read.** The letters cite Buffett's partnership letters, Munger, Robert Cialdini's *Influence*, Marathon's capital-cycle work, and a steady diet of business history. The letters themselves are now a primary text (see `05`).

**Transferable lesson.** The most replicable thing about Nomad is not the stock selection but the *architecture*: a fee structure that removed the incentive to gather assets, an investor base selected for patience, a small portfolio (concentrated to the point of holding Amazon and Costco at very large weights), and a written record that forced the thinking to be explicit. They then closed the fund rather than let it grow into something worse. Very few managers voluntarily cap their own income.

## 6. Chuck Akre (b. 1942/43) — the English major and the three-legged stool

**Education.** Blair Academy; American University, BA in **English literature** — he began as a pre-med student and switched.

**Career sequence.** Entered the securities business in 1968. Long tenure at Johnston, Lemon & Co. as shareholder, director, head of the asset management division and director of research. Founded Akre Capital Management in 1989, initially operating within Friedman, Billings, Ramsey & Co.; ACM became independent in 2000; moved to Middleburg, Virginia in early 2002 while managing the FBR Focus Fund; launched the Akre Focus Fund in 2009. The firm managed approximately $6.7bn across an ETF, a private partnership and separately managed accounts as at 31 July 2026 (firm's own website).

**The approach — the three-legged stool.** Akre keeps an actual three-legged milking stool in his office. The three legs are:
1. **An extraordinary business** — one with a durable competitive advantage that produces high returns on owners' capital.
2. **Talented management of high integrity** — people who both run the business well and treat outside shareholders as partners.
3. **Reinvestment opportunity and skill** — a large runway to redeploy the cash the business generates at similarly high rates, and a management team that has demonstrated it can do so.

The third leg is the one Akre emphasises and most investors underweight. A business earning 30% on capital that can only reinvest a fifth of its earnings compounds far more slowly than one earning 20% that can reinvest all of it. He calls the resulting businesses "compounding machines," and the seat's discipline is then to buy them at a price that does not pre-consume the compounding, and to sit still.

The stool metaphor has a second meaning he likes: it is low, so if it tips over you do not fall far.

**Track record (claimed).** Kiplinger reported FBR Focus at 12.5% annualised from December 1996 to October 2009, roughly ten percentage points a year ahead of the Russell 2000 Growth index. That is the cleanest independently reported figure available for him; the private partnership's record is not public.

**Transferable lesson.** An English literature degree was no impediment, because the job is reading a business and judging people. And the *reinvestment* question — not the quality question, not the price question — is the one that separates a good business from a good compounder.

## 7. Terry Smith (b. 1953) — the analyst who was sacked for being right

**Education.** University College Cardiff, first-class degree in **history**, 1974. MBA, Henley Management College, 1979.

**Career sequence.** Barclays Bank 1973–1984 (starting in a branch); research analyst at W. Greenwell & Co.; Barclays de Zoete Wedd 1984–1989, where he was the top-rated UK bank analyst; UBS Phillips & Drew 1990–1992 as head of UK company research. In 1992 he published *Accounting for Growth*, which dissected the accounting devices FTSE 100 companies used to flatter earnings — using named companies, several of them UBS clients. UBS demanded withdrawal; Smith and Random House refused; UBS suspended and then sued him, settling out of court after eighteen months. The book sold over 100,000 copies and displaced *A Brief History of Time* at the top of the bestseller list. He then ran Collins Stewart (1992–2006) and Tullett Prebon (2003–2014), taking both public. He founded Fundsmith in 2010, at 57.

**The approach.** Three sentences, which he repeats verbatim and has never varied: *buy good companies; don't overpay; do nothing.* "Good" is defined operationally — high and stable return on capital employed, funded by internally generated cash, in businesses selling small-ticket repeat-purchase items that are not vulnerable to technological change, with no or minimal leverage. "Don't overpay" is enforced with a free-cash-flow yield discipline rather than P/E. "Do nothing" is the hardest and the most distinctive: portfolio turnover is deliberately kept very low, and he publishes the annual cost of dealing as a line item to shame the industry.

His annual letters are notable for reporting the portfolio's *look-through* financial characteristics — the weighted-average ROCE, gross margin, cash conversion and leverage of the underlying businesses versus the index — which converts a philosophy into an audited number.

**Track record (claimed).** Fundsmith Equity Fund is a UK-authorised fund with published, independently calculated performance; Smith reports it in his annual letters against the MSCI World. The firm managed over £35bn as at December 2022. I did not fetch the fund's published factsheet, so specific annualised figures are `needs-verification` — but unlike most records in this file, this one *is* independently verifiable from regulated fund documentation.

**Transferable lesson.** A history degree, a career spent as an accounting sceptic, and a firm founded at 57. The forensic-accounting phase was not a detour: knowing precisely how earnings are manipulated is what allows a quality investor to trust the ones that are not.

## 8. Nick Train and Michael Lindsell — the concentrated franchise buyers

**Education.** Train: modern history, Queen's College, Oxford (`needs-verification` — not confirmed from a fetched source; Wikipedia has no article on him). Lindsell: `needs-verification`.

**Career sequence.** The two met at GT Management. Train subsequently worked at M&G. They founded Lindsell Train in 2000, naming it after themselves. Their first external client mandate was the Finsbury Growth & Income Trust at the end of 2000; the Lindsell Train Investment Trust launched on the LSE in 2001; the UK Equity Fund in 2006; they took over the Japanese Equity Fund in 2009; the Global Equity Fund launched in 2011. AUM was £12.8bn as at January 2025 (reported May 2025) — down substantially from the firm's peak, which is itself part of the story.

**The approach.** Extreme concentration (typically 20–35 holdings) in businesses with durable, usually brand- or franchise-based, competitive positions — Diageo, Burberry, Nintendo, Unilever, London Stock Exchange Group, Sage, Hargreaves Lansdown — bought with the explicit intention of never selling. Turnover is close to zero; Train has described holding periods measured in decades and has written that the ideal is to own a business through several generations of its management. Ownership of the *intangible* asset — a brand, a data set, a network, an exchange — is the organising idea.

**Track record (claimed).** Lindsell Train's funds are UK-regulated with published performance. The record is genuinely two-sided: a very strong first fifteen years followed by a severe and prolonged period of underperformance from roughly 2020 onward as the concentrated consumer-franchise style fell out of favour and AUM fell sharply. `needs-verification` on the specific figures. This is the most useful profile in the file precisely because the drawdown is visible and ongoing — it is what the surviving records of others would look like if we could see them mid-crisis.

**Transferable lesson.** Concentration plus permanence produces long stretches of both spectacular and dismal relative performance from the *same* portfolio and the *same* process. The style did not stop working when performance stopped; either the price of quality had risen too far, or the franchises were less durable than believed — and distinguishing those two is the entire question. Also: an investment trust (closed-end vehicle) cannot be redeemed, which is why Lindsell Train's structure has let them hold through it.

## 9. Chris Hohn (b. October 1966) — activism as a fiduciary technology

**Education.** University of Southampton, first-class honours in **accounting and business economics**, 1988. Harvard Business School MBA, 1993, graduating as a Baker Scholar (top 5% of the class). His father was a Jamaican-born car mechanic; his mother a legal secretary — a background almost unique among managers of his scale.

**Career sequence.** Briefly at Coopers & Lybrand; Apax Partners from 1994; Perry Capital in New York from 1996, running its London operation from 1998; founded The Children's Investment Fund Management (TCI) in 2003. Knighted KCMG in the 2014 Birthday Honours for services to philanthropy and international development.

**The approach.** TCI runs a highly concentrated portfolio of large, high-quality, cash-generative businesses — infrastructure-like assets such as railroads, airports, exchanges, ratings agencies, aerospace, and a small number of large-cap technology names — combined with willingness to engage publicly and aggressively with management when he believes value is being destroyed. Hohn's activism is unusual in being applied to *good* businesses to make them better rather than to bad ones to break them up: the campaign against Deutsche Börse's bid for the LSE (2005), the ABN AMRO letter (2007) that precipitated the bank's break-up, the "Say on Climate" initiative requiring companies to put emissions plans to a shareholder vote, and the November 2022 letter to Alphabet's Sundar Pichai demanding headcount reduction and cost discipline at Waymo.

**The philanthropic structure.** The original TCI design was distinctive and directly relevant to this domain's themes: the fund contractually donated to the Children's Investment Fund Foundation on a formula — 0.5% of fund assets per year, plus a further 0.5% for every year in which returns exceeded 11%. The contractual arrangement ended following divorce proceedings that began in 2012. The point is structural: the fee design made the charitable outcome a function of investment performance, which is a form of alignment almost nobody else has attempted.

**Track record (claimed).** TCI's returns are not publicly disclosed by the firm and were not verifiable from a source I fetched (`needs-verification`). Press reports of very large multi-year gains circulate; treat them as press reports.

**Transferable lesson.** Concentration plus voice. Most investors accept a company as they find it; an owner with a large enough stake and enough public credibility can change the asset itself. And the Southampton-accounting-degree-to-Baker-Scholar path is a reminder that the credential ladder can be climbed rather than inherited.

## 10. Thomas A. Russo (b. 1955) — the "capacity to suffer"

**Education.** Dartmouth College, BA, 1977; Stanford, joint JD/MBA, 1984. `needs-verification` — the Wikipedia article titled *Thomas A. Russo* is a different person (a Lehman Brothers lawyer), and I could not fetch the firm's site (robots-disallowed). The details here are widely reported but unconfirmed by a source I retrieved.

**Career sequence.** He attended Warren Buffett's guest lecture at Stanford Business School in 1982, which he has repeatedly described as the decisive event of his career. Worked at Ruane, Cunniff & Co. (the Sequoia Fund firm) before joining Gardner Investments in Lancaster, Pennsylvania in 1989, which became Gardner Russo & Gardner and later Gardner Russo & Quinn. AUM in the region of $9–10bn in recent years, largely in separately managed accounts (`needs-verification`).

**The approach.** Russo buys global consumer-brand companies — Nestlé, Heineken, Pernod Ricard, Philip Morris International, Unilever, Richemont, Berkshire, Mastercard — held for decades, typically family- or foundation-controlled, and typically bought once and never sold. Two ideas are his own contribution to the vocabulary:

*Capacity to reinvest.* The company must have a large emerging-market or category runway into which it can pour capital.

*Capacity to suffer.* The controlling family or board must be willing to depress reported profits for years — spending on brand-building, distribution and market entry that hits the income statement immediately and pays off a decade later — without being punished by a management incentive scheme or a quarterly-earnings culture. Public companies with dispersed ownership almost never have this. Family-controlled ones sometimes do. Russo's portfolio is essentially a bet that the market persistently misprices this willingness.

**Track record (claimed).** Long-term outperformance over multiple decades is reported in interviews and conference appearances; no independently audited figure is available. `needs-verification`.

**Transferable lesson.** The most valuable corporate attribute may be one that never appears in a financial ratio: an ownership structure that permits deliberate short-term pain. Identifying it requires reading proxy statements, ownership registers and family history — not spreadsheets.

## 11. François Rochon — Giverny Capital

**Education.** Rochon trained as an **engineer** and is a serious collector of art (Giverny is named after Monet's garden). The specific institution and degree are `needs-verification` — the firm's website gives no biography and there is no Wikipedia article.

**Career sequence.** Began managing his own money in 1993 (the "Rochon Global Portfolio"), founded Giverny Capital in Montreal in 1998, and later established Giverny Capital Asset Management in New York. He publishes a detailed annual letter every year, in the Buffett format, including — unusually — an explicit annual review of his own mistakes.

**The approach.** A concentrated portfolio (roughly 20–25 holdings) of high-quality North American businesses with high returns on equity, owner-oriented management and predictable earnings, held for very long periods. His stated framework is the "three rules": companies with growing intrinsic value, competent and honest management, and a purchase price well below intrinsic value. His most distinctive published idea is the **owner's earnings versus market price gap** — he tracks, each year, the growth in the intrinsic value (measured as growth in earnings plus dividends) of his portfolio companies against the change in their market prices, and shows that over long periods the two converge while over short periods they diverge wildly. This turns "the market is a voting machine in the short run and a weighing machine in the long run" into a table he publishes annually.

**Track record (claimed) — `needs-verification`.** Giverny's letters report the Rochon Global Portfolio's returns since 1993 against a blended benchmark, with the manager claiming meaningful annualised outperformance over three decades. This is a self-reported personal-portfolio composite; the letters state the methodology, but no independent audit is public.

**Transferable lesson.** The annual public accounting of one's own errors is a genuine technology, not a rhetorical flourish. So is separating *business* results from *stock* results in the reporting: it tells you whether the thesis or the market is what went wrong.

## 12. Robert Vinall (b. c. 1976) — RV Capital

**Education.** `needs-verification` — his firm's website carries no biography, and there is no Wikipedia article. He is British, worked in London before moving to Switzerland, and is reported to have worked at Sloane Robinson prior to founding his own vehicle (unconfirmed).

**Career sequence.** Founded RV Capital in 2006, based in Kilchberg, Switzerland; advises the Business Owner Fund; runs separately managed accounts for institutional clients including major endowments; Andreas Lechner is co-managing director. He hosts an annual investor gathering in Engelberg every January, which has become a fixture of the European value-investing calendar.

**The approach.** Four questions, published on the firm's site and applied to every candidate:
1. Will the company be around and flourishing in ten or more years' time?
2. Is the company building a long-term competitive advantage?
3. Does the management set the right example?
4. Is the price attractive?

The portfolio is around ten positions, global, sector-agnostic, with the stated intention of permanent ownership once a position is established. The name — Business Owner Fund — is the thesis: he buys businesses run by their owners, on the grounds that owner-operators think in decades and salaried managers think in quarters.

**Track record (claimed).** Not published on the firm's website and not verifiable from a source I fetched. Vinall's annual letters report the fund's returns since 2008 with claimed substantial outperformance of the MSCI World; he has also written candidly about severe drawdowns. `needs-verification`.

**Transferable lesson.** A one-person research operation running ten positions can compete with institutions, and the binding constraint is not information but the ability to hold. The written annual letter plus the annual gathering are how he recruits capital that will let him hold — the capital-shaping insight again.

## 13. Tom Gayner (b. 1961) — compounding inside an insurance company

**Education.** University of Virginia, BS in commerce/accounting; qualified as a CPA. `needs-verification` — Markel's leadership pages were not fetchable and there is no Wikipedia biography; the UVA and CPA details are widely reported but unconfirmed here.

**Career sequence.** PriceWaterhouse as an accountant; then equity analyst at Davenport & Company in Richmond covering, among others, Markel; joined Markel in 1990 (`needs-verification` on the year) to run its investment operation, through the entity Markel-Gayner Asset Management. Became president and chief investment officer in 2010; co-CEO alongside Richard R. Whitt III from 1 January 2016; subsequently sole CEO of what is now Markel Group. Markel joined the Fortune 500 in 2016. He has served on the boards of Graham Holdings, Coca-Cola Consolidated, Cable ONE and the Davis Series funds (`needs-verification` on the current roster).

**The approach.** Gayner's stated four-part filter for an equity investment:
1. Profitable businesses with good returns on capital and modest leverage.
2. Management with equal measures of talent and integrity.
3. Businesses with reinvestment opportunity and/or capital discipline.
4. A fair price.

What makes it distinctive is the *chassis*. Insurance premiums are collected before claims are paid, generating float — capital Markel holds and invests but does not own. Provided underwriting is at least break-even, float is free leverage that cannot be redeemed in a panic. Gayner runs an equity portfolio inside an insurance balance sheet, with a much higher equity allocation than the industry norm, and Markel Ventures buys whole non-insurance businesses (Costa Farms, CapTech, Weldship, PartnerMD and roughly fifteen others) to be held permanently. The structure is explicitly modelled on Berkshire Hathaway, and the "Markel Style" document — a one-page statement of values written in the 1980s — functions as the firm's constitution.

**Track record (claimed).** Markel reports book value per share and, more recently, a five-year compound growth in a composite measure; the equity portfolio's long-run returns are reported in the annual letter. `needs-verification` on specific figures — I did not fetch a Markel annual report.

**Transferable lesson.** If you want to invest with a genuinely long horizon, the highest-leverage decision is not which securities to buy but what kind of capital to invest. Insurance float, an endowment, a closed-end trust and a locked-up partnership are four solutions to the same problem.

## 14. Walter Schloss (1916–2012) — no degree, ninety-two clients, 45 years

**Education.** **None beyond secondary school.** He started as a runner on Wall Street in 1934 at eighteen, and took Benjamin Graham's investment courses at the New York Stock Exchange Institute while employed at Loeb, Rhoades & Co. Later worked directly for Graham at the Graham-Newman Partnership.

**Career sequence.** Founded Walter J. Schloss Associates in 1955 with 19 investors and roughly $100,000 (`needs-verification` on the initial figure). Later Walter & Edwin Schloss Associates, with his son. Closed the fund in 2000 and stopped managing outside money in 2003. He managed money for 92 investors over the fund's life. Named by Buffett as one of the nine in *The Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville* (1984).

**The approach.** Pure, unmodified Graham. Schloss bought statistically cheap securities — low price-to-book, out-of-favour, often loss-making — in a diversified portfolio of 60 to 100 names, held for around four years on average, and sold when they reverted toward book value. He did not visit companies. He did not talk to management (he said it biased him). He worked from published financial statements and the Value Line survey, in a single room without a computer, without an assistant for most of his career, and famously without a Bloomberg terminal. The 16 rules he published ("Make Money in the Stock Market") are the most compressed statement of the Graham method by a practitioner.

**The fee structure — the underrated part.** Schloss charged *no management fee at all*. He took 25% of profits, and only above a high-water mark. This meant that in a bad year he earned nothing, and that he had no incentive whatsoever to grow assets for their own sake. He kept the fund small deliberately. Buffett's later observation was that Schloss's structure meant he only made money when his partners did — the fee design and the investment philosophy were the same decision.

**Track record (claimed).** Buffett's 1984 article and subsequent reporting give the WJS Partnership 21.3% compound annually from 1956 to 1984 gross, 16.1% to limited partners, versus roughly 8.4% for the S&P over the same period; over roughly four and a half decades the compound return is reported as 15.3% versus about 10% for the index. The claimant for the earliest figures is Buffett; the source data is Schloss's own partnership accounting.

**Transferable lesson.** The clearest counter-example in this domain to any credential thesis. No degree, no CFA, no MBA, no computer, no company visits, no leverage, no shorting, and 45 years of results. What he had was a method he understood completely, a fee structure that removed the pressure to grow, and the temperament to apply the method unchanged through five decades of fashion.

## 15. Joel Greenblatt (b. 13 December 1957) — special situations, then teaching

**Education.** Wharton, University of Pennsylvania: BS 1979 (summa cum laude) and MBA 1980. One year at Stanford Law School before dropping out.

**Career sequence.** Founded Gotham Capital in 1985 with $7m, most of it from Michael Milken. Returned outside capital in 1994. Adjunct professor at Columbia Business School for over twenty years — the direct institutional continuation of the Graham–Dodd lineage. Co-founded the Value Investors Club with John Petry, an invitation-only forum capped at 250 members that pays $5,000 bi-monthly for the best idea posted. Wrote *You Can Be a Stock Market Genius* (1997), *The Little Book That Beats the Market* (2005), *The Big Secret for the Small Investor* (2011) and *Common Sense* (2020).

**The approach.** *Stock Market Genius* is the best public description of the special-situations opportunity set: spin-offs, restructurings, rights offerings, merger securities, stub stocks, LEAPs and bankruptcies. The unifying idea is that these situations are created by forced or indifferent sellers — index funds that must sell a spun-off subsidiary because it does not fit the mandate, institutions that cannot hold sub-investment-grade paper — and that price is therefore temporarily disconnected from value for structural rather than informational reasons. The later "magic formula" is a deliberately simplified two-variable screen (high earnings yield, high return on capital) designed to make the quality-plus-cheapness idea mechanical enough for a non-professional to follow.

**Track record (claimed).** Gotham Capital is reported at roughly 50% annualised gross / around 30% net of all fees from 1985 to 1994. That is an extraordinary number over a short period, on a small capital base, in a strategy with tiny capacity, during a strong bull market — every one of which qualifies it.

**Transferable lesson.** Look for opportunities created by *structure* rather than by *insight*: places where somebody must sell for a reason unrelated to value. And note that Greenblatt then handed the method away in a $12 paperback and spent two decades teaching it, which is not the behaviour of someone who believed the edge was fragile.
