# Finance careers — domain overview and how to read these profiles

**Summary.** This domain maps what a career in finance actually consists of, and profiles a set of people who have been unusually successful in it without becoming household names. It was assembled to answer a specific question: *what did the quietly excellent actually study, and how did they build their careers?* The answer is less tidy than the question implies. The single most important thing to hold in mind while reading is that this is a domain in which the evidence is systematically biased toward the winners, in which skill and luck are extraordinarily hard to separate, and in which the base rate for the activity these people excelled at is failure. Every profile here should be read as a case study in *what was done*, not as evidence that doing it works.

## The four caveats that govern everything here

### 1. Survivorship bias is not a footnote

The selection process that produced this list is: *be successful enough for long enough that someone wrote it down.* Managers who ran the same process and were unlucky, or who were right too early and lost their capital base, do not appear. Nick Sleep's Nomad and Chuck Akre's funds are documented; the several hundred concentrated value partnerships launched in the same years that closed quietly are not.

Buffett's 1984 Columbia address, *The Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville* (delivered 17 May 1984, published in Hermes, Fall 1984), is the canonical rebuttal: he conceded the coin-flipping argument and then argued that the winners were disproportionately drawn from one intellectual "village," which chance alone would not produce. That argument has real force — it is a clustering argument, not a survivorship argument — but note what it does *not* establish: it does not show that a randomly chosen adherent of that method will beat the market, and Buffett chose his nine after the fact.

### 2. Attribution of returns is genuinely hard

When a fund compounds at 20% for two decades, the honest decomposition is unavailable. Some part is market beta, some is a factor exposure (value, quality, small-cap, momentum) that was not identified as a factor at the time and was therefore free, some is leverage, some is fee structure, some is timing of capital flows, and some — possibly a large part, possibly none — is skill. Reported figures also vary in basis: gross versus net of fees, time-weighted versus money-weighted, the manager's own composite versus an audited fund NAV. Renaissance's Medallion is usually quoted as *66% average gross / 39% average net annual return, 1988–2018* — the gap between those two numbers is the fee structure, and it is enormous.

### 3. Most track records in this domain are self-reported

Only some of the figures here come from regulated fund documents or audited accounts. Others come from letters the manager wrote, from a journalist's reconstruction, or from a Wikipedia summary of a journalist's reconstruction. Wherever this file quotes a return, it names the claimant and the period. Where the source is a firm's own marketing, treat it as a claim, not a measurement.

### 4. Structural opportunity is often mistaken for skill

Several of the most impressive records were earned in conditions that no longer exist: convertible bond mispricings that a single mathematician could arbitrage by hand in 1969 (Thorp); an illiquid, under-researched South African market in the 1970s (Gray); an endowment world in which almost nobody would touch private equity or timber in 1985 (Swensen); statistical patterns in futures data that nobody else was looking at in 1988 (Simons). File `09` treats this question directly. It is the most important question in the domain and the one most consistently dodged.

## How to read the profiles

Each profile in `02` and `03` is structured to separate four different kinds of claim, because they have different evidential status:

1. **Education and career sequence** — usually verifiable from institutional sources. High confidence.
2. **Stated approach** — what the person says they do. Verifiable as a statement; not verifiable as a description of what actually drove returns.
3. **Track record** — a claim, attributed to a claimant, over a stated period. Medium confidence at best.
4. **Transferable lesson** — my inference. Lowest confidence. Read sceptically.

Where a fact could not be verified from a source I actually fetched, it is marked `needs-verification` inline rather than smoothed over. Several such marks appear; that is the point.

## Who is deliberately excluded

Buffett, Munger, Dalio, Soros, Icahn, Griffin and Ackman are referenced where they are structurally necessary (Buffett's letters are a primary source; Citadel is a structural feature of the market-making and pod-shop landscape) but are not profiled. The brief was explicitly about the tier below celebrity, and the celebrity tier is already over-documented. Ken Griffin appears in `01` because Citadel and Citadel Securities are load-bearing facts about how the industry works, not because he is a quiet operator.

## The honest framing of the user's underlying question

If the question behind this domain is "what should I study to become good at this," the profiles do not deliver a clean answer, and file `04` says so at length. The distribution of degrees among successful investors is close to the distribution of degrees among ambitious, numerate people who ended up in finance — with two real exceptions (an extreme over-representation of hard mathematics and physics in quantitative finance, and a striking *absence* of undergraduate finance degrees among concentrated value managers). What the profiles do deliver is a much more consistent pattern in *behaviour*: extreme reading volume, unusual tolerance for looking wrong for years, structural arrangements (capital lock-ups, fee designs, ownership structures) deliberately engineered to permit that tolerance, and a willingness to operate outside the professional consensus for long enough that the consensus becomes irrelevant.

That behavioural pattern is more replicable than the returns are.

## A vocabulary check before you start

Finance argot is used loosely in public and precisely inside the industry. These distinctions matter for reading everything that follows.

- **Buy-side vs sell-side.** The sell-side (investment banks, brokers, market makers) manufactures, distributes and prices securities and earns fees and spreads. The buy-side (asset managers, hedge funds, pensions, endowments, insurers) owns risk and earns returns on capital. Almost every person profiled in `02` is buy-side; almost every entry-level job described in `01` is sell-side. The standard career move is sell-side → buy-side, and it is much harder in the other direction.
- **Alpha vs beta.** Beta is the return you get for bearing a risk anyone can bear cheaply (equity market exposure, credit exposure). Alpha is return in excess of the appropriate risk-adjusted benchmark. The entire epistemic problem of this domain is that alpha is defined relative to a model, and the model is chosen after the fact. Value and quality were alpha in 1985 and are purchasable beta in 2026 for a few basis points.
- **Gross vs net.** Gross returns are before fees; net are what an investor actually received. Medallion's 66% gross / 39% net (1988–2018) is the extreme case, but a 2-and-20 structure on a 15% gross return leaves roughly 10% net, and the compounding difference over thirty years is roughly a factor of five.
- **AUM vs capital at work.** Assets under management is often quoted including leverage, committed-but-undrawn capital (in private equity), or advisory assets the manager does not discretionarily control. Renaissance's April 2021 figure was quoted both as $165bn "including leverage" and $130bn AUM — the same firm, two weeks apart, two numbers.
- **Track record period selection.** A record quoted from an inception date the manager chose, ending on a date the manager chose, is not a measurement. Watch for records that begin at a market bottom.
- **Permanent capital.** Capital that cannot be withdrawn: insurance float (Markel, Fairfax, Berkshire), a closed-end vehicle (Lindsell Train Investment Trust), an endowment (Yale), or a partnership with long lock-ups and voluntary investor selection (Nomad). Several of the best records in this domain are, in part, records of having engineered better capital rather than better analysis. This is an underrated and highly transferable insight.

## The shape of a finance career, in one page

There are four broadly distinct career architectures, and they have different risk profiles, not merely different pay:

1. **The institutional escalator.** Target university → summer internship → analyst programme at a bank, consultancy or Big Four → associate → buy-side or corporate move → MBA (optional and decreasingly necessary) → VP/director/PM. Predictable, credential-gated, front-loaded in hours, and highly path-dependent on the first two years. The risk is not failure but plateau.
2. **The technical specialist.** PhD or strong master's in a quantitative discipline → quant research or trading at a systematic fund or market maker → senior researcher or PM. Pay is high and early, hiring is meritocratic in a narrow technical sense, and the risk is that the specialisation is deep and non-portable outside the industry.
3. **The apprenticeship-then-own-shop.** Work for a good investor, learn the craft, leave with a track record and a few backers, run your own vehicle. This produced most of the people in `02`. Its base rate of success is unknown and probably poor, and the failures are invisible.
4. **The autodidact.** No relevant credential; a public written track record; capital raised on the strength of the writing. Michael Burry (a physician who posted on Silicon Investor from 1996 and founded Scion in 2000) is the documented case. It is rarer than internet folklore suggests, but the route exists and is more open in 2026 than in 2000 because publishing is free.

Nothing in this domain justifies a claim that one of these routes dominates. They select for different things and fail in different ways.

## What "success" is being measured

Three quite different things are conflated when people describe someone as successful in finance:

- **Investment success** — compounding capital at a rate that beats an appropriate benchmark, net of fees, over a long period, with the returns attributable to repeatable judgement. Rare and hard to prove.
- **Business success** — building a firm that gathers assets and earns fees. Much more common, much easier to achieve, and only loosely correlated with the first. A manager charging 1% on $30bn earns $300m a year whether or not clients do well.
- **Personal wealth** — which can arise from either of the above, from equity in a firm sold at the right moment, or from a single concentrated bet.

Several people in these profiles have all three. Several have only the first. At least one route to great personal wealth in finance — building and selling a distribution business — involves no investment skill at all. Be precise about which one you are trying to acquire.
