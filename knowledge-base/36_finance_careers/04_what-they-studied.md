# What they actually studied — a cross-cutting analysis of educational backgrounds

**Summary.** This file tabulates the verified education of everyone profiled or referenced in this domain and then tests, rather than confirms, the hypothesis that a particular kind of education produces good investors. The short answer: **there is no single educational path, and the apparent patterns are weaker than they look**. Two real signals survive scrutiny — an overwhelming concentration of hard mathematics, physics, electrical engineering and computer science in systematic/quantitative finance, and a striking scarcity of *undergraduate finance degrees* among discretionary investors — but the most common single field among discretionary managers is economics, not the liberal arts, and the most important variable is almost certainly not the degree at all.

> ⚠️ **Selection warning.** This is a table of winners. Without the corresponding distribution of degrees among people who entered the industry and *failed*, none of these frequencies license a causal claim. See `00_overview.md`.

## Testing the hypotheses

### Hypothesis 1: "Quant finance is dominated by mathematics and physics." — **Supported, strongly.**

This is the one place where the evidence is unambiguous. Every verified quantitative figure here holds a doctorate in mathematics, physics, electrical engineering or computer science. Renaissance's institutional policy makes it explicit: the firm employs roughly 150 researchers and programmers, about half with PhDs, and *disfavours* Wall Street experience. Simons's key 1993 hires, Mercer and Brown, came from IBM's speech-recognition group; Brown's doctoral supervisor was Geoffrey Hinton. The mechanism is not mystique: extracting weak, non-stationary signal from noisy sequential data is the same technical problem in speech, in coding theory and in short-horizon price prediction, and people who have already solved it once transfer directly.

The one qualification: this is a claim about *systematic* investing. It says nothing about discretionary investing, where the technical bar is far lower and the field distribution is completely different.

### Hypothesis 2: "Concentrated value investors come from the liberal arts." — **Partly supported, and over-stated.**

The liberal-arts cases are real and striking: Akre's English literature degree, Terry Smith's first in history, Armitage's modern history at Cambridge, Bill Miller's abandoned philosophy PhD, A.W. Jones's sociology doctorate. Train's reported modern history at Oxford would add to it. There is a plausible mechanism — the job of a discretionary investor is to construct and stress-test a narrative from incomplete evidence, which is what a good humanities education trains — and the profiled investors themselves often say so.

But the count does not support "dominated by." Economics is the single largest verified cluster (7), and accounting/commerce (4) is close behind the humanities (5). The honest statement is: **a humanities degree is no impediment, and appears at a higher rate than a naive observer would expect, but it is not the modal background.**

There is also an availability problem: the liberal-arts cases are *memorable* precisely because they are counter-stereotypical, so they get repeated in interviews and profiles far more than the economics graduates do. This file's own construction is subject to that bias.

### Hypothesis 3: "Almost none of them studied finance." — **Supported for undergraduate finance specifically; misleading if stated loosely.**

Not one of the profiled discretionary managers holds an undergraduate degree labelled "finance," with the single exception of **Howard Marks**, whose Wharton BS in economics carried a finance major. That is a genuine and interesting absence.

But it dissolves partly on inspection. Economics is adjacent, and seven of them studied it. Accounting — four of them, including three southern Africans — is arguably *more* directly relevant to security analysis than finance is, since the raw material of investing is financial statements. And Greenblatt, Spier and Eveillard studied business or PPE.

The more defensible version of the claim is: **the specific technical apparatus of an undergraduate finance curriculum — CAPM, efficient markets, portfolio theory, corporate finance formulae — is conspicuously absent from the intellectual formation of the best discretionary investors, and several of them are actively hostile to it.** Terry Smith made his name attacking accounting practice; Taleb's entire career is an attack on the assumptions of the standard curriculum; Buffett and Munger have been explicit about the uselessness of beta as a risk measure. What these people needed was accounting, business history, probability and psychology — which is a different reading list from the one an undergraduate finance degree provides.

### Hypothesis 4: "The Columbia / Graham–Dodd lineage matters." — **Supported as an institutional transmission mechanism, not as a credential.**

The line is unusually traceable. Benjamin Graham and David Dodd taught at Columbia from the late 1920s; *Security Analysis* appeared in 1934 and *The Intelligent Investor* in 1949. Buffett studied under Graham there. Walter Schloss took Graham's courses at the **New York Stock Exchange Institute** (not at Columbia, and without any degree) and then worked at Graham-Newman. Buffett returned to Columbia on **17 May 1984** to deliver *The Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville* for the 50th anniversary of *Security Analysis*. **Joel Greenblatt** has taught value investing to Columbia MBAs as an adjunct for over twenty years. **Seth Klarman** edited the 6th edition of *Security Analysis* (2008). Bruce Greenwald's Heilbrunn Center for Graham and Dodd Investing institutionalised it.

But note what the lineage actually transmits: a *method and a social network*, not a qualification. Schloss received the method with no degree at all. The teaching was largely done through books, letters and apprenticeship. The Columbia MBA is the visible artefact; the transmission would have happened without it.

### Hypothesis 5: "The Chicago / MIT / LSE academic finance lineage matters." — **Supported for the industry, not for these people.**

Chicago (Markowitz, Miller, Fama, French, Scholes, Roll) and MIT (Samuelson, Modigliani, Merton) produced modern portfolio theory, the efficient-market hypothesis, CAPM, factor models and option pricing. That work built the index-fund industry, the factor-investing industry, the derivatives market and essentially all of quantitative risk management. It is intellectually the dominant tradition in finance.

Yet almost none of the discretionary investors in this table came through it, and several built careers on the proposition that it is wrong. Two people bridge the gap: **David Swensen**, whose Yale economics PhD under James Tobin gave him a rigorous theoretical basis for the endowment model's diversification logic, and **Howard Marks**, whose Chicago MBA (1969) exposed him to efficient-markets thinking at its source — he has written that it made him a better investor by telling him where *not* to look for inefficiency.

That is the honest reconciliation: academic finance is extremely useful as a map of where markets are already efficient. It is not useful as a manual for exploiting the places where they are not.

### Hypothesis 6: "There is an accounting pipeline in southern Africa." — **Supported.**

Allan Gray studied accounting at Rhodes. Jannie Mouton qualified CA(SA) in 1973 after articles at Coopers Brothers. Peter Cundill (Canadian, but the same pattern) qualified CA in 1963 and CFA in 1968. Chris Hohn's degree was accounting and business economics. Coronation's CIO and much of the South African investment establishment are CA(SA)s. The CA(SA)/CA(NAM) qualification is the dominant credential in southern African finance in a way it simply is not in the US. This is a structural fact about the region, not a global one, and it matters directly for anyone planning a career from Namibia. **[NA]** **[ZA]**

## The value of the CFA — an honest assessment

**What it is.** A postgraduate professional certification from the CFA Institute, in three examination levels: Level I (tools, foundations, financial reporting), Level II (asset valuation applications), Level III (portfolio management, with specialised pathways in Portfolio Management, Private Markets and Private Wealth introduced in 2025). It requires 4,000 hours of qualified work experience over at least three years, and successful candidates take an **average of four years** to complete. Pass rates between 2014 and 2024 ranged from **22% to 59%**, with pandemic-era lows of 25% (May 2021 Level I), 22% (July 2021 Level I) and 29% (August 2021 Level II). There were at least **200,000 charterholders globally as at December 2024**, growing about 5.5% annually since 2012.

**What it actually does.**
- **It is a signal, and it is most valuable where other signals are weakest.** For a candidate from a non-target university, a career changer, or someone in a small market with no recognised pipeline, it is one of the few credible, internationally legible demonstrations of both competence and persistence. **[NA]** This makes it *more* valuable from Windhoek than from Princeton.
- **The Level I and II content is genuinely useful** — financial reporting and analysis, fixed income, derivatives, quantitative methods. Level III's portfolio-construction and behavioural material is the weakest part relative to what practitioners actually do.
- **It is close to mandatory in institutional long-only asset management** and in many research and wealth roles, and close to irrelevant in investment banking, private equity, market making and quantitative research, where nobody will ask.
- **It does not make you a good investor.** Not one person in the table above attributes their record to it; Cundill held it (1968) and Leinberger is reported to (`NV`), and that is the extent of its presence in this file.

The literature the Wikipedia article cites finds a positive human-capital effect, but note the obvious endogeneity: people who complete a four-year, 22%-pass-rate examination sequence while working full time were probably going to do well anyway.

## The value of the MBA — an honest assessment

**Where it appears in this file:** Allan Gray (Harvard 1965), Jeremy Grantham (Harvard 1966), Howard Marks (Chicago 1969), Terry Smith (Henley 1979), Seth Klarman (Harvard 1982, Baker Scholar), Nassim Taleb (Wharton 1983), Joel Greenblatt (Wharton 1980), Chris Hohn (Harvard 1993, Baker Scholar), Guy Spier (Harvard 1993), Li Lu (Columbia 1996), Prem Watsa (Ivey), Thomas Russo (Stanford, `NV`).

That is a substantial proportion — roughly half of the discretionary managers. But look at what it did for each of them:

- **Career switching.** Gray was an accountant from South Africa; the Harvard MBA got him into Fidelity in Boston. Watsa was a chemical engineer from IIT Madras; the Ivey MBA got him into Canadian finance. Hohn was an accountant at Coopers & Lybrand; HBS got him to Apax and then Perry Capital. Spier was a strategy consultant. **In every case the MBA functioned as a passport across a border — country, industry, or class — not as technical training.**
- **Network.** Spier and Pabrai's joint $650,100 bid for the 2007/2008 Buffett charity lunch is the visible tip of a network effect that runs through this whole cohort.
- **Content.** Almost nobody credits the curriculum. Marks is the exception, and what he credits is the efficient-markets *theory*, which told him where not to fish.

**The honest conclusion:** the MBA is a high-cost, high-effectiveness mechanism for changing tracks and acquiring a network. It is a poor mechanism for learning to invest. Its value has also declined at the margin for the pure institutional escalator, because banks and PE firms increasingly promote from within rather than requiring it — while its value for *switchers* is undiminished.

## The bifurcation: credentialed institutional path versus autodidact path

The evidence in this file supports a real and widening split.

**The credential-heavy institutional path** — target university, summer internship, analyst programme, possibly MBA, CFA for the long-only track — is more formalised, earlier-recruiting and more competitive than it has ever been. It is the only route into the large multi-strategy platforms and the elite market makers, and the gate is now effectively closed by the end of a candidate's second undergraduate year. Its advantage is capital access and training; its cost is that it selects hard for conformity and produces the career-risk distortions described in `08` and `09`.

**The autodidact path** exists and is documented. Walter Schloss had no degree and ran a partnership for 45 years. Michael Burry was a neurology resident who wrote about value investing on the Silicon Investor message board from 1996, attracted the attention of professional firms including Vanguard, closed his website in November 2000 and founded Scion Capital — a fund raised on the strength of public writing, by a physician. Robert Vinall runs a global concentrated fund from a Swiss village on the strength of an annual letter and an annual gathering.

What has changed since 2000 is the cost of the autodidact path's essential input: a public, dated, falsifiable written record. It is now zero. What has not changed is everything else — capital raising, regulatory registration, custody, operational infrastructure, and the several years of unpaid work required before anyone will take you seriously.

**The two paths are not equally available and they do not have equal base rates.** The institutional path has a known, mediocre-but-real success distribution. The autodidact path has an unknown and probably very poor one, whose visible examples are the extreme survivors. Anyone choosing it should do so with that asymmetry stated out loud.

## What the table actually predicts

If you strip out the noise, the fields represented here have three things in common, and the degree label is not one of them:

1. **Enough quantitative training to read a financial statement and reason about probability.** That is a low bar — roughly first-year university mathematics plus accounting — and every single person here clears it, including the English literature graduate and the man with no degree.
2. **Training in constructing and destroying arguments from incomplete evidence.** History, philosophy, law, medicine and physics all do this. Vocational finance mostly does not.
3. **A demonstrated capacity to work very hard at something difficult for years without external validation** — which a PhD, a CA articles contract, a medical residency, the CFA sequence, and a 45-year partnership run out of one room all evidence equally well.

The user's implicit hypothesis — that studying the right thing is what produced these careers — is not supported. What the table supports is a weaker and more useful claim: **almost any rigorous field is sufficient, provided it is genuinely rigorous, and the differentiating variables lie elsewhere** — in reading volume, in writing practice, in the structure of the capital eventually managed, and in temperament. Those are the subjects of `05`, `06` and `09`.

## Open questions

- The denominator is missing. A proper test would compare the field distribution of successful managers against the field distribution of *all entrants* to the industry in the same cohorts. No such dataset was obtained, and without it the frequency table above is descriptive only.
- 12 of the ~40 rows are `needs-verification`, disproportionately among the less-famous managers — exactly the population the domain is about. Firm websites for Gardner Russo & Quinn, Giverny, RV Capital, Markel and Denker either lack biographies or are robots-disallowed.
- CFA cost figures were not obtained from the CFA Institute directly.
- No source was fetched for the claim that MBA requirements have relaxed in banking and private equity; that is stated from general knowledge.
