# The canonical reading — annotated

**Summary.** What the people in `02` and `03` actually read, recommend and cite, organised by function rather than by fame, with an honest note on which books repay reading and which are cited more than they are read. Cost and legality are marked: **[FREE]** for legitimately free material with a link, **[PAID]** for books, and **[GREY]** where the material circulates in ways that are not clearly authorised.

> ⚠️ **On reading lists.** Reading is necessary and hugely over-credited. Every person in this domain reads enormously, but so do thousands of people who never earn a return above the index. Reading is the price of entry, not the source of edge. The material below is also heavily survivor-selected: it is the canon of the value and quantitative traditions *that worked*, and it under-represents everything that has been quietly discredited.

## 1. Start here — the four books, in order

If someone reads only four, these are them, and the order matters.

1. **Benjamin Graham, *The Intelligent Investor* (1949, and the 4th revised edition of 1973 with Buffett's preface).** **[PAID]** Not a technique book. It is a book about temperament, and the two chapters that matter — Chapter 8 (Mr Market) and Chapter 20 (margin of safety) — are the philosophical foundation of everything in `02`. Read the Jason Zweig commentary edition; the original 1949 examples are period pieces.
2. **Howard Marks, *The Most Important Thing* (2011).** **[PAID]** The single best modern statement of second-level thinking, cycles, and risk-as-permanent-loss. Marks assembled it from his memos, which are free (below), so read the memos first and buy the book only if you want it organised.
3. **Peter Lynch, *One Up on Wall Street* (1989).** **[PAID]** Frequently patronised and consistently under-rated: it is the clearest exposition of how to categorise a business (slow grower, stalwart, fast grower, cyclical, turnaround, asset play) before valuing it, and category selection is where most beginners' errors happen.
4. **Ed Thorp, *A Man for All Markets* (2017).** **[PAID]** The counterweight to the whole value tradition — an entirely different epistemology, in which edge is computed rather than judged, and sizing matters as much as selection. Reading 1 and 4 together is more instructive than reading either twice.

## 2. The value canon

### Foundational
- **Graham & Dodd, *Security Analysis* (1934; 6th edn 2008, edited by Seth Klarman).** **[PAID]** More a reference than a read. The 6th edition's value is the modern commentary essays by Klarman, Greenblatt, Greenwald, Marks and others, each introducing a section. The original text's fixed-income analysis remains excellent and is almost never read.
- **Philip Fisher, *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits* (1958).** **[PAID]** The other half of Buffett's stated formation ("85% Graham, 15% Fisher"). The "scuttlebutt" method — build a picture of a company by talking to its customers, suppliers, competitors and ex-employees — is the origin of everything now sold as "primary research" and "expert networks." The fifteen points remain a usable checklist.
- **Lawrence Cunningham (ed.), *The Essays of Warren Buffett* (1997, multiple editions).** **[PAID]** Buffett's shareholder letters, thematically rearranged. Worth it *only* if you will not read the letters chronologically. If you will, skip it.

### Practitioner
- **Joel Greenblatt, *You Can Be a Stock Market Genius* (1997).** **[PAID]** Badly titled, seriously good. The definitive public treatment of spin-offs, restructurings, rights offerings, merger securities, stub stocks and LEAPs — opportunities created by forced or indifferent sellers rather than by superior analysis. Uniquely actionable for a small investor because the opportunity set is capacity-constrained and therefore ignored by institutions.
- **Seth Klarman, *Margin of Safety* (1991).** **[GREY]** Out of print by the author's choice; physical copies trade at $700–$4,000; PDFs circulate without authorisation. On the merits it is excellent — the best written statement of absolute-return thinking and of risk as the permanent impairment of capital — but the cult around it is disproportionate, and its core arguments appear in Klarman's 6th-edition *Security Analysis* essays and his Baupost letters. Do not overpay, financially or in effort.
- **Howard Marks, *Mastering the Market Cycle* (2018).** **[PAID]** Weaker than *The Most Important Thing* but the best available treatment of how credit cycles actually behave, from a man who ran distressed debt from 1988.
- **Mohnish Pabrai, *The Dhandho Investor* (2007).** **[PAID]** "Heads I win, tails I don't lose much," illustrated through the Patel motel business. Short, unoriginal, and a good introduction to asymmetric payoff thinking for someone new. Not deep.
- **Bruce Greenwald et al., *Value Investing: From Graham to Buffett and Beyond* (2001, 2nd edn 2020).** **[PAID]** The most rigorous academic treatment of value investing as a discipline: asset value, earnings power value, and the value of growth, in that order. Greenwald's earnings-power-value framework is the single most useful valuation tool in this file for someone who distrusts DCF.
- **Christopher Browne, *The Little Book of Value Investing* (2007).** **[PAID]** Tweedy Browne's distillation. Read alongside the firm's *What Has Worked in Investing* (1992), which surveyed the empirical evidence for low price-to-book, low P/E, insider buying, sharp price declines and small capitalisation.
- **Edward Chancellor (ed.), *Capital Returns: Investing Through the Capital Cycle* (2015).** **[PAID]** Marathon Asset Management's essays — the intellectual water in which Nick Sleep and Qais Zakaria swam. The capital cycle framework (capital floods into high-return industries, destroys the returns, then withdraws, restoring them) is the most reliably useful mental model in `09`.
- **Robert Hagstrom, *The Warren Buffett Way* (1994).** **[PAID]** Serviceable; largely superseded by reading the primary letters.

## 3. The quantitative canon

### Entry
- **Ed Thorp, *Beat the Dealer* (1962, rev. 1966) and *Beat the Market* (1967, with Sheen Kassouf).** **[PAID]** Historically indispensable. *Beat the Market* describes delta-hedged convertible arbitrage in 1967, six years before Black–Scholes was published. Read for the method of thought, not the specific trades.
- **John Kelly, *A New Interpretation of Information Rate* (Bell System Technical Journal, 1956).** **[FREE]** The original Kelly criterion paper. Twelve pages. Thorp took it from information theory into gambling and then into markets. Essential and short.
- **Ernest Chan, *Quantitative Trading* and *Algorithmic Trading*.** **[PAID]** The most accessible practical entry to backtesting and mean-reversion strategies. Thin on statistics, useful on plumbing.

### Core technical
- **John Hull, *Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives*.** **[PAID]** The standard textbook, everywhere, for good reason. If you learn one derivatives book, this is it.
- **Steven Shreve, *Stochastic Calculus for Finance I & II*.** **[PAID]** The rigorous route: binomial models in Volume I, continuous-time in Volume II. Volume II requires real measure-theoretic probability.
- **Richard Grinold & Ronald Kahn, *Active Portfolio Management* (2nd edn 1999).** **[PAID]** The source of the Fundamental Law of Active Management — that information ratio ≈ information coefficient × √breadth. It is the single most important structural insight about why Renaissance's high-breadth, low-accuracy model works and why a 20-stock concentrated portfolio needs a very high information coefficient to compete.
- **Paul Wilmott, *Paul Wilmott on Quantitative Finance* / *Introduces Quantitative Finance*.** **[PAID]** Idiosyncratic, opinionated, strong on where the models break.
- **Marcos López de Prado, *Advances in Financial Machine Learning* (2018).** **[PAID]** The most useful and the most quoted-without-being-read book on this list. Its genuine contributions are the practical traps: the deflated Sharpe ratio and multiple-testing correction, purged and embargoed cross-validation for serially correlated financial data, meta-labelling, fractional differentiation, and the triple-barrier labelling method. Its unifying argument — that most published quantitative finance research is false because of backtest overfitting — is correct and should be internalised before any of the techniques are applied.

### The sceptical wing
- **Nassim Taleb, *Dynamic Hedging* (1997).** **[PAID]** The technical book, on options market making in the real world where models fail. Far better than the Incerto for anyone who actually trades.
- **Taleb, the Incerto: *Fooled by Randomness* (2001), *The Black Swan* (2007), *The Bed of Procrustes* (2010), *Antifragile* (2012), *Skin in the Game* (2018).** **[PAID]** *Fooled by Randomness* is the one that matters for this domain — it is the best popular statement of the survivorship problem that governs everything in `00`. The later volumes are increasingly polemical.
- **Benoit Mandelbrot & Richard Hudson, *The (Mis)Behavior of Markets* (2004).** **[PAID]** Fat tails, long memory, and why the normal distribution is the wrong prior. Short and clear.

## 4. Accounting and valuation

This is the section that most reading lists shortchange and that matters most for actual work. See `06` for the techniques themselves.

- **Stephen Penman, *Financial Statement Analysis and Security Valuation*.** **[PAID]** The best single book on the articulation between accounting and value — residual income, the relationship between book value and price, and why accounting conservatism creates predictable patterns. Demanding.
- **Aswath Damodaran, *Investment Valuation*, *The Dark Side of Valuation*, *Narrative and Numbers*.** **[PAID]** — but Damodaran publishes **[FREE]** his complete lecture videos, slides, spreadsheets, data sets (industry-level betas, cost of capital, margins, updated annually) and blog at [pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/). This is the single most valuable free resource in finance education anywhere. *Narrative and Numbers* is the most original: it argues that a valuation is a story disciplined by arithmetic, and that most analysts do one or the other.
- **McKinsey (Koller, Goedhart, Wessels), *Valuation: Measuring and Managing the Value of Companies*.** **[PAID]** The corporate-finance standard. Its enduring contribution is the ROIC-and-growth value driver framework: value creation requires ROIC above the cost of capital, and growth destroys value when it does not.
- **Howard Schilit, *Financial Shenanigans* (4th edn 2018).** **[PAID]** A taxonomy of earnings manipulation, organised by device, with real case studies. The best forensic-accounting book for a practitioner.
- **Thornton O'glove, *Quality of Earnings* (1987).** **[PAID]** Older, thinner, still excellent on the difference between reported earnings and the cash a business actually produces.
- **Terry Smith, *Accounting for Growth* (1992).** **[PAID]** Historically important and still instructive on the *mentality* of accounting scepticism, though many of the specific UK GAAP devices it dissects have been closed. Notable as the book its author was sued over.
- **Martin Fridson & Fernando Alvarez, *Financial Statement Analysis: A Practitioner's Guide*.** **[PAID]** Practical, credit-flavoured, well-written.

## 5. Market history

The most under-read category, and the one that best inoculates against believing that the present is unprecedented.

- **Charles Kindleberger & Robert Aliber, *Manias, Panics, and Crashes*.** **[PAID]** The structural anatomy of a bubble — displacement, credit expansion, euphoria, distress, revulsion — applied across four centuries. The framework survives every new instance.
- **Edward Chancellor, *Devil Take the Hindmost* (1999).** **[PAID]** The best-written financial history in English. Written *before* the dot-com peak, which is part of its authority.
- **John Kenneth Galbraith, *The Great Crash 1929* (1955) and *A Short History of Financial Euphoria* (1990).** **[PAID]** The second is 100 pages and should be read every few years.
- **Roger Lowenstein, *When Genius Failed* (2000).** **[PAID]** LTCM. The definitive case study in leverage, model risk, correlation regime change and the reflexivity of forced liquidation. Two Nobel laureates on the board did not help.
- **Michael Lewis, *Liar's Poker* (1989) and *The Big Short* (2010).** **[PAID]** *Liar's Poker* is the best account of sell-side culture ever written and was intended as a warning; it functioned as a recruitment brochure, which is itself the lesson. *The Big Short* is the popular account of Michael Burry's trade.
- **Sebastian Mallaby, *More Money Than God* (2010).** **[PAID]** The best single history of the hedge fund industry, from A.W. Jones's 1949 partnership onward. Necessary background for `01`.
- **Gregory Zuckerman, *The Man Who Solved the Market* (2019).** **[PAID]** The only substantial account of Renaissance Technologies. Journalistic rather than technical, but it is where the Simons narrative in `02` largely originates — treat its numbers as reported rather than verified.
- **Andrew Ross Sorkin, *Too Big to Fail* (2009).** **[PAID]** Blow-by-blow on 2008. Long, and better as a reference than a read.
- **Ron Chernow, *The House of Morgan* (1990).** **[PAID]** The long institutional history of American finance.
- **Peter Bernstein, *Against the Gods* (1996) and *Capital Ideas* (1992).** **[PAID]** The history of risk, and the intellectual history of modern portfolio theory. *Capital Ideas* is the most readable account of the Chicago/MIT lineage discussed in `04`.
- **Marc Levinson, *The Box* (2006).** **[PAID]** Not a finance book. The best case study in existence of how a boring technology restructures an entire industry's returns — which is the capital cycle in a single narrative.

## 6. Psychology and decision-making

- **Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011).** **[PAID]** The canonical text. Note honestly that several of the priming and social-psychology studies it cites have not replicated; the core work on heuristics, prospect theory and loss aversion has held up much better.
- **Kahneman, Sibony & Sunstein, *Noise* (2021).** **[PAID]** More directly useful for an investment process than *Thinking, Fast and Slow*: the argument that inconsistency in judgement (noise) costs more than bias, and that structured protocols and independent assessments before discussion reduce it. This is the theoretical case for the written thesis and the pre-mortem in `06`.
- **Richard Thaler, *Misbehaving* (2015).** **[PAID]** The history of behavioural economics from the inside, funnier and more useful than *Nudge*.
- **Robert Cialdini, *Influence*.** **[PAID]** Cited constantly by this cohort — Munger built a whole lecture on it, and the Nomad letters draw on it. Read it as a description of how managements and sell-side analysts will attempt to move you.
- **Charlie Munger, *Poor Charlie's Almanack* (2005; expanded editions since; a Stripe Press edition appeared in 2023).** **[PAID]** The "Psychology of Human Misjudgment" speech — 25 tendencies — is the centrepiece and is the most-cited single document in this cohort's mental furniture. The rest is repetitive. Munger's USC Law School (2007) and USC Business School (1994, "A Lesson on Elementary Worldly Wisdom") commencement speeches circulate **[FREE]** and cover most of it.
- **Morgan Housel, *The Psychology of Money* (2020).** **[PAID]** Aimed at individuals, not professionals, and excellent at what it does: the argument that behaviour dominates knowledge in personal outcomes, and that the ability to survive is worth more than the ability to optimise.
- **Philip Tetlock & Dan Gardner, *Superforecasting* (2015).** **[PAID]** The empirical case for base rates, calibration, granular probability estimates, frequent small updates, and the practice of scoring your own forecasts. The most directly transferable methodology on this entire list.
- **Annie Duke, *Thinking in Bets* (2018).** **[PAID]** "Resulting" — judging a decision by its outcome — named and attacked. Short, and directly relevant to evaluating your own investment record honestly.
- **Michael Mauboussin, *More Than You Know*, *The Success Equation*, *Expectations Investing* (with Alfred Rappaport).** **[PAID]**, but Mauboussin's research pieces (at Morgan Stanley, previously Credit Suisse and BlueMountain) are **[FREE]** and are among the best available on base rates, the luck-skill continuum, ROIC, and the reverse-DCF discipline. *Expectations Investing* is the book-length treatment of reverse DCF and is under-read relative to its usefulness.

## 7. Primary sources — the letters and memos

**This is the most valuable section on the page.** Secondary books about investors are always worse than the investors' own contemporaneous writing, because letters are dated, were written before the outcome was known, and cannot be retrofitted.

- **Warren Buffett, Berkshire Hathaway shareholder letters, 1977–present.** **[FREE]** at [berkshirehathaway.com/letters/letters.html](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/letters.html). Read chronologically; the evolution from cigar-butt Graham to quality-franchise Fisher/Munger happens visibly between roughly 1972 and 1989. The **Buffett Partnership letters (1957–1970)** are a separate and in some ways more instructive body of work — they describe running a small partnership with a real fee structure and explicit performance standards — and circulate **[GREY]** in compiled PDF form.
- **Howard Marks, Oaktree memos, 1990–present.** **[FREE]** at [oaktreecapital.com/insights/memos](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/insights/memos). The single best free education in cycle awareness, risk and second-level thinking available anywhere. "You Can't Predict. You Can Prepare." (2001), "The Most Important Thing" (2003), "Us and Them" (2004), "The Limits to Negativism" (2008) and "Sea Change" (2022) are the usual entry points.
- **Nomad Investment Partnership letters, 2001–2013 (Nick Sleep and Qais Zakaria).** **[GREY]** — compiled PDFs circulate widely; I could not locate an authorised host, and the partnership no longer exists. Substantively they are the best-written investment letters of the century so far, and the source of "scale economics shared" and "destination analysis." Treat the performance figures within them as the managers' self-report (see `02`).
- **Seth Klarman, Baupost Group letters.** **[GREY]** Not published; excerpts circulate. Worth reading if encountered legitimately; not worth hunting.
- **David Einhorn, Greenlight Capital letters.** **[GREY]** Quarterly letters circulate; Einhorn's *Fooling Some of the People All of the Time* (2008) **[PAID]** is the book-length account of the Allied Capital short and is the best available description of what it actually costs to be publicly right about a fraud.
- **Christopher Bloomstran, Semper Augustus annual letters.** **[FREE]** at semperaugustus.com. Extraordinarily detailed — recent letters run to 100+ pages — and the best public demonstration of forensic analysis of a single complex company (Berkshire) sustained over many years.
- **François Rochon, Giverny Capital annual letters.** **[FREE]** at givernycapital.com. Notable for the annual self-audit of mistakes and for separating business results from stock results.
- **Robert Vinall, RV Capital annual letters and blog.** **[FREE]** at rvcapital.ch.
- **Terry Smith, Fundsmith annual letters and shareholder-meeting transcripts.** **[FREE]** at fundsmith.co.uk. Notable for publishing the portfolio's look-through financial characteristics against the index each year.
- **Chuck Akre / Akre Capital commentary; Markel annual reports (Tom Gayner's investment section).** **[FREE]** on the respective corporate sites.
- **Corporate primary sources.** **[FREE]** via [SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/edgar) — 10-K, 10-Q, 8-K, DEF 14A proxy statements (for compensation and incentive analysis), 13F (quarterly US institutional holdings, with a 45-day lag), 13D/G (activist and >5% stakes), and Form ADV (adviser registration, AUM, fee arrangements and disciplinary history). **[NA]/[ZA]** Regional equivalents: SENS announcements via the JSE, NSX announcements, and CIPC/BIPA company registries.

## 8. What is over-cited relative to its usefulness

Stated plainly, because reading lists rarely do:

- ***Margin of Safety*** — the scarcity is doing most of the work. Its arguments are available elsewhere from the same author.
- ***The Black Swan*** — one genuinely important idea, delivered at book length, three times.
- ***Reminiscences of a Stock Operator*** (Lefèvre, 1923) — beautifully written, endlessly quoted, and almost entirely about short-term speculation, which is the opposite of what the people quoting it do.
- ***Rich Dad Poor Dad*** and the personal-finance-guru genre — no relationship to this domain.
- **Most "how the market works" books published after 2015** — largely recycled from the sources above.

## What to read if you have three months

Week 1–2: Marks's memos (start with ten of them) and Buffett's letters from 1977 to 1990.
Week 3–6: *The Intelligent Investor* chapters 8 and 20; Greenwald's *Value Investing*; Penman or Fridson for accounting.
Week 7–8: *Devil Take the Hindmost* and *When Genius Failed*.
Week 9–10: Damodaran's free valuation lectures, doing the spreadsheets.
Week 11: *Fooled by Randomness* and *Superforecasting*.
Week 12: Write a full investment thesis on one company, using the structure in `06`, and a pre-mortem against it.

The last week is worth more than the other eleven.
