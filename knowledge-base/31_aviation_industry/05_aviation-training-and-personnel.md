# Aviation training and personnel

**Summary.** Every licensed role in aviation derives ultimately from ICAO **Annex 1 (Personnel Licensing)**, but the routes to each licence, their cost, and the philosophy behind them differ enormously between the EASA and FAA systems and again in the Gulf and Asia. The pilot pathway is the most expensive self-funded professional qualification in the world outside medicine, ranging from roughly €80,000–150,000 for a European integrated ATPL course to a fully sponsored cadetship with a bond. This file sets out each route, its regulatory basis, its realistic cost and duration, and how recurrent training has shifted from prescriptive manoeuvre repetition to evidence-based, competency-assessed training.

> ⚠️ **Every cost and duration figure in this file is indicative, sourced from general industry knowledge rather than from a cited price list, and moves quickly with fuel prices, exchange rates and demand. Treat them as orders of magnitude and verify against a specific ATO before acting.** They are marked `[indicative]` throughout.

## 1. The ICAO licence structure (Annex 1)

Annex 1 defines licence categories, the knowledge, experience, skill and medical requirements for each, and the framework for validation and conversion. The flight crew licences:

- **Student Pilot** (not a licence in all States)
- **Private Pilot Licence (PPL)** — non-commercial, no remuneration
- **Commercial Pilot Licence (CPL)** — may be paid; single-pilot commercial operations and co-pilot in multi-pilot operations
- **Multi-Crew Pilot Licence (MPL)** — co-pilot in multi-pilot operations only
- **Airline Transport Pilot Licence (ATPL)** — pilot-in-command in multi-pilot commercial air transport
- **Instrument Rating (IR)**, class and type ratings, instructor and examiner ratings
- **Flight Navigator**, **Flight Engineer** (both effectively obsolete on modern types)

Non-flight-crew licences and certificates in Annex 1: **Aircraft Maintenance Licence**, **Air Traffic Controller Licence**, **Flight Operations Officer / Flight Dispatcher** (a licence in some States, a certificate in others), **Aeronautical Station Operator**.

**Medical classes**: Class 1 (professional pilots), Class 2 (private), Class 3 (air traffic controllers). Class 1 renewal is annual to age 40 for single-pilot commercial operations and six-monthly above 60 in multi-pilot operations; the exact intervals are national and worth checking. **Age 65** is the ICAO upper limit for pilot-in-command in international commercial air transport with a co-pilot under 60; several States are lobbying to raise it and the ICAO position should be re-checked.

**PANS-TRG (Doc 9868)** carries the competency-based training and assessment (CBTA) framework and the MPL syllabus; **Doc 9995** is the Evidence-Based Training manual.

## 2. Pilot training pathways

### PPL → CPL/IR/ME — the modular route

Common in Europe, the UK, South Africa, Australia and much of the developing world. The student buys each qualification separately, usually while working.

| Stage | Typical minimum | Duration | Indicative cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PPL(A)** | 45 h flight time (EASA); 40 h (FAA) — in practice 55–70 h | 3–12 months part-time | €10,000–18,000 / US$12,000–20,000 `[indicative]` |
| **Hour building** | to 150–200 h total | 3–12 months | €90–200 per hour on a C152/PA-28 `[indicative]` |
| **ATPL theory (frozen ATPL)** | 650 h distance learning + classroom, 13 exams | 6–18 months | €3,000–8,000 `[indicative]` |
| **Night rating** | 5 h | days | €1,500–2,500 `[indicative]` |
| **Multi-engine piston (MEP) class rating** | 6 h | 1–2 weeks | €4,000–8,000 `[indicative]` |
| **Instrument Rating (multi-engine)** | 50 h instrument instruction (some in FNPT II) | 2–3 months | €12,000–25,000 `[indicative]` |
| **CPL** | 200 h total time, 25 h CPL training | 1–2 months | €8,000–15,000 `[indicative]` |
| **MCC / APS-MCC** | 20–25 h (MCC) or 40 h (APS MCC) in an FSTD | 2–4 weeks | €4,000–12,000 `[indicative]` |
| **Total** | ~200–250 h | 18 months–4 years | **€45,000–90,000** `[indicative]` |

The output is a CPL/ME/IR with "frozen ATPL" theory credit — the ATPL is unfrozen and issued when the 1,500-hour experience requirement is met.

### The integrated route (Part-ATO ab-initio)

A single continuous course at an Approved Training Organisation from zero hours to CPL/ME/IR with ATPL theory and APS MCC, typically 14–20 months full time, ~200 hours flying. Cost in Europe is commonly quoted at **€80,000–130,000**, and up to **£130,000–150,000** for the best-known UK/European ATOs including accommodation `[indicative]`. Providers: CAE, L3Harris Airline Academy, FTEJerez, BAA Training, Skyborne, Lufthansa Aviation Training, Egnatia, Bartolini, Flight Training Europe.

Advantages: airline-recognised, structured, faster, and the ATO's airline relationships give access to selection events. Disadvantages: the cost is front-loaded, usually debt-financed, and the market can turn between enrolment and graduation — which happened brutally in 2001, 2008 and 2020.

### The Multi-Crew Pilot Licence (MPL)

Created by ICAO in **2006** and added to Annex 1 in **November 2006**. It abandons the assumption that a multi-crew airline pilot should first be trained as a single-pilot aviator. The MPL is competency-based, airline-specific, and delivered as a single continuous course by an ATO in partnership with a named operator.

Four phases:

1. **Core flying skills** — basic handling, mostly in light aircraft.
2. **Basic** — introduction of multi-crew operation and instrument flight, largely in FNPT II MCC / FTD.
3. **Intermediate** — multi-crew operations in a multi-engine turbine environment, in an FSTD representing a type of the same class as the target aircraft.
4. **Advanced** — the type rating itself, in a Level D full flight simulator, with line-oriented flight training.

Requirements: **at least 240 hours total flight time** (aircraft plus qualified FSTD), **750 hours** of theoretical knowledge instruction, and **40 hours and 12 take-offs and landings in the actual aircraft** before carrying passengers. The licence restricts the holder to **co-pilot in multi-crew operations**; converting to a CPL requires additional single-pilot training.

Uptake: 4,018 graduates worldwide as of March 2021, Lufthansa alone accounting for 1,358; also used by easyJet, several Asian and Gulf carriers, and in Australia. **The United States and Canada do not accept it**, and ALPA opposes it. That non-acceptance is the licence's practical problem: an MPL holder's career is tied to operators and States that recognise it.

Duration is typically **16–18 months** from zero experience to the right-hand seat. Cost is usually funded by the airline against a bond, or by the cadet at a level similar to an integrated course plus type rating `[indicative]`.

### The FAA route

Structurally different, cheaper at the front and slower at the back:

- **PPL** (40 h minimum, realistically 60–75 h), **Instrument Rating** (40 h simulated/actual instrument), **Commercial** (250 h total), **Multi-engine**, then **CFI/CFII/MEI** instructor ratings.
- The pilot then **instructs** (or flies traffic watch, banner tow, pipeline patrol, skydive) to accumulate hours toward the **1,500-hour ATP** requirement imposed by the Airline Safety and FAA Extension Act of 2010 after Colgan 3407.
- **R-ATP** (restricted) reductions: 1,000 h with a bachelor's degree from an approved Part 141 aviation programme, 1,250 h with an associate degree, 750 h for military pilots.
- **ATP-CTP** (Certification Training Program) — a mandatory 30-hour academic plus 10-hour FSTD course before the ATP written.

Cost: roughly **US$80,000–120,000** to CFI `[indicative]`, but the instructing phase is paid work, so the net cost is lower than the European integrated route, at the price of two to three additional years.

Philosophical difference worth stating plainly: the FAA route produces a pilot with substantially more single-pilot decision-making and instructional experience and much less structured multi-crew exposure; the EASA integrated/MPL route produces the reverse. Both systems produce competent airline pilots. The evidence that either is safer is weak.

### Airline cadet programmes

The dominant route into Gulf and Asian carriers, and increasingly into European ones. Structures:

- **Fully sponsored** — the airline pays; the cadet signs a bond (typically 5–7 years, with a declining repayment liability). Common at Emirates (National Cadet Programme, for UAE nationals), **Qatar Airways** (the QR cadet programme, primarily for Qatari nationals), Singapore Airlines, Cathay Pacific, Turkish, Ethiopian.
- **Self-funded with a guaranteed assessment or job offer** — the cadet pays an ATO, the airline commits to an interview or a conditional offer. easyJet/CAE, Wizz, Ryanair (with FTEJerez and others), British Airways' Speedbird programme (partially funded).
- **Type rating "pay-to-fly"** — the pilot funds their own type rating and sometimes line training hours. Widely criticised as a safety and equity issue; EASA and national authorities have taken an increasingly dim view of self-funded line training in particular.

Selection typically includes: aptitude testing (COMPASS, PILAPT, ADAPT, or an in-house battery), maths and physics, group exercises, technical and HR interview, simulator assessment, and a Class 1 medical.

### Type rating

A **type rating** is required for every aircraft above 5,700 kg MTOM or turbojet-powered. Structure (EASA Part-FCL, Appendix 9 skill test standards; FAA equivalent under Part 61/121 with an ATP-type):

1. **Ground school / technical course** — 2–4 weeks, systems, limitations, performance, computer-based training plus classroom, ending in a technical examination.
2. **FTD / fixed-base simulator** — flows, procedures, normal and non-normal drills.
3. **Full flight simulator** — typically 8–12 four-hour sessions covering normal operations, engine failures, system failures, low-visibility operations, upset recovery, and non-precision and precision approaches, ending in the **skill test** (LST) with an examiner (TRE).
4. **Base training / ZFTT** — if the FFS is Level C or D and the pilot meets the experience criteria, **Zero Flight Time Training** allows the first landings to be made on a line flight under supervision with a training captain; otherwise 4–6 circuits are flown in the actual aircraft.
5. **Line training** — 20–100+ sectors under a Line Training Captain, ending in a **line check**.

Cost of a self-funded type rating: **€20,000–45,000** depending on type and provider, plus line training if charged `[indicative]`. For an airline it is a similar internal cost plus the productivity loss.

### Recurrent training and the six-monthly cycle

The classical EASA structure:

- **Operator Proficiency Check (OPC)** — a check of the operator's procedures, twice per 12 months, in an FSTD.
- **Licence Proficiency Check (LPC)** — the type rating revalidation, once per 12 months, usually combined with one of the OPCs.
- **Line Check** — once per 12 months on a normal line flight.
- **Recurrent ground and refresher training** — annual, covering aircraft systems, performance, dangerous goods, security, first aid, emergency and safety equipment, CRM.
- **CRM training** — annual recurrent, plus a modular programme over a three-year cycle.
- **Low visibility, ETOPS/EDTO, PBN, RVSM, steep approach** — specific approval currencies.
- **Recency** — three take-offs and landings in the preceding 90 days on type (or class).

The result is a simulator visit roughly every six months. The traditional content was largely fixed by regulation: a repeating cycle of engine failure after V1, rejected take-off, engine-out approach and go-around, non-precision approach, and a small set of system failures.

### LOFT, EBT and CBTA

**LOFT (Line Oriented Flight Training)** is a full-length, real-time, uninterrupted simulated line flight in which the crew manages a realistic scenario with no instructor intervention, followed by a facilitated debrief usually with video. LOFT was the first serious attempt to train crew *performance* rather than pilot *manoeuvres*, and is the ancestor of everything below. **LOE (Line Operational Evaluation)** is the assessed variant used in the FAA's AQP.

**AQP (Advanced Qualification Program)** — the FAA's voluntary alternative to the prescriptive Part 121 Appendices E and F, allowing an operator to build a data-driven, competency-based curriculum. Most large US carriers operate under AQP.

**EBT (Evidence-Based Training)** — developed through the IATA Training and Qualification Initiative (ITQI) and published as **ICAO Doc 9995**, adopted into EASA rules through the ORO.FC and Part-FCL amendments. The core idea: stop training against a list of historical accident scenarios and start training and assessing the **competencies** that determine whether a crew can handle whatever they meet. The evidence base is accident and incident data, FDM data, LOSA observations and training data, analysed by aircraft generation.

The **ICAO/IATA competency framework** (as used in EBT and CBTA) comprises:

1. Application of procedures (and compliance with regulations)
2. Communication
3. Aircraft flight path management — automation
4. Aircraft flight path management — manual control
5. Leadership and teamwork
6. Problem solving and decision making
7. Situation awareness (and management of information)
8. Workload management

EASA's competency set adds **Application of Knowledge** as a ninth, and ICAO has separately articulated **Knowledge** as an underpinning element.

An EBT module has **three phases**:

- **Evaluation phase** — an unassisted LOFT-style scenario used to establish the individual crew's competency profile. Formative, not pass/fail in the traditional sense, though a serious deficiency triggers additional training.
- **Manoeuvres training phase** — repetitive practice of specific handling items to proficiency, with the instructor free to repeat until the standard is met.
- **Scenario-based training phase** — realistic line scenarios exercising the competencies, again with facilitation rather than intervention.

**Mixed EBT** retains the regulatory proficiency check alongside EBT modules; **baseline EBT** replaces the check entirely with continuous competency assessment across the cycle. Baseline EBT requires an approved programme, a mature data system and instructor standardisation, and is the direction of travel.

**CBTA (Competency-Based Training and Assessment)** is the general PANS-TRG framework; EBT is CBTA applied to recurrent training of experienced crew. The same framework now underpins the MPL, cabin crew training, and the instructor and examiner qualifications.

### UPRT — Upset Prevention and Recovery Training

Loss of control in flight (LOC-I) has been the largest killer in commercial aviation for two decades. The response, mandated in EASA rules from 2018–2019 and in the FAA's Part 121 rules from 2019:

- **Advanced UPRT** as a prerequisite to a first multi-pilot type rating: at least 3 hours of ground instruction and 3 hours of dual flight instruction in an aerobatic-capable aeroplane at an approved ATO.
- **On-type UPRT** within the type rating and in recurrent training, in an FSTD.
- Simulator upgrades: FSTDs used for UPRT must have a validated aerodynamic model beyond the normal flight envelope (including stall to full stall and beyond, and high angle of attack), **instructor feedback** on when the simulator is being flown outside its validated envelope, and often an enhanced motion/buffet cueing. **CS-FSTD(A) Issue 2** was specifically the "Update of flight simulation training device requirements — Upset prevention and recovery training".

The doctrinal shift matters: **prevention** (recognising the developing upset — energy state, attitude, automation mode) is emphasised over **recovery**, because most LOC-I accidents are avoidable long before they become recoveries.

### Line training, command upgrade and the instructor path

- **Line training** — the newly type-rated pilot flies with a **Line Training Captain (TRI/LTC)** for a defined number of sectors, with a supervised **Line Check** to release to normal line flying. Length varies from ~20 sectors on short-haul to 8–15 long-haul sectors, extended for low-experience cadets. Under EASA the operator's low-experience co-pilot rules restrict pairing (an inexperienced co-pilot may not fly with an inexperienced captain, and airfield/weather restrictions apply).
- **Command upgrade** — an internal process, typically requiring a minimum total time and time on type (commonly 3,000–5,000 h total and 1,500–2,500 h on type at a network carrier, far less at an LCC in a growth phase `[indicative]`), a command assessment, a command course (leadership, decision-making, commercial and legal responsibilities, one-engine-inoperative and abnormal handling from the left seat), a simulator command check, and command line training with a line check.
- **Instructor and examiner path**:
  - **TRI(A)** — Type Rating Instructor: instructs for the type rating, in FSTD and aircraft.
  - **SFI** — Synthetic Flight Instructor: instructs in the FSTD only; does not require a current type rating on the aircraft.
  - **TRE(A)** — Type Rating Examiner: conducts skill tests, proficiency checks and assessments of competence for TRIs.
  - **SFE** — Synthetic Flight Examiner.
  - **CRMI / CRMIE** — CRM instructor and instructor-examiner.
  - **EBT instructor** — requires specific facilitation and grading standardisation training; instructor standardisation is the acknowledged weak point of EBT implementation.
  - **FIE, CRE, IRE, FI, IRI, CRI, MCCI** for the light and instrument end.

## 3. Cabin crew

Regulatory basis: **ICAO Annex 6 Part I Chapter 12**; in Europe **Part-CC** (Annex V to Regulation (EU) No 1178/2011) creating the **Cabin Crew Attestation (CCA)**, plus **Part-ORO Subpart CC** for operator conversion and recurrent training. In the US, cabin crew hold an FAA **Certificate of Demonstrated Proficiency**.

Initial training (typically 4–8 weeks) covers: aviation general knowledge and regulations; communication; human factors and CRM; passenger handling and unruly passengers; safety and emergency procedures; emergency equipment; fire and smoke (including live fire training in a mock-up); survival (land and water, with wet drill); aeromedical and first aid (including CPR/AED, decompression); dangerous goods; security; and the operator's SEP for each type.

**Recurrent** is annual, with a **three-year cycle** on some elements (e.g. practical fire and smoke, and slide/wet drills at defined intervals). Cabin crew must be trained on each type operated, with differences training, and are subject to the same FTL scheme as flight crew under ORO.FTL, with different rest requirements.

Cost: employer-funded almost universally, though some carriers charge for uniforms or initial training with a bond `[indicative]`.

## 4. Licensed aircraft maintenance engineers

### EASA Part-66

The **Aircraft Maintenance Licence (AML)** categories:

| Category | Scope |
|---|---|
| **A** (A1 turbine aeroplanes, A2 piston aeroplanes, A3 turbine helicopters, A4 piston helicopters) | Certifies minor scheduled line maintenance and simple defect rectification, within the limits of tasks specifically endorsed by the Part-145 organisation. Not a full certifying engineer. |
| **B1** (B1.1 turbine aeroplanes, B1.2 piston aeroplanes, B1.3 turbine helicopters, B1.4 piston helicopters) | Mechanical: structure, powerplant, mechanical and electrical systems. May certify work on avionic systems requiring only simple tests. |
| **B2** | Avionic and electrical systems. |
| **B2L** | Avionics limited to specified system ratings, for aircraft other than complex motor-powered aircraft. |
| **B3** | Piston-engine non-pressurised aeroplanes of 2,000 kg MTOM and below. |
| **L** | Sailplanes, balloons and airships (L1–L5). |
| **C** | Base maintenance: issues the certificate of release to service for the complete aircraft after a base maintenance check, on the basis of the B1/B2 certifications underneath. Requires either prior B1/B2 experience or an academic route. |

Route: **Part-147** approved basic training (a modular set of 17 modules covering mathematics, physics, electrical and electronic fundamentals, digital techniques, materials and hardware, maintenance practices, aerodynamics, human factors, aviation legislation, turbine/piston theory, propellers, and aircraft structures and systems) plus **experience** — typically 2 years with a Part-147 basic course, or 3 years with practical experience and self-study, or 5 years with no relevant technical training. Then **type training** (theoretical and practical) at a Part-147 organisation and an entry on the licence for that type, followed by **certifying staff authorisation** issued by the employing **Part-145** organisation.

Duration and cost: a Part-147 basic B1.1 course is typically **2–3 years** and **€15,000–40,000** if self-funded, though most engineers come through an employer apprenticeship at no personal cost `[indicative]`. Type courses run **3–8 weeks** and **€6,000–20,000** `[indicative]`.

### FAA A&P

The **Airframe and Powerplant** certificate under **14 CFR Part 65**: 18 months of practical experience for either rating, or 30 months for both, or graduation from a **Part 147 Aviation Maintenance Technician School** (traditionally 1,900 hours of curriculum, restructured under the 2022 Part 147 rewrite to a competency basis). Then written, oral and practical tests. The **Inspection Authorization (IA)** is an additional privilege for annual inspections and major repairs.

Structural difference from EASA: the FAA A&P is a *personal* certificate carrying broad privileges without type endorsement, with the type-specific competence controlled by the employer's training programme and Operations Specifications. EASA puts type endorsement on the licence itself. The FAA system is more flexible; the EASA system is more traceable.

Cost: US$15,000–40,000 for a Part 147 school `[indicative]`, or free through military service or an airline apprenticeship.

### Approvals the engineer works under

- **Part-145 / FAR Part 145** — the maintenance organisation approval. Scope-rated by aircraft type and by work class (base, line, components, engines, NDT, specialised services).
- **Part-CAMO** — the continuing airworthiness management organisation, which owns the Aircraft Maintenance Programme, airworthiness review, defect deferral policy and reliability programme. An airline typically holds a **combined AOC + CAMO + Part-145** structure, or contracts the 145 out.
- **Part-147** — basic and type training organisation.
- **Part-21 Subpart G/J** — production and design organisation approvals for manufacturers and modification houses.

## 5. Air traffic controllers

Regulatory basis: **ICAO Annex 1 Chapter 4**; in Europe **Regulation (EU) 2015/340** (the ATCO Licensing Regulation, Part-ATCO); in the US the FAA's own hiring and academy system.

Structure of the qualification:

1. **Initial (ab-initio) training** — basic and rating training at an approved training organisation. In Europe this is typically 6–12 months and includes basic ATC theory (air law, ATM, meteorology, navigation, aircraft performance, human factors, equipment) and rating-specific simulation.
2. **Ratings** — Aerodrome Control Visual (ADV), Aerodrome Control Instrument (ADI), Approach Control Procedural (APP), Approach Control Surveillance (APS), Area Control Procedural (ACP), Area Control Surveillance (ACS). Rating endorsements add tower/ground movement control and surveillance, radar, and precision approach radar.
3. **Unit training** — transitional, pre-on-the-job, and **on-the-job training (OJT)** under an OJTI at the specific unit, ending in a unit endorsement. This is where most trainees fail; unit endorsement failure rates of 20–40% are commonly cited `[indicative]`, and a controller who cannot be endorsed at one unit may be re-streamed to another.
4. **Continuation training and competency checks** — annual, plus refresher and emergency/unusual situation training, plus a **unit competence scheme** with defined assessment intervals.
5. **Class 3 medical** and, in Europe, **English Language Proficiency Level 4** minimum (Level 6 is non-expiring).

Routes:

- **EUROCONTROL Institute of Air Navigation Services (IANS)**, Luxembourg — training and standardisation for member ANSPs, plus the ESSIP/Master Plan support role.
- **National academies** — DFS in Langen, ENAC in France, NATS in Whiteley, ENAV Academy, Entry Point North (Sweden/Denmark/Hungary), the Irish Aviation Authority's academy.
- **FAA Academy, Oklahoma City** — the US route: hiring bid, ATSAP/ATSA aptitude testing, medical and security, then the academy (basics plus a stream to terminal or en-route), then facility training which takes a further 2–4 years to certified professional controller.
- **CATC/Gulf and Asian academies** — Qatar's ANSP training, GCAA-approved organisations in the UAE, Singapore's ATC training centre.

Cost: almost always **employer-funded**; controllers are usually paid a trainee salary from day one. The trade is a long, high-attrition selection funnel. Duration from selection to certified professional controller: **3–5 years** at a busy en-route or major terminal facility `[indicative]`.

## 6. Flight dispatchers / flight operations officers

- **United States** — the **Aircraft Dispatcher Certificate** (14 CFR Part 65 Subpart C) is a licence with real legal weight: under Part 121 the dispatcher shares operational control with the pilot-in-command. Requirements: 21 years old, English proficiency, a knowledge test covering essentially the ATP syllabus, and a practical test. Training is typically a **5–6 week full-time course** (about 200 hours) costing **US$4,000–8,000** `[indicative]`.
- **ICAO/EASA** — Annex 1 provides for the Flight Operations Officer/Flight Dispatcher licence, but it is not mandatory in Europe. EASA requires that where the operator uses an operational control system with flight operations officers, they are trained and assessed to an approved standard (ORO.GEN.110 and associated AMC, plus the operator's own manual). ICAO **Doc 7192 Part D-3** is the training manual.
- **IATA/Jeppesen/Sheffield/other providers** offer commercial FOO courses, commonly **4–8 weeks** `[indicative]`.

Competence areas: meteorology, NOTAM and AIP interpretation, mass and balance, aircraft performance, flight planning and fuel, ETOPS/EDTO, ATC procedures, dangerous goods, emergency procedures, and the operator's specific approvals.

## 7. Ground handling staff

Not licensed in most States, which is a persistent safety-system weakness. The framework is:

- **IATA AHM** (Airport Handling Manual) and **IGOM** (IATA Ground Operations Manual) — the de facto standards.
- **ISAGO** — IATA's Safety Audit for Ground Operations, a registry-based audit of ground service providers at both corporate and station level.
- **EASA Ground Handling rulemaking** — a long-running task (RMT.0728) intended to bring ground handling into the EU regulatory framework with organisational requirements and training standards; status should be re-checked.
- **Dangerous goods** — mandatory training under Annex 18 / the ICAO Technical Instructions and the **IATA DGR**, with competency-based training and assessment (CBTA) replacing the old function-category matrix from 2023.
- **Airside driving permits**, **de-icing** (SAE AS6285/6286 and the Association of European Airlines/EASA de-icing training requirements), **loading and load control**, **pushback and towing**, **GSE operation**.

Training is employer-funded, short (days to weeks) and — given turnover — repeated constantly. The economics of ground handling contracts, which are won on price at renewal, are the direct upstream cause of most ground damage.

## 9. The supply picture

The much-quoted long-range forecasts — Boeing's Pilot and Technician Outlook and CAE's Aviation Talent Forecast — project demand in the hundreds of thousands of new pilots and technicians over 20 years. Treat them with caution: they are marketing documents produced by companies that sell training, and their historical accuracy is poor.

What is verifiable and matters more:

- Training capacity is constrained by **instructors and simulators**, not by demand for places.
- The 1,500-hour rule in the US created a structural bottleneck at the regional carriers that has driven regional pilot pay up several-fold since 2015 and forced mainline carriers to fund cadet pipelines.
- **Retirement** is a demographic wave: the mandatory age (65 in the US and most of the world) means the cohort hired in the 1980s expansion is leaving now.
- The **cost barrier** is the single largest constraint on the pipeline and the principal reason the profession's demographics remain narrow. Airline-funded cadetships, apprenticeship levies and state loan schemes are the only mechanisms that have measurably changed it.
- **Engineering** is worse off than flying: the licensed engineer pipeline is shrinking in Europe and North America while the fleet grows, and the pay differential against other skilled trades has narrowed.
