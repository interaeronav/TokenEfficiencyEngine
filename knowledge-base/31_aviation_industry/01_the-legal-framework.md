# The legal framework of international civil aviation

**Summary.** International aviation runs on a 1944 treaty that begins by asserting the exact opposite of free trade: every State has complete and exclusive sovereignty over the airspace above its territory. Everything else — traffic rights, technical standards, liability, consumer protection, security, financing security interests — is built on top of that assertion, either by multilateral treaty (safety standards, passenger liability, unlawful interference, financing) or by bilateral bargain (who may fly where, and how much). Understanding the industry commercially means understanding which parts of it are governed by treaty and which by negotiation, because that division explains almost every strategic conflict in the business, including the Gulf-carrier subsidy disputes and the fights over Open Skies.

## 1. The Chicago Convention 1944

Negotiated in the closing phase of the Second World War, when the United States had an overwhelming commercial aviation advantage and wanted multilateral traffic rights, and the United Kingdom and most of Europe did not. The compromise: agree everything technical multilaterally, and leave the commercial rights to bilateral bargaining. That compromise still defines the industry eighty years later.

**Article 1** — "The contracting States recognize that every State has complete and exclusive sovereignty over the airspace above its territory." This is not a novel rule invented in 1944; it codifies pre-war customary law. Its consequence is that no airline has any right to fly anywhere except where its State has negotiated one.

**Article 5** — non-scheduled flights may transit and make non-traffic stops without prior permission (in practice heavily qualified by States).

**Article 6** — scheduled international services require special permission from the State whose territory is entered. This is the article that creates the bilateral system.

**Article 12** — each State keeps its rules of the air consistent with the Convention; over the high seas, ICAO's rules (Annex 2) apply directly and without exception.

**Article 17–21** — nationality and registration. An aircraft has the nationality of the State in which it is registered; dual registration is prohibited. This is why leasing structures care so much about registry.

**Article 24–28** — customs duty exemption for fuel and stores on international flights, and the State's obligation to provide air navigation facilities.

**Article 33** — certificates of airworthiness and licences issued by the State of Registry must be recognised by other States, *provided* they meet or exceed ICAO minimum standards. This is the load-bearing article for the whole system: it is why a Qatari-registered A350 can land in France on a Qatari C of A.

**Article 37** — States undertake to collaborate in securing the highest practicable degree of uniformity, and ICAO adopts SARPs.

**Article 38** — where a State finds it impracticable to comply, it must **notify ICAO of the difference**. Differences are published in **Supplements to the Annexes** and, operationally, in each State's **AIP GEN 1.7**. A pilot who wants to know whether a State actually applies a given ICAO rule looks at GEN 1.7.

**Article 83 bis** — allows the State of Registry to transfer specified Article 12/30/31/32(a) functions to the State of the Operator by agreement. Critical for dry-leased aircraft on a foreign register.

### Standards versus Recommended Practices

- A **Standard** is a specification "the uniform application of which is recognized as necessary for the safety or regularity of international air navigation" and to which Contracting States **will conform**. Non-conformity triggers the Article 38 notification duty. Standards use the word *shall*.
- A **Recommended Practice** is one "recognized as desirable in the interest of safety, regularity or efficiency" and to which States **will endeavour to conform**. Uses *should*. Notification of differences from Recommended Practices is encouraged rather than obligatory.

Beneath the Annexes sit **PANS** (Procedures for Air Navigation Services — e.g. PANS-ATM Doc 4444, PANS-OPS Doc 8168, PANS-AIM Doc 10066, PANS-TRG Doc 9868), which are approved by Council but are not SARPs, and **SUPPs** (Regional Supplementary Procedures, Doc 7030), plus a large body of **guidance material** in Doc-series manuals. ICAO maintains over 12,000 SARPs across the 19 Annexes; the standard development cycle from proposal to applicability takes roughly two years.

ICAO audits implementation through **USOAP CMA** (Universal Safety Oversight Audit Programme, Continuous Monitoring Approach), producing an **Effective Implementation (EI)** score per State across eight critical elements. A poor EI score has commercial consequences: the FAA's **IASA** programme downgrades States to Category 2, which freezes their carriers' US operations, and the EU maintains an **Air Safety List** ("blacklist") banning specific operators or entire State registries from EU airspace.

## 2. Traffic rights: the freedoms of the air

The Chicago conference produced two side agreements:

- **International Air Services Transit Agreement (IASTA, the "Two Freedoms Agreement")** — widely ratified, grants first and second freedoms multilaterally.
- **International Air Transport Agreement (the "Five Freedoms Agreement")** — almost nobody ratified it; the third to fifth freedoms remained bilateral.

| Freedom | Right |
|---|---|
| 1st | Overfly a foreign country without landing |
| 2nd | Land in a foreign country for technical (non-traffic) purposes — fuel, maintenance |
| 3rd | Carry traffic from the home State to a foreign State |
| 4th | Carry traffic from a foreign State back to the home State |
| 5th | Carry traffic between two foreign States on a service originating or terminating in the home State |
| 6th | Carry traffic between two foreign States via the home State (in practice a 3rd + 4th combination) |
| 7th | Operate entirely outside the home State between two foreign States |
| 8th | Consecutive cabotage — carry domestic traffic within a foreign State on a service continuing from/to the home State |
| 9th | Stand-alone cabotage — purely domestic operation in a foreign State |

Only the first five are "official" in the sense of being defined in ICAO documents; sixth to ninth are industry terminology.

The **sixth freedom** is the entire commercial thesis of the Gulf carriers, Turkish Airlines, and to a degree Singapore Airlines and Copa: place a hub where the great-circle geography lets you intermediate traffic between two other regions, then buy third and fourth freedom rights at both ends. Legally this is unimpeachable — a Doha–London and a Doha–Bangkok service are separate third/fourth freedom operations that a passenger happens to connect between. Politically it is contested, because the incumbent carriers of the two end markets lose the O&D revenue. That is the substance of the US "Big Three vs Gulf Three" dispute of 2015–2018 and the European equivalents.

**Cabotage** is the hard boundary. The US does not grant it (49 U.S.C. and the foreign ownership limit of 25% voting equity); the EU internal market grants it among member states, which is precisely what makes it a *single* aviation market rather than a set of bilaterals.

### Bilateral air services agreements and open skies

A classic **ASA** specifies: designated carriers (single or multiple designation), routes (a route schedule annex, sometimes point-by-point), capacity (frequencies or seats per week), tariffs (historically requiring approval, often via IATA tariff conferences), and ownership and control (the "substantial ownership and effective control" clause that has kept airline nationality intact while every other industry globalised). Fifth freedom rights are usually the hardest-fought item.

An **Open Skies** agreement removes capacity, frequency and route restrictions, liberalises pricing, and usually grants fifth freedom rights and open cargo rights. The US has concluded well over 100. The **EU–US Air Transport Agreement** (signed 2007, Stage 2 in 2010) created a single transatlantic aviation area but stopped short of allowing cabotage or changing the US foreign ownership cap — the reason the deal is asymmetric and the reason full transatlantic consolidation happens through **antitrust-immunised joint ventures** rather than mergers.

The **EU single aviation market** goes far beyond open skies: Regulation (EC) 1008/2008 gives any Community air carrier the right to operate any route within the Community, including domestic routes in another member state, with no capacity, frequency or price restriction. Ownership rules apply at the Union level, not the member-state level. This is what made Ryanair, easyJet and Wizz possible, and its absence is why Africa's Yamoussoukro Decision (1999) and the Single African Air Transport Market (SAATM, launched 2018) have delivered so much less — the political commitment exists on paper, but the implementation and the underlying bilateral protectionism have not shifted.

The EU also has **external competence**: after the ECJ's 2002 "Open Skies" judgments, nationality clauses in member-state bilaterals were held incompatible with the freedom of establishment, so the Commission negotiates horizontal agreements replacing them with a "Community carrier" designation.

## 3. EASA and national CAAs

**EASA** was established by Regulation (EC) 1592/2002, began work in 2003, took over airworthiness and environmental certification on 28 September 2003, and was renamed the European Union Aviation Safety Agency in 2018. The current **Basic Regulation is (EU) 2018/1139**.

Competences:

- **Certification** — EASA is the certifying authority for type certificates, STCs, and design and production organisation approvals for the whole Union. This is exclusive; national authorities have no role.
- **Rulemaking** — EASA drafts Implementing Rules (adopted by the Commission through comitology), plus non-binding **AMC** (Acceptable Means of Compliance) and **GM** (Guidance Material). The AMC/GM structure matters: an operator may propose **Alternative Means of Compliance (AltMoC)** to the authority.
- **Standardisation** — EASA inspects national authorities to check they are applying EU rules consistently, and can escalate to the Commission.
- **Third Country Operators (TCO)** — a non-EU airline flying into the EU needs an EASA TCO authorisation on top of its own AOC. Qatar Airways, Emirates and every other foreign carrier hold one.
- **Occurrence reporting** — Regulation (EU) 376/2014, which also enshrines just culture protections.

The **Implementing Rule families** a professional pilot deals with: Part-FCL (licensing), Part-MED, Part-ARA/ORA (authority and organisation requirements), Part-ORO (organisation requirements for air operations, including ORO.FTL for flight time limitations), Part-CAT (commercial air transport operations), Part-SPA (specific approvals: LVO, EDTO/ETOPS, PBN, dangerous goods), Part-NCC/NCO/SPO, Part-M / Part-CAMO / Part-145 / Part-66 / Part-147 (continuing airworthiness), Part-21 (design and production), Part-ATO / Part-DTO (training organisations), CS-FSTD(A)/(H) for simulators.

**National CAAs** retain: AOC issue and oversight, licensing (through their own or delegated examiners), aerodrome certification (under EASA rules), and airspace. The UK left EASA on 31 December 2020 and the **UK CAA** now runs a "retained EU law" version diverging slowly. Non-EU national systems worth knowing: **FAA** (14 CFR, with a very different structure — Parts 61/91/117/119/121/135/145/147), **Qatar CAA** (QCAR series, largely EASA-derived), **UAE GCAA** (CAR series), **SACAA** in South Africa (SA-CATS/SA-CARs, ICAO-aligned), **Namibia's NCAA** (Namibian Civil Aviation Regulations, closely modelled on the South African set) **[NA]**.

## 4. Passenger liability: Warsaw to Montreal

### The Warsaw system

The **Warsaw Convention 1929** unified rules for international carriage by air. It set very low liability caps (originally 125,000 gold francs, roughly US$8,300 in later terms) in exchange for a presumption of carrier fault, on the reasoning that the young airline industry needed protection from ruinous claims. It was then patched repeatedly, producing an unmanageable patchwork:

- **Hague Protocol 1955** — doubled the limit.
- **Guadalajara Convention 1961** — extended the regime to the actual carrier where different from the contracting carrier (codeshare/wet-lease).
- **Montreal Agreement 1966** — a private inter-carrier agreement, not a treaty, imposed by US pressure: carriers serving the US accepted a US$75,000 limit and waived the fault defence.
- **Guatemala City Protocol 1971** and **Montreal Additional Protocols 1–4 (1975)** — variously in force or not; MAP4 modernised cargo documentation.
- **IATA Intercarrier Agreements 1995–96** — carriers voluntarily waived the limits for passenger death and injury.

The result was that the applicable regime depended on which instruments both the origin and destination States had ratified. **The Montreal Convention 1999 exists to end this.**

### Montreal Convention 1999 (MC99)

Adopted 28 May 1999, entered into force **4 November 2003**. As of 2026 IATA reports **141 of 191 ICAO Contracting States** are parties (~72%), which IATA describes as producing "a patchwork of liability regimes"; the ICAO 2025 Assembly again urged universal ratification.

Core architecture:

- **Two-tier liability for death and bodily injury (Article 21).** Tier one is **strict liability** up to a stated SDR figure — the carrier cannot escape by proving absence of fault, only by proving contributory negligence. Above that figure, tier two is **presumed fault with a reversed burden**: the carrier is liable without limit unless it proves the damage was not due to its negligence or was solely due to a third party.
- **Article 17** — liability for "an accident which took place on board the aircraft or in the course of any of the operations of embarking or disembarking". The definition of "accident" (*Air France v Saks*, and in the EU the CJEU's *Niki Luftfahrt* spilled-coffee case) is heavily litigated.
- **Article 19** — delay liability, capped, with a defence of all reasonable measures.
- **Article 22** — limits for baggage and cargo. Cargo liability is an unbreakable limit per kilogram unless a special declaration of value is made.
- **Article 33 — the fifth jurisdiction.** A claimant may sue in the State of the passenger's principal and permanent residence, provided the carrier operates there — a major US-driven addition, since it lets US residents sue at home.
- **Article 28** — advance payments to meet immediate economic needs where required by national law (the EU requires 16,000 SDR within 15 days).
- **Article 24** — the limits are **reviewed every five years** against inflation and revised by the depositary unless a majority of States object.
- **Article 50** — States must require carriers to maintain adequate insurance.
- **Article 3(4)/4** — electronic ticketing and air waybills recognised.

**Current limits.** The most recent five-yearly revision took effect on **28 December 2024**. The baggage limit at that revision is **1,519 SDR per passenger**. The 2019 revision figures were 128,821 SDR for death/injury tier one, 5,346 SDR for delay, 1,288 SDR for baggage and 22 SDR/kg for cargo; the 2024 revision raised each by roughly 18%. **[needs-verification]** — the exact 2024 death/injury, delay and cargo figures should be taken from ICAO's official "2024 Revised Limits of Liability under the 1999 Montreal Convention" notice before being relied on; the ICAO page returned 403 to automated retrieval at the time of writing.

In the EU, **Regulation (EC) 2027/97 as amended by 889/2002** incorporates MC99 into Union law and applies it to domestic as well as international carriage by Community carriers — removing the international/domestic distinction inside Europe.

## 5. Consumer protection: EU261 and the rest of the world

**Regulation (EC) 261/2004** is the most commercially significant consumer instrument in aviation, and the most disliked by airlines, because its remedies are fixed sums unrelated to the ticket price.

Compensation for cancellation, denied boarding, and (post-*Sturgeon*) long delay:

| Distance | Compensation |
|---|---|
| ≤ 1,500 km | €250 |
| 1,500–3,500 km (and all intra-EU over 1,500 km) | €400 |
| > 3,500 km | €600 |

Right to care thresholds (meals, refreshments, two communications, hotel and transfers if an overnight results): 2 h for short-haul, 3 h for medium, 4 h for long-haul. At 5 h the passenger may abandon the journey and take a refund of the unused portion plus, where relevant, a flight back to the first point of departure.

Cancellation notice defences: no compensation if cancelled ≥14 days ahead; or 7–14 days ahead with re-routing departing ≤2 h earlier and arriving <4 h later; or <7 days with re-routing departing ≤1 h earlier and arriving <2 h later.

**Extraordinary circumstances** (Article 5(3)) is the battleground. Courts have narrowed it aggressively: technical faults inherent in normal operation do not qualify (*Wallentin-Hermann*), nor do wildcat strikes by the carrier's own staff (*Krüsemann*); ATC strikes, severe weather, security risks, political instability and the 2010 volcanic ash cloud do. Even where the defence applies, **the duty of care is unlimited in time and money** (*McDonagh v Ryanair*).

**Scope:** any flight departing an EU/EEA airport regardless of carrier; and any flight arriving in the EU/EEA operated by a Union carrier. The CJEU has extended this to connecting itineraries booked as a single contract even where the delay occurs on a non-EU leg operated by a non-EU carrier, provided the first leg departed the EU.

**Reform.** The Commission adopted revised proposals in **June 2025**, with Parliament consideration from September 2025: short-haul compensation raised to €300 but only after 4 hours; medium-haul threshold extended; long-haul cut to €500 but only after 6 hours. Consumer bodies estimate this would remove eligibility from a large majority of currently-qualifying passengers. Status as of August 2026 should be re-checked — the file has been in trilogue-adjacent limbo for years.

**UK261** — the retained version, with compensation in pounds (£220/£350/£520 at the conversion adopted). It covers flights departing the UK on any carrier, flights arriving in the UK on a UK or EU carrier, and UK domestic flights. Some itineraries are covered by both regimes.

**United States** — no general compensation right. The DOT regime covers: denied boarding compensation (14 CFR Part 250, cash compensation scaled to delay in reaching destination, capped); tarmac delay rules (3 h domestic / 4 h international before deplaning must be offered); the full-fare advertising rule; and, from the 2024 rulemaking, automatic refunds for cancelled and significantly changed flights and for unprovided ancillary services. The DOT's proposed compensation-for-delay rule was withdrawn under the subsequent administration **[needs-verification for current status in 2026]**.

**Elsewhere** — Canada's **Air Passenger Protection Regulations** (2019, tiered compensation up to C$1,000, weakened by "safety" carve-outs and under review); Brazil's ANAC Resolution 400; India's DGCA CAR Section 3 Series M Part IV; Turkey's SHY-Passenger; the GCC has no equivalent, and Qatar, the UAE and Saudi Arabia rely on MC99 plus contract-of-carriage terms. This asymmetry is a real competitive factor: an EU carrier's cost of irregular operations is structurally higher than a Gulf carrier's on the same route.

## 6. Aircraft financing security: the Cape Town Convention

The **Convention on International Interests in Mobile Equipment** and its **Protocol on Matters Specific to Aircraft Equipment**, adopted at Cape Town on 16 November 2001, in force for aviation from **1 March 2006**, with **85 contracting parties** (84 States and the EU).

It covers airframes certified to carry at least 8 persons or 2,750 kg of cargo, engines with at least 1,750 lbf thrust (or 550 shp for turboprops), and helicopters seating 5 or more. Note that **engines are separate objects** — a departure from common-law accession doctrine and enormously important for engine lessors.

Mechanisms:

- **The International Registry**, operated from Ireland (Aviadocs/Aviareto), is an asset-based, notice-filing, electronic register. Priority is determined by order of registration, not by notice or by underlying contract date.
- **Default remedies** — repossession, sale, lease, or de-registration and export, exercisable without court order where the debtor has consented and the State has made the relevant declaration.
- **IDERA** — Irrevocable De-Registration and Export Request Authorisation, filed with the aviation authority, giving the creditor the ability to de-register the aircraft without the airline's cooperation.
- **Alternative A** insolvency remedy (Article XI of the Protocol) — the closest thing aviation has to the US Bankruptcy Code §1110: if the debtor does not cure defaults and perform obligations within a fixed waiting period (usually 60 days), the creditor gets possession. A State's declaration of Alternative A with a 60-day waiting period is what lenders look for.
- **The Cape Town Discount** — under the OECD **Aircraft Sector Understanding**, States that have ratified with the qualifying declarations (the "Cape Town list") get a reduction in the minimum export credit premium, typically cited at up to 10%. The commercial effect is that ratification directly lowers the cost of capital for that State's airlines.

The 2022 Russian lessor expropriations were the convention's hardest test and demonstrated its limit: it works where courts and registries cooperate, and not where a State chooses not to.

## 7. Unlawful interference: the security conventions

- **Tokyo Convention 1963** (Convention on Offences and Certain Other Acts Committed on Board Aircraft) — establishes jurisdiction of the State of Registry, and, importantly for line pilots, gives the **aircraft commander** powers to restrain a person he reasonably believes has committed or is about to commit an offence or an act jeopardising safety, and immunity for doing so. Amended by the **Montreal Protocol 2014**, which added jurisdiction for the State of the Operator and the State of Landing (closing the gap for foreign-registered aircraft), and clarified the position of in-flight security officers.
- **Hague Convention 1970** (Unlawful Seizure of Aircraft) — hijacking; extradite or prosecute.
- **Montreal Convention 1971** (Unlawful Acts Against the Safety of Civil Aviation) — sabotage, attacks on aircraft and air navigation facilities. Supplemented by the **1988 Airport Protocol** covering attacks at airports serving international civil aviation.
- **MEX Convention 1991** — marking of plastic explosives for detection.
- **Beijing Convention and Beijing Protocol 2010** — modernised the Hague/Montreal regime: using an aircraft as a weapon, releasing biological/chemical/nuclear material from an aircraft, cyber attacks on air navigation, and criminalising the transport of such materials. In force from 2018; ratification is still incomplete.

Operationally these sit alongside **Annex 17** and, in Europe, Regulation (EC) 300/2008 and its implementing acts, which impose the security regime everyone experiences at the checkpoint.

**Overflight of conflict zones** is the live problem. MH17 (2014) exposed the absence of any mechanism to close airspace that a sovereign State chooses to leave open. The responses were ICAO's Conflict Zone Information Repository (short-lived), EASA's **Conflict Zone Information Bulletins**, and national systems (the FAA's NOTAMs and Special Federal Aviation Regulations; the UK's Overseas Territories and DfT advice). The legal position remains: the State controls its airspace, the operator decides whether to accept the risk, and the insurance market prices it.

## 8. Accident investigation: Annex 13 and just culture

**Annex 13** governs the investigation of accidents and serious incidents. Its foundational rule is **Chapter 3.1**: "The sole objective of the investigation of an accident or incident shall be the prevention of accidents and incidents. It is not the purpose of this activity to apportion blame or liability."

Structure:

- **State of Occurrence** normally institutes the investigation; it may delegate to another State.
- **Accredited representatives** from the States of Registry, Operator, Design and Manufacture participate, each with **advisers** (typically the OEM, the operator, the engine maker, the pilot association).
- **Records protected** (Chapter 5.12) — CVR recordings and transcripts, ATC recordings, medical information, opinions expressed in the analysis — must not be made available for purposes other than the investigation unless a competent authority determines that disclosure outweighs the adverse domestic and international impact.
- **Preliminary report** within 30 days for accidents to aircraft over 2,250 kg; **Final Report** to be published as soon as possible and, if not within 12 months, an **interim statement** on each anniversary.
- **Safety recommendations** may be made at any time and must be addressed to the relevant authority; recipients must respond within 90 days.
- **ADREP** — the ICAO Accident/Incident Data Reporting system, into which States file, and which underpins the ECCAIRS taxonomy used in Europe.

Investigation authorities must be **independent of the regulator and the operator**: NTSB (US), AAIB (UK), BEA (France), BFU (Germany), ATSB (Australia), TSB (Canada), JTSB (Japan). In the EU, Regulation (EU) 996/2010 mandates independence and creates the European Network of Civil Aviation Safety Investigation Authorities.

**Just culture** is the doctrine that makes reporting possible: front-line personnel are not punished for actions, omissions or decisions commensurate with their experience and training, but gross negligence, wilful violations and destructive acts are not tolerated. It is codified in **Regulation (EU) 376/2014** (occurrence reporting), which requires member states to refrain from instituting proceedings in respect of unpremeditated or inadvertent infringements that come to their attention only through reporting, and requires operators to have a just culture policy agreed with staff representatives.

The tension between just culture and criminal law is unresolved and jurisdiction-dependent. Prosecutions after accidents in Italy, France, Greece, Spain, Brazil and Indonesia have chilled reporting; the Netherlands and the UK have generally protected it. For an airline pilot the practical implication is that the legal protection attaching to a mandatory occurrence report varies materially with where the event happened, and with whether the report is a company report, a mandatory occurrence report, or a statement to an investigator.

## 9. Aviation insurance

Four principal covers:

1. **Hull all risks** — physical loss or damage to the aircraft, on an agreed-value basis. Deductibles are large (typically a percentage of hull value on widebodies).
2. **Hull war and allied perils** — war, hijacking, confiscation, terrorism. Written separately because the all-risks policy excludes them (the LSW 555 exclusion clause set). This market repriced violently after the 2022 Russian confiscations.
3. **Passenger, baggage, cargo and third-party legal liability** — a combined single limit, commonly US$1.5–2.5 bn for a large widebody operator, driven by MC99 exposure and by the fifth-jurisdiction risk of US litigation.
4. **Products liability** for manufacturers and MROs, and **airport/ANSP liability** for infrastructure providers.

Also: **loss of licence** cover for pilots (usually via the union or the company scheme), **spares and equipment**, **excess war third-party liability** (raised after 9/11, when the primary war market capped third-party war liability at US$50 m and States had to backstop it).

The market is concentrated in the **Lloyd's of London** subscription market plus Munich Re, Swiss Re, AXA XL, Allianz Global Corporate & Specialty, and the Bermuda and Asian markets. Placement is by broker (Marsh, Aon, WTW, Gallagher), typically at a single annual renewal per airline, with rating driven by fleet value, exposure (passenger numbers, seat counts), loss record, safety audit standing (IOSA) and route structure.

**Regulation (EC) 785/2004** sets minimum insurance requirements for air carriers and aircraft operators flying into, within or over the EU — minimum third-party liability by MTOM band, and minimum per-passenger cover of 250,000 SDR. MC99 Article 50 imposes the general obligation.
