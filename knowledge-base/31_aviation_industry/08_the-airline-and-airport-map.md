# The airline and airport map

**Summary.** This is the reference file of actors and numbers. Everything here carries a date; anything without one should not be used. The industry's shape as of 2026: three US majors and Southwest dominate the largest domestic market; three groups (Lufthansa, IAG, Air France-KLM) plus Ryanair, Wizz and easyJet dominate Europe; IndiGo dominates India and is becoming a global player; the three Gulf carriers run the world's sixth-freedom connecting business; China's big three are large but internationally under-developed; and Africa's market is dominated by Ethiopian with almost every other flag carrier in some stage of restructuring, liquidation or state rescue.

> ⚠️ **Every figure below carries its date. Fleet counts change weekly and financial results annually. Verify before use.**

## 1. Rankings — the caveat first

Ranking airlines requires choosing a metric, and the metrics do not agree:

- **By RPK** — United Airlines led in 2024.
- **By mainline fleet size** — United Airlines, 2024.
- **By revenue, assets, market capitalisation and brand value** — Delta Air Lines, 2024.
- **By passengers carried** — American Airlines Group (including American Eagle), 2024.
- **By destinations served** — United Airlines, 2024.
- **By number of routes** — Southwest Airlines, 2024.
- **By countries served** — Turkish Airlines, 2024.
- **By freight tonne-kilometres** — FedEx Express, 2024.

Sixth-freedom carriers rank high on RPK relative to their revenue because RPK counts the whole two-leg journey. Domestic-heavy carriers rank high on passengers relative to RPK. Neither distortion is an error; they are different questions.

**Exposure**: the June 2026 IATA data showed **Middle East carrier RPK down 13.9% year on year with a 76.1% load factor**, and Qatar Airways' own FY2025/26 statement referenced geopolitical events affecting the final month of the year. The sixth-freedom model has no domestic market to absorb a regional shock.

Etihad's history is the cautionary tale of the three: the "equity alliance" strategy of the 2010s — minority stakes in airberlin, Alitalia, Jet Airways, Air Serbia, Air Seychelles, Virgin Australia, Darwin — collapsed when several partners failed, and **Etihad Airways Partners ceased in 2018**. The response was retrenchment: cancelling 42 A350-900s, 2 A350-1000s and 19 of 24 777X in **February 2019**; divesting Air Seychelles (2021) and completing the Air Serbia exit by 2023; and reframing as a smaller, premium, Abu Dhabi-focused carrier. **Also in the region**: Turkish Airlines (the largest sixth-freedom operator by countries served, hub at Istanbul, ~49% state-owned via the Turkey Wealth Fund), Saudia and the new **Riyadh Air** (PIF-owned, launched to build a second Saudi hub), flydubai, Air Arabia, Gulf Air, Oman Air (oneworld from 2024), Kuwait Airways.

## 5. The European groups

### Lufthansa Group

Airlines: Lufthansa, SWISS, Austrian, Brussels Airlines, **ITA Airways**, Lufthansa City Airlines, Air Dolomiti, Eurowings, Discover Airlines, plus Lufthansa Cargo and Lufthansa Technik. Second-largest European group by passengers, largest in Europe and fourth in the world by revenue. Carried 93 million passengers in 2022.

**ITA Airways** — Lufthansa agreed on 25 May 2023 to pay **€325 million for 41%**, with an option to acquire the remainder at a price contingent on performance; the deal cleared the European Commission with remedies and closed in January 2025 **[needs-verification on the current shareholding]**.

Lufthansa Technik is one of the largest independent MRO businesses in the world and a significant profit contributor in its own right.

## 6. Asia-Pacific

- **IndiGo (InterGlobe Aviation)** — **434 aircraft (August 2025)**, over **2,700 daily flights to 137 destinations** — 94 domestic, 43 international (November 2025) — **64.2% domestic market share (August 2025)**, **31.9 million passengers in Q4 2025**, and **profit after tax of ₹7,258 crore for FY ended 31 March 2025**. Order book: **500 A320neo family aircraft (June 2023)**, the largest commercial aircraft order in history, plus **30 A350-900 with options for 70 more (April 2024, ~US$5 bn)** with deliveries from 2027. IndiGo is becoming the fourth force in global connecting traffic.
- **Air India / Air India Express** — privatised to Tata in January 2022; a very large 2023 order (470 aircraft across Airbus and Boeing) and the absorption of Vistara; a long and difficult transformation programme.
- **China's big three** — China Southern, China Eastern, Air China. Enormous domestic operations; international networks that have never matched the domestic scale, and that shrank further with the Russian overflight advantage and the post-COVID recovery lag. IATA's June 2026 data showed **China domestic RPK down 5.2% year on year**.
- **Singapore Airlines** — the quality benchmark; Temasek-owned; the Air India stake (25.1% after the Vistara merger); Scoot as the LCC arm.
- **Cathay Pacific** — recovering from the Hong Kong closure years; Qatar Airways holds ~9.99%.
- **ANA and JAL** — Japan's duopoly, both with strong JVs into the US.
- **Qantas / Jetstar** — Australia's dominant group; Project Sunrise A350-1000ULRs for nonstop Sydney–London and Sydney–New York, deliveries from 2026 **[needs-verification on the current schedule]**.
- **Korean Air / Asiana** — the merger completed in 2024 after four years of regulatory review, creating a single Korean mega-carrier.
- **AirAsia Group (Capital A)**, **Lion Air**, **VietJet**, **Cebu Pacific** — the Southeast Asian LCCs.

## 7. Africa

Africa carries roughly 2% of world air traffic on 18% of the world's population, with the highest cost structures, the thinnest markets, and the most protectionist bilateral regime. IATA's June 2026 data showed African carriers as the **fastest-growing region at +3.8% RPK, with the lowest load factor at 73.9%** — which is exactly the structural story: growth without density.

### South African Airways

The collapse and the aftermath:

- Not profitable since **2011**.
- Entered **business rescue on 5 December 2019**.
- Government halted emergency funding in April 2020; on **2 May 2020** it was announced SAA would cease operations after 86 years with liquidation to begin 8 May.
- **July 2020**: creditors accepted a restructuring plan, avoiding liquidation.
- **R50 billion** of government assistance between 2004 and 2020; **R32 billion** of accumulated losses 2008–2020; a further **R10.5 billion** bailout in October 2020.
- **June 2021**: a Strategic Equity Partnership with the **Takatso Consortium** — 51% to Takatso, 49% to the state, with a promised R3 billion investment. After nearly three years the government **terminated** the SEP and reverted to 100% state ownership.
- **February 2026 position**: **19 aircraft** with plans for 21; **17 destinations** across Africa, Australia and South America; 100% state-owned; a **R155 million profit on R8.8 billion of revenue for the 2025 financial year**, revenue up 35.9% year on year.

The rest of the South African market is where the actual capacity is: **FlySafair** (the dominant domestic LCC), **Airlink** (the largest independent regional carrier in Southern Africa, in which **Qatar Airways took 25% in August 2024**), **CemAir**, and **Lift**. **[ZA]** The 2020s South African market has effectively been rebuilt around private operators after the flag carrier's collapse and the failures of SA Express, Mango and Comair/kulula (Comair, which operated British Airways' franchise, was liquidated in 2022).

### Kenya Airways

- Fleet as of January 2026: Boeing 787s, 737-800s, Embraer E190s and 737-800SFs, with a **US$400 million five-year fleet expansion** to take the fleet from **34 to 53 aircraft by 2029**.
- **44 destinations on 61 routes** (August 2023).
- Ownership after the 2017 restructuring: **Government of Kenya 48.9%**, **KQ Lenders Company 2017 Ltd (bank consortium) 38.1%**, **KLM 7.8%**, employees and private holders 5.2%.
- **FY2024**: profit of **KSh 5.4 billion**, passengers up 10% to **2.54 million**, revenue up 22% to **KSh 91 billion**. A pre-tax profit of **US$42.82 million** reported in March 2025.
- The 2017 debt-to-equity restructuring converted roughly half a billion US dollars of loans into equity; the government guaranteed a further **US$525 million** owed to US EXIM and **US$225 million** in bank loans. "Project Kifaru" is the current turnaround programme.

### Air Namibia and the Namibian air service situation **[NA]**

**The liquidation.** Air Namibia had been loss-making since Namibian independence. In October 2020 the Belgian lawyer acting for **Challengair** notified the airline that it was insolvent and owed **€18.5 million**, with repayment scheduled to September 2021. On **11 February 2021** the Namibian government announced the immediate shutdown and liquidation. At closure the airline operated **nine aircraft** and employed approximately **600 staff**. Liabilities had reached about **N$3 billion** against assets below **N$1 billion**, and roughly **N$11 billion** of government support had been provided over the preceding 20 years. The cost of the shutdown itself was estimated at **US$453 million**.

**Since then.** The government ruled out reviving the airline or its name. A successor, referred to as **"Namibia Air"**, received approval in **2024** and was anticipated to begin operations between **June and December 2026** — a date that should be re-verified, as similar announcements have slipped repeatedly.

**What actually flies in Namibia now:**

- **FlyNamibia** (formerly FlyWestair; scheduled operations began June 2019, rebranded 2 November 2021) is the de facto domestic and regional carrier. Its parent is **Westair Aviation**, whose overall fleet is over 30 aircraft; FlyNamibia operates Embraer, Beechcraft and Cessna types with the **ERJ-145** as its largest, plus a Reims-Cessna F406 for safari work. **Airlink acquired a 40% equity stake in September 2022**, and from **28 August 2023** FlyNamibia flights operate under **Airlink's 4Z code** under a franchise agreement. It joined IATA in September 2023. Routes include domestic services plus Cape Town, Victoria Falls and Maun.
- **International carriers at Hosea Kutako (WDH)** include South African Airways (Johannesburg), Airlink, Ethiopian Airlines (Addis Ababa), KLM (Amsterdam), Eurowings Discover (Cologne/Bonn, Munich and Victoria Falls, daily since July 2022), Air Botswana (Gaborone, Maun, Kasane, from late 2024), Qatar Airways (Doha), and **Edelweiss Air (Zurich) from June 2026**. Turkish Airlines announced a future service in October 2023.
- **Hosea Kutako International Airport** handled **1,681,336 passengers in 2019**. A **N$250 million** congestion alleviation project was announced in September 2019 targeting doubled capacity to 2030 with a new arrivals hall and expanded departures; a **third terminal** was announced in August 2025 along with apron expansion; and long-term plans exist for a new **Code 4F** aerodrome with a new runway, taxiways, terminal and control tower.

**The lesson.** Namibia is the clearest case study in the file of the central African aviation question: whether a small state should own an airline at all. The N$11 billion spent over 20 years bought a network that a 40%-Airlink-owned private operator and a set of foreign carriers now provide at no cost to the fiscus — but with less control over which routes exist and at what price. That trade-off, not the airline's operational failings, is the real argument.

### Other African carriers

**Royal Air Maroc** (Star Alliance from 2020, growing as a Casablanca hub), **EgyptAir**, **Air Algérie**, **Tunisair**, **RwandAir** (Qatar Airways 49%, and the partner in the planned Bugesera airport), **TAAG Angola**, **Air Tanzania**, **Uganda Airlines**, **Air Senegal**, **Air Côte d'Ivoire**, **ASKY** (Ethiopian 27%, Lomé hub), **Fastjet** (the low-cost experiment that has repeatedly restructured), **Mozambique's LAM** (in near-permanent crisis), **Air Zimbabwe**, **Air Botswana**. The **Single African Air Transport Market (SAATM)**, launched in 2018 to implement the 1999 Yamoussoukro Decision, has been signed by a majority of AU states and implemented by very few.

## 8. Airports

The busiest airport rankings for 2025 (ACI World data referenced but not fully retrieved here — the figures below are indicative and should be verified):

- **Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta (ATL)** has been the world's busiest airport by total passengers continuously since 1998 and retained the position in 2025.
- Other consistent top-ten members: **Dubai International (DXB)** — the busiest by *international* passengers for over a decade; **Dallas/Fort Worth (DFW)**; **Tokyo Haneda (HND)**; **London Heathrow (LHR)**; **Denver (DEN)**; **Istanbul (IST)**; **Chicago O'Hare (ORD)**; **Los Angeles (LAX)**; **Delhi (DEL)**; and the large Chinese hubs (**Guangzhou CAN**, **Shanghai PVG**, **Beijing PEK/PKX**, **Shenzhen SZX**, **Chongqing CKG**, **Kunming KMG**, **Xi'an XIY**). **Singapore Changi** handled approximately 70 million and **Amsterdam Schiphol 68.8 million** in the referenced 2025 data.
- **Cargo**: **Hong Kong (HKG)** has led by cargo tonnage for most of the last two decades, followed by **Memphis (MEM, FedEx)**, **Shanghai Pudong (PVG)**, **Anchorage (ANC)**, **Louisville (SDF, UPS)**, **Incheon (ICN)**, **Dubai (DXB)** and **Doha (DOH)**.

Hub types worth distinguishing:

| Type | Examples | Characteristic |
|---|---|---|

Structural trend: engine OEMs have captured a large share of aftermarket value through long-term service agreements, and independent MROs are being squeezed into airframe and component work. The PMA (Parts Manufacturer Approval) and DER-repair sector, led by HEICO, is the main competitive counterweight and is fought over hard.
