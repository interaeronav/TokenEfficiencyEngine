# The aviation industry — domain map

**Summary.** Commercial aviation is not one industry but a chain of loosely coupled, differently regulated businesses that between them move roughly 5 billion passenger journeys and about 70 million tonnes of cargo a year, on revenues of just over one trillion US dollars, at a net margin that has averaged low single digits across the cycle (IATA, December 2025 forecast for 2026: US$1.053 trillion revenue, US$41 billion net profit, 3.9% net margin). The airline sits in the middle of that chain and captures the least of its value. Around it sit manufacturers, lessors, airports, air navigation service providers, maintenance organisations, ground handlers, distributors and insurers — most of which earn better returns than the airline does, and several of which are monopolies the airline cannot avoid. This file is the map: who the actors are, what each one sells, and how money and legal authority flow between them.

## The actors

### 1. The airline

The airline is an asset-light-or-heavy operator of a perishable inventory. Its product — a seat on a specific flight — has zero value the instant the door closes, which drives everything about how it prices (see `02_airline-economics-and-finance.md`). Legally it is an **air operator** holding an **Air Operator Certificate (AOC)** issued by the State of the Operator, plus an **operating licence** (in the EU, under Regulation 1008/2008) that establishes ownership, control, financial fitness and insurance. It does not usually own the airport, the airspace, the ground handling, the reservation system, or in many cases the aircraft.

Structural types worth distinguishing:

- **Legacy / full-service network carrier (FSNC)** — hub-and-spoke, multiple cabins, alliance membership, cargo belly business, large distribution cost. Lufthansa, Delta, Singapore Airlines.
- **Low-cost carrier (LCC)** — single fleet type, high aircraft utilisation, point-to-point, unbundled fares, direct distribution. Ryanair, Southwest, IndiGo.
- **Ultra-low-cost carrier (ULCC)** — LCC economics pushed further: higher seat density, ancillary revenue exceeding half of total revenue. Wizz Air, Spirit (pre-restructuring), Frontier.
- **Sixth-freedom global connector** — hub placed geographically to intermediate traffic between two other regions. Emirates, Qatar Airways, Turkish Airlines, Singapore Airlines to an extent.
- **Regional / feeder** — operating under capacity purchase agreements for a mainline brand, often not carrying commercial risk at all. SkyWest, Air Nostrum.
- **Charter / leisure / ACMI** — vertically integrated with tour operators (TUI, Condor) or wet-leasing to other airlines (Hi Fly, Avion Express).
- **Cargo** — freighter operators (FedEx, UPS, Cargolux, Atlas Air) and combination carriers using belly capacity.

### 2. The manufacturer (OEM)

Two firms — **Airbus** and **Boeing** — hold the large commercial jet duopoly; **Embraer** and **ATR** own the regional segment; **COMAC** is the credible state-backed challenger. Below them a deep tier of systems and engine suppliers: **CFM International** (GE Aerospace + Safran), **Pratt & Whitney (RTX)**, **Rolls-Royce**, **Honeywell**, **Collins Aerospace**, **Thales**, **Safran**, **Liebherr**, **Spirit AeroSystems** (being reabsorbed by Boeing and Airbus).

The OEM sells three things: airframes, a certification basis (type certificate and its continued airworthiness obligations), and — increasingly — the aftermarket. Engine makers in particular sell power by the hour and make most of their money on spares and overhaul, not on the engine sale, which is often loss-leading. This is why engine choice is a strategic decision for an airline, not a technical one.

Authority flow: the OEM holds the **Type Certificate (TC)** issued by the State of Design's authority (FAA for Boeing, EASA for Airbus), validated by other authorities under bilateral aviation safety agreements. It issues **Service Bulletins**; the authority issues **Airworthiness Directives (AD)** which are mandatory. Design changes by third parties require a **Supplemental Type Certificate (STC)**.

### 3. The lessor

Roughly half the world commercial fleet is leased rather than owned (widely cited as 50–55%; the exact share depends on definition and is best re-verified against a Cirium or IBA figure with a date attached). Lessors buy aircraft — often in very large orders at deep discounts — and rent them to airlines. Their business is spread: the difference between their cost of capital and the lease rate factor, plus residual value at end of lease.

The majors as of the mid-2020s: **AerCap** (the largest, after acquiring ILFC in 2014 and GECAS in November 2021; 3,532 aircraft, engines and helicopters owned, managed or on order at 31 Dec 2022; revenue US$7.996 bn and net income US$2.099 bn for FY2024), **Air Lease Corporation**, **SMBC Aviation Capital**, **BOC Aviation**, **Avolon**, **ICBC Leasing**, **Aircastle**, **Nordic Aviation Capital** (regional jets, twice restructured). Ireland is the centre of gravity for tax and legal reasons — Dublin hosts the leasing industry and the Cape Town Convention's International Registry.

### 4. The airport

An airport is a regulated infrastructure monopoly selling two products: **aeronautical services** (landing, parking, passenger service charges, sometimes security) and **non-aeronautical** (retail concessions, car parking, property, fuel farm rents). At most large airports non-aeronautical revenue is the profit engine; aeronautical charges are frequently capped by an economic regulator using a single-till or dual-till RAB model (UK CAA regulates Heathrow this way).

Airports do not usually employ the people who touch your aircraft. They lease space to ground handlers, fuel companies, caterers and the airlines themselves. Ownership varies from fully state-owned (most of Asia and the Gulf) through corporatised (Schiphol Group, Aéroports de Paris) to privately listed (AENA, Fraport, Auckland) to concession-operated (Vinci, GMR, TAV).

**Capacity** is the scarce commodity. At coordinated (Level 3) airports, access is rationed by **slots** allocated under the IATA Worldwide Airport Slot Guidelines and, in Europe, Regulation 95/93 — historic slots are grandfathered subject to the use-it-or-lose-it rule (80/20 in normal times). Slots at Heathrow have traded for tens of millions of dollars per pair; treat any specific price as needing verification.

### 5. The air navigation service provider (ANSP)

The ANSP owns nothing the passenger sees and controls everything the pilot does between gates. It provides air traffic control, aeronautical information, and often communication/navigation/surveillance infrastructure, under the State's Annex 11 obligations. Models range from government department (many States), through state-owned corporation (DFS, ENAV, Airways New Zealand), to non-profit user cooperative (**NAV CANADA**, the model most often held up as best-in-class), to the US **FAA Air Traffic Organization**, which is inside the regulator — an arrangement most of the world has moved away from precisely because it mixes oversight with service provision.

Charges are levied per flight, generally on a formula involving distance flown in the charging zone and aircraft weight. In Europe, **EUROCONTROL's Central Route Charges Office** bills on behalf of member states — a single invoice covering dozens of national airspaces. Terminal charges are billed separately. Under the Single European Sky performance scheme, unit rates are set ex ante against cost-efficiency targets with a traffic risk-sharing mechanism.

**CANSO** is the ANSP trade body, the counterpart to IATA.

### 6. The regulator

Three tiers:

1. **ICAO** — a UN specialised agency, not a regulator. It sets Standards and Recommended Practices (SARPs) in the 19 Annexes to the Chicago Convention; States implement them in national law. ICAO audits States via **USOAP** (safety) and **USAP** (security).
2. **Regional authority** — **EASA** in the EU (Basic Regulation (EU) 2018/1139), which has genuine supranational competence for certification, and shares operations/licensing rulemaking with national authorities that do the oversight.
3. **National CAA** — issues AOCs, licences, certificates of airworthiness, and does surveillance. FAA (US), UK CAA, QCAA (Qatar), GCAA (UAE), SACAA (South Africa), NCAA (Namibia).

Authority flows down: ICAO SARP → State law → operator's Operations Manual → crew. It never flows the other way, but States may **file differences** under Article 38 when they cannot or will not comply.

### 7. The MRO

Maintenance, repair and overhaul splits into:

- **Line maintenance** — turnaround checks, defect rectification, done at the gate, usually by the airline or a contracted line station provider.
- **Base (heavy) maintenance** — the aircraft is out of service for days or weeks. Increasingly offshored to lower-cost bases: Central and Eastern Europe, Turkey, North Africa, Southeast Asia, China.
- **Engine overhaul** — the highest-value segment, dominated by the OEMs and their joint ventures (GE, Safran, MTU, Rolls-Royce, Lufthansa Technik, ST Engineering, SIA Engineering).
- **Component MRO** — wheels, brakes, avionics, APUs, landing gear.

Legally the MRO holds an approval — **EASA/UK Part-145** or **FAA Part 145** — and works to data controlled by the airline's **CAMO** (Continuing Airworthiness Management Organisation, EASA Part-CAMO) or the FAA equivalent under the operator's Continuous Airworthiness Maintenance Program. The **CAMO is the airline's**, the **Part-145 is the shop's**: the CAMO decides what work is due and issues the work order; the 145 does the work and issues the **Certificate of Release to Service (CRS)**.

### 8. The ground handler

Ramp handling, baggage, pushback, de-icing, passenger check-in, cargo terminal operations. A high-volume, low-margin, labour-intensive business with chronic staff turnover. **Swissport**, **Menzies Aviation** (now merged with Agility's National Aviation Services as Menzies), **dnata** (Emirates Group), **WFS** (Worldwide Flight Services, owned by SATS), **Çelebi**, **Havaş**. Ground damage to aircraft is one of the industry's largest uninsured-cost problems; IATA's Ground Damage Prevention work puts the annual cost in the low billions of dollars, a figure worth re-verifying against a current IATA source.

### 9. The distributors and the money

- **GDS / Global Distribution Systems**: Amadeus, Sabre, Travelport — the pipes between airline inventory and travel agents. They charge a booking fee per segment. The **NDC (New Distribution Capability)** transition is the airlines' attempt to disintermediate them (see `07_aviation-software.md`).
- **OTAs**: Booking Holdings, Expedia, Trip.com — retailers with enormous customer acquisition advantage.
- **IATA Clearing House / ICH and ACH** — multilateral netting of interline billings between airlines.
- **BSP (Billing and Settlement Plan)** — how travel agent money reaches the airline.
- **Card acquirers** — take 1–3% of every ticket, and hold back cash from airlines they consider a credit risk (holdbacks have killed airlines faster than fuel prices).

### 10. Insurance and finance

Aviation insurance is written by a small London/Bermuda/continental market: hull all-risks, hull war, passenger and third-party liability, and the products liability tower for manufacturers. Post-2022 the market hardened sharply over the Russia lessor claims. Aircraft finance uses commercial bank debt, export credit agency support (limited by the Aircraft Sector Understanding), capital markets (EETCs in the US, JOLCO in Japan), and increasingly private credit. The **Cape Town Convention** (2001, Aircraft Protocol in force 1 March 2006, 85 contracting parties including the EU) makes repossession enforceable and cheapens the debt.

## How money flows

For a rough sense of proportion on a typical network airline's cost base (see `02` for the detail and the caveats), per US$100 of revenue collected from the passenger:

- ~US$25–30 leaves as **fuel** (varies enormously with crude; IATA's 2026 forecast has fuel at US$252 bn on US$1.053 trn revenue, ~24%)
- ~US$20–30 as **labour**
- ~US$10–15 as **aircraft ownership** (lease rentals, depreciation, interest)
- ~US$10 as **maintenance and materials**
- ~US$8–12 as **airport, en-route and ground handling charges**
- ~US$5–8 as **distribution, sales and reservations**
- ~US$5 as **catering, passenger services, insurance, overheads**
- ~US$3–4 remains as **net profit** in a good year

The airline is therefore a pass-through entity with a thin residual claim. Almost every counterparty in the list above has pricing power the airline lacks: the airport is a monopoly, the ANSP is a monopoly, the OEM is half a duopoly, the engine shop holds the certification data, the GDS holds the corporate travel channel, and the fuel supplier at an airport is often a single consortium. The airline's only genuine pricing power is over the passenger, which is why revenue management is the most sophisticated function in the business.

## How authority flows

```
Chicago Convention 1944 (Art. 37/38)
        │
        ▼
ICAO Annexes 1–19 (SARPs)
        │  States adopt, or file differences
        ▼
State law  ── EU: Basic Regulation 2018/1139 → EASA Implementing Rules (Part-FCL, Part-ORO, Part-CAT, Part-145, Part-66, Part-CAMO, Part-ATO)
        │  ── US: 14 CFR Parts 1, 21, 25, 43, 61, 91, 117, 119, 121, 135, 145, 147
        ▼
National CAA: AOC, operating licence, C of A, licences, approvals
        │
        ▼
Operator's Operations Manual Parts A–D, MEL, CAME, Training Manual
        │
        ▼
Crew, engineers, dispatchers — the individual's licence and the company's procedure
```

Two accountability constructs matter and are frequently misunderstood:

- **Accountable Manager** — a named individual with corporate authority to ensure all operations and maintenance can be financed and carried out to the required standard. Not a figurehead; personally answerable to the authority.
- **Commander's authority** — Annex 2 and national law give the pilot-in-command final authority over the disposition of the aircraft, which can override the company. This is the last defence in the system and the reason just culture (see `09`) matters commercially, not just ethically.
