# Aircraft design and manufacturing — the state of play and where it is going

**Summary.** The commercial aircraft industry in 2026 is production-constrained rather than demand-constrained: Airbus had delivered 418 aircraft in the first seven months of 2026 against a backlog of about 9,358, while Boeing's programmes remain caught up in the certification and quality aftermath of the MAX crisis, with the 737 MAX 7 and MAX 10 now expected in service around 2027 and the 777-9 first delivery pushed to 2027 — roughly seven years late. The next genuinely new single-aisle is the industry's biggest open question, and its answer is being deferred because none of the candidate technologies (open rotor, hydrogen, ultra-high bypass, truss-braced wing, blended wing body) yet offers a step change large enough to justify the tens of billions and the certification risk. Meanwhile the design process itself has changed more than the products: model-based systems engineering, the digital thread, and simulation-driven certification have quietly replaced a large fraction of the drawing and the physical testing that defined the previous generation.

## 2. The narrowbody duopoly and its challengers

The A320 family and 737 between them account for the overwhelming majority of commercial jet deliveries and an even larger share of profit. Barriers to entry: certification credibility, a global product support network, residual values that depend on a large operator base, and a supply chain that will not prioritise a low-rate newcomer.

**C919.** Type certificated by the CAAC on **29 September 2022**; first delivery to China Eastern on 9 December 2022; **16 aircraft delivered by end-2025**, well short of the 30 targeted for 2025, with engine supply the binding constraint. The aircraft uses CFM **LEAP-1C** engines and a large amount of Western content (Honeywell, Collins, Parker, Liebherr, Safran), which makes it hostage to US export policy — sales were suspended in May 2025 and restored in July 2025. The indigenous **CJ-1000A** engine is targeted for certification around 2027–2028. EASA validation is in progress (EASA test pilots flew verification flights in Shanghai in January 2026) with approval realistically 2028–2031.

Assessment: the C919 is not a near-term competitive threat to Airbus and Boeing outside China, but it is a permanent structural change — it removes roughly one-fifth of the world narrowbody market from contestation over time, which is a material loss of future volume for the duopoly.

**MC-21.** MC-21-300 with PW1400G engines was certificated in Russia in December 2021 and denied European certification in March 2022. The import-substituted **MC-21-310** with Aviadvigatel **PD-14** engines flew in December 2020; the first fully Russian-produced aircraft (s/n 73055, Russian avionics, domestic carbon-fibre wing) flew in April 2025. Certification is expected between September and December 2026, with Aeroflot deliveries planned from 2026. Production forecasts have collapsed from 72/year to around 36/year even after 2029. It is a domestic substitution programme, not a global competitor.

## 3. The next single-aisle question

Both OEMs face the same problem: the A320neo and 737 MAX will be commercially viable into the 2040s, the backlogs stretch most of a decade, and the cost of a clean-sheet narrowbody plus its engine is plausibly US$25–50 billion. Nobody launches that for a 10% improvement. The threshold is generally taken to be a **20–30% fuel-burn reduction** at acceptable risk. The candidate technologies:

### Open rotor / open fan — CFM RISE

An unducted single-stage rotor followed by non-rotating variable-pitch stator vanes that de-swirl the flow and can close almost completely to act as an air brake, plus a recuperator recovering exhaust heat to preheat compressor discharge air. Announced June 2021; target **>20% fuel-burn and CO₂ reduction** versus current engines; 100% SAF capable; entry into service mid-2030s. GE tested first rotating components in June 2023. CFM and Airbus agreed at Farnborough 2022 to fly the demonstrator on an **A380 flying testbed from 2026**, using a GE Passport core.

Why it is hard: very large fan diameter forces a high-wing or aft-mounted installation (ground clearance), noise certification with an unshrouded rotor, blade-off containment without a nacelle (a certification problem that has never been solved for a commercial transport), and installed drag/interference.

### Hydrogen — Airbus ZEROe

Three liquid-hydrogen concepts unveiled September 2020 with a stated target of entry into service by **2035**. At the Airbus Summit in **March 2025** Airbus provided programme updates; the widely-reported outcome was a substantial delay to that 2035 target and a reprioritisation toward fuel-cell architectures and enabling technologies rather than a near-term product **[needs-verification on the exact revised date and scope]**.

Honest assessment: liquid hydrogen has roughly three times the gravimetric energy density of kerosene and about a quarter of the volumetric energy density, which forces large insulated cryogenic tanks that cannot be put in a wing. That changes the aircraft's shape, its structural weight, its cabin volume and its turnaround procedures. Add the absence of green hydrogen at scale, of airport distribution infrastructure, and of a certification basis, and a commercially meaningful hydrogen transport aircraft before 2040 looks improbable. Small regional applications (fuel cell, 20–80 seats) are more plausible and are where ZeroAvia, Universal Hydrogen (which ceased operations) and others have concentrated.

### Ultra-high bypass geared turbofan

The lowest-risk path: push bypass ratio from ~11–12 toward 15–20 with a geared architecture, accept the nacelle drag and weight penalty, and take perhaps 10–15%. Pratt & Whitney's GTF Advantage and its successors are on this line. The trouble is that the installation on a low-wing narrowbody is already at the limit of ground clearance — the 737 MAX's engine position, and the aerodynamic consequences that led to MCAS, is the direct result of that constraint on a 1960s airframe. A new airframe with taller gear or a different wing position solves it, which is another argument for a clean sheet.

### Truss-braced wing — Boeing X-66

NASA's **Sustainable Flight Demonstrator** awarded Boeing **US$425 million over seven years in January 2023**, with Boeing and partners contributing an estimated **US$725 million**. The X-66A was to be a modified MD-90 with a very high aspect ratio, strut-braced transonic wing, targeting **~30% better efficiency** than a 737 MAX/A320neo when combined with advanced engines. Airframe conversion began at Palmdale in 2023.

**On 24 April 2025 Boeing indefinitely paused the X-66 programme**, saying it would continue thin-wing research without the flight demonstrator. This is the single most important data point about the next narrowbody: the airframer with the greatest commercial need for a step change chose to stop the flight demonstration, which pushes any TTBW product well into the 2040s.

### Blended wing body

Structurally efficient (lift distributed along the span, less bending relief penalty), aerodynamically excellent (high L/D), and commercially awkward: non-cylindrical pressure vessel (the hardest structural problem in the concept), passengers far from the centreline experiencing large vertical accelerations in roll, evacuation certification, and no compatibility with existing gates, jet bridges or cargo handling. **JetZero** (with US Air Force funding and a stated Alaska Airlines and United interest) is the live programme; a full-scale demonstrator has been repeatedly targeted for the late 2020s. Treat all timelines as aspirational.

### The realistic answer

The consensus that has emerged by 2026 is that the next single-aisle will be a conventional tube-and-wing with a very high aspect ratio wing (folding tips), extensive composite or hybrid structure, an open-fan or very-high-bypass engine, more-electric systems, and 100% SAF capability, entering service in the mid-to-late 2030s — and that whoever launches second will win, because the first mover carries the engine risk. Boeing's balance sheet and certification standing make Airbus more likely to move first, and Airbus has no commercial reason to.

## 4. Composite versus metallic structure

The 787 and A350 established large-scale composite primary structure: barrel-section fuselage (787) or composite panels on a composite frame (A350), composite wings on both. Benefits: better specific strength and stiffness, no fatigue cracking in the metallic sense, no corrosion, higher cabin altitude and humidity possible because the structure is not corrosion-limited, and aeroelastic tailoring of the wing.

Costs: capital-intensive autoclave tooling; slow cure cycles that cap production rate; difficult, expensive and skills-dependent repair (a metallic patch is a day, a bonded composite repair is a certification exercise); barely-visible impact damage that requires NDT to find; and, decisively, **rate**. A narrowbody must be built at 60–75 per month. Autoclave-cured composite structure cannot economically reach that rate today.

Hence the research emphasis on **out-of-autoclave** processing: resin transfer moulding (RTM) and its variants, thermoplastic composites (which can be welded rather than fastened, and re-melted), and automated fibre placement at much higher deposition rates. Airbus's **Wing of Tomorrow** and the Clean Aviation programmes, and Boeing's composite wing work at the 777X's Composite Wing Center, are all fundamentally rate-and-cost programmes rather than weight programmes. A parallel line of work is advanced aluminium-lithium alloys and friction-stir welding, which can recover much of the weight benefit at metallic cost and rate — which is why the A220 and 737 MAX have metallic wings and are competitive.

## 5. Model-based design and the digital thread

The change in *how* aircraft are designed is more advanced than the change in what they look like.

### CAD and PLM

- **Airbus** — **CATIA V5** has been the production standard for two decades (the A350 was largely V5), with **3DEXPERIENCE** (V6) adopted for newer work; PDM historically via ENOVIA and Airbus's own PASS/Zamiz layers. The 3DEXPERIENCE move is as much about the platform (requirements, systems, simulation and manufacturing data in one model) as about geometry.
- **Boeing** — historically **CATIA V5** on the 787 and 777X, with **Dassault ENOVIA/DELMIA**, but Boeing announced a strategic shift to **Siemens NX and Teamcenter** for future programmes as part of its digital transformation; execution has been slower and more contested than announced **[needs-verification on the current programme scope]**.
- **Embraer** and much of the supply chain — **Siemens NX/Teamcenter**, or **PTC Creo/Windchill**.

### The digital mock-up (DMU)

The 777 (1990s) was the first aircraft designed without a physical mock-up — the DMU replaced it, allowing interference checking, clash detection, maintainability and access analysis, and assembly sequencing in software. That is now table stakes. What has changed since is that the DMU has become the **single source of geometric truth** feeding: tooling design, NC programming, work instructions on the shop floor (often on a tablet in 3D), the illustrated parts catalogue, the maintenance manual figures, and the training courseware.

### The digital thread and MBSE

The **digital thread** is the ambition that a requirement can be traced forward to a geometric feature, a manufacturing operation, an inspection record and an in-service part, and backward from a field failure to the requirement that allowed it. **MBSE** (Model-Based Systems Engineering, usually SysML with tools such as Cameo/MagicDraw or Capella) replaces the document-centric systems engineering that ARP4754A describes with an executable model of the system architecture, its functions, its failure modes and its allocations.

The certification argument (ARP4754A/ED-79A, and DO-331 for model-based software development) is that a model can be shown to satisfy requirements more completely than a document review can. The practical benefit is change impact analysis: when a requirement moves, the model shows what it touches.

**Digital twin** in aerospace has two distinct meanings that are frequently conflated: (a) an as-designed high-fidelity simulation model used in development; (b) an as-built, as-maintained instance model of a specific tail number, updated with operational data, used for prognostics. (b) is where the money is (GE's and RR's engine health businesses) and (a) is where most of the marketing is.

### Generative design, topology optimisation and additive manufacturing

**Topology optimisation** (SIMP, level-set methods; tools such as Altair OptiStruct, nTop, Ansys, Autodesk Fusion's generative tools) removes material from a design domain subject to load cases and constraints, producing organic-looking structures with substantially better stiffness-to-mass. The output is usually only manufacturable additively.

**Additive manufacturing in production parts** is now genuine but narrow:

- **GE Aerospace LEAP fuel nozzle tip** — the canonical case: 20 parts consolidated into one, ~25% lighter, five times the durability, produced in six figures cumulatively.
- **GE9X** — a substantial number of additively-manufactured components including the combustor mixer and heat exchangers.
- **Airbus** — titanium brackets, bleed pipes, cabin partitions (the A320 "bionic partition"), and a large number of spares.
- **Boeing** — structural titanium parts on the 787 (Norsk Titanium's rapid plasma deposition), and thousands of polymer parts in non-structural applications.

Constraints: qualification of the process (every machine, powder lot and parameter set must be controlled, and the FAA/EASA certification path for AM is still maturing — see the FAA's additive manufacturing roadmap and EASA's CM-S-008), surface finish and fatigue, and inspection (internal porosity requires CT scanning at production rates).

### Simulation-driven certification

Physical testing is being displaced, not eliminated. Certification credit for analysis is granted where the method is validated:

- **CFD** now sizes and refines the aerodynamic design almost entirely, with the wind tunnel used for validation at a small number of conditions and for the certification database rather than for design iteration. RANS is the workhorse; hybrid RANS-LES for separated and buffet-onset conditions.
- **Structural FEA** with virtual testing reduces but does not remove the static and fatigue test articles; full-scale static test to ultimate load and a two-lifetime fatigue test remain mandatory in practice.
- **Systems** are certified through model-in-the-loop, software-in-the-loop and hardware-in-the-loop rigs — the "iron bird" — with a large fraction of the flight-test points now demonstrated on the rig first.
- **Flight test** has shrunk in hours per programme but not disappeared. The A350 programme flew roughly 2,600 flight-test hours to certification; earlier programmes of similar complexity flew far more per unit of new technology.

EASA's and the FAA's positions are set out in the "Modelling and Simulation — CS-25 Structural Certification Specifications" work and in the FAA's policy on the use of analysis for certification credit. The direction of travel is clear and the pace is limited by validation evidence, not by computing power.

## 6. Rate ramp and supply chain, 2026

The industry's binding constraint since 2021 has been supply, not demand:

- **Engines** — the largest single problem. Pratt & Whitney's GTF powder-metal contamination issue grounded a large share of the A320neo fleet for accelerated inspections from 2023, with hundreds of aircraft on ground at the peak and a multi-year recovery. LEAP durability in hot-and-harsh environments (Middle East, India) forced early shop visits. Engine MRO capacity is now itself a constraint.
- **Structures** — Spirit AeroSystems' quality and financial problems led to Boeing agreeing to reacquire it and Airbus taking over the Spirit work packages for the A220 and A350; that transaction has absorbed enormous management attention on both sides.
- **Castings and forgings** — a small number of qualified suppliers, long lead times, and a workforce that left during COVID.
- **Labour and skills** — the experience lost in 2020–21 has not been replaced; first-time quality on the line is measurably worse than pre-pandemic.
- **Raw materials** — titanium supply was disrupted by the Russian sanctions (VSMPO-AVISMA was a major supplier to both OEMs).

The consequence for airlines: delivery slips of 6–18 months are normal, lease rates and used aircraft values have risen sharply, retirements have been deferred, and the fleet is ageing. This is a large part of why airline margins improved in 2024–26 — capacity discipline was imposed by the supply chain rather than chosen by management.

## 7. Sustainable aviation fuel and net zero — an honest assessment

Aviation's commitment, adopted by IATA in 2021 and reflected in ICAO's 2022 Long-Term Aspirational Goal, is **net zero CO₂ by 2050**. IATA's roadmap allocates the reduction as approximately: **SAF ~65%**, new aircraft technology ~13%, operations and infrastructure ~3%, and offsets/carbon capture ~19%.

The SAF numbers:

- **2024 production ~1 Mt; 2025 ~1.9 Mt; 2026 projected ~2.4 Mt = 0.8% of jet fuel consumption** (IATA).
- Requirement for the 2050 roadmap: **~500 Mt/year** — over **250×** current production.
- Cost: the HEFA premium in Europe reached roughly **US$1,000 per tonne over production cost** in 2024, including certification, transaction and supply-chain margins.

Pathways, in rough order of maturity:
1. **HEFA** (hydroprocessed esters and fatty acids) — used cooking oil, tallow, vegetable oils. Commercially mature and the source of essentially all current SAF. Feedstock-limited: the global supply of waste fats and oils cannot support more than a few percent of jet demand.
2. **ATJ** (alcohol-to-jet) — ethanol or isobutanol. Scaling now (LanzaJet, Gevo).
3. **FT-SPK** (Fischer-Tropsch) from municipal solid waste, forestry residue or biomass. Capital-intensive, several first-of-a-kind plants have failed.
4. **PtL / e-fuel** — hydrogen from renewable electricity plus captured CO₂. The only pathway without a feedstock ceiling, and by far the most expensive; requires very large amounts of cheap renewable power.

Mandates are doing the work that markets are not: the EU's **ReFuelEU Aviation** regulation requires 2% SAF in 2025, 6% in 2030, 20% in 2035, 34% in 2040, 42% in 2045 and **70% in 2050**, with a sub-mandate for synthetic fuels; the UK has a comparable mandate; the US uses tax credits (the 45Z/40B structure) instead. **CORSIA** (ICAO Annex 16 Vol IV) requires offsetting of growth in international emissions above a baseline, and is in its first mandatory phase from 2024 for participating States, with eligible-unit supply itself now a constraint.

**Honest assessment.** The 2050 target is not currently on a credible trajectory. SAF at 0.8% of fuel in 2026 needs a compound growth rate that no fuel industry has ever sustained, and the capital required is measured in trillions. The technology levers (13%) are real but slow, because fleet turnover takes 25 years and the next-generation aircraft has not been launched. The offsets component (19%) depends on a carbon removal market that does not yet exist at scale and whose integrity is contested. What is likely: material progress, mandates that raise ticket prices, a growing gap between the stated target and the outcome, and increasing political pressure on demand (short-haul flight bans, frequent-flyer levies, aviation's inclusion in the EU ETS with free allowances now phased out). Airlines that treat this as a compliance-cost forecasting problem rather than a communications problem will be better placed.

Non-CO₂ effects are the elephant: contrail cirrus is estimated to contribute effective radiative forcing of the same order as, or greater than, aviation's cumulative CO₂, and is entirely absent from the net-zero accounting. Contrail avoidance by tactical routing around ice-supersaturated regions is cheap (a small fuel penalty on a small percentage of flights) and is being trialled by several airlines and ANSPs; it is probably the highest-leverage near-term climate action available to the industry, and it is not in anyone's target.

## 8. Electric, hybrid and eVTOL

**Regional electric.** Battery specific energy (~250–300 Wh/kg at pack level for current automotive-derived chemistry, against ~11,900 Wh/kg for kerosene, before accounting for the ~2× efficiency advantage of an electric powertrain) confines pure battery-electric flight to short ranges with small payloads. Programmes: Eviation Alice (uncertain), Heart Aerospace ES-30 (hybrid, 30 seats, repeatedly rescheduled), Harbour Air's electric Beaver conversion, and ZeroAvia and Universal Hydrogen on the fuel-cell side (Universal Hydrogen ceased operations in 2024). Realistic near-term outcome: hybrid-electric commuter aircraft in the 2030s serving very short sectors, not a mainstream segment.

**eVTOL.** The FAA created a certification path via **powered-lift** special class rules under 14 CFR 21.17(b), and issued the powered-lift SFAR for operations in 2024. Status:

- **Joby Aviation** — G-1 certification basis February 2021; FAA Part 135 certificate May 2022 (operating conventional aircraft); first FAA-conforming S4 began flight testing **March 2026** at Marina, with aircraft N547JX designated for **Type Inspection Authorization** testing by Joby and FAA pilots; a JFK-to-Manhattan demonstration flight in **April 2026**. Type certification remains pending.
- **Archer Aviation**, **Beta Technologies**, **Vertical Aerospace**, **EHang** (which holds a CAAC type certificate for the unmanned EH216-S and is the only certificated eVTOL in commercial service anywhere) are the other credible programmes.

Assessment: certification of the aircraft is being solved; the unsolved problems are battery life and replacement economics, noise acceptance, vertiport infrastructure, and — decisively — the pilot supply and the operating cost per seat-mile. The business case in most published models depends on eventual autonomy, which is a further certification problem an order of magnitude harder.

## 9. Autonomy and single-pilot operations

The debate is real and is moving. Terminology:

- **eMCO / Extended Minimum Crew Operations** — two pilots for departure and arrival, one pilot at the controls in cruise while the other rests, on long-haul. This is the near-term step and is under active study at EASA with Airbus and Dassault.
- **SiPO / Single-Pilot Operations** — one pilot for the whole flight, with ground support.
- **Reduced Crew Operations (RCO)** with a ground-based "pilot" monitoring several aircraft.

Drivers: crew cost (roughly 5–8% of operating cost, but a much larger share of the *controllable* cost), pilot supply constraints, and cargo operators' economics.

Objections, which are substantial and mostly not answerable with current technology:

1. **Incapacitation.** The two-crew system's principal safety function is mutual monitoring and redundancy against subtle incapacitation, not just collapse. Automated detection of subtle incapacitation is unsolved.
2. **The monitoring problem.** A single pilot supervising a highly automated system for eight hours will suffer vigilance decrement; the accident record (AF447, Asiana 214, and the automation-surprise literature generally) shows that two well-trained pilots already fail at this.
3. **Workload in the non-normal case.** The design case is not the normal flight; it is the depressurisation, the engine failure with a fire, the diversion into weather. The two-crew workload split (pilot flying / pilot monitoring) is the mechanism by which those are managed.
4. **Security.** Removing the second person from the flight deck reverses the post-Germanwings 9525 two-person rule.
5. **Datalink dependency.** Any ground-support concept depends on a communication link that must be assumed to fail.

ICAO has an Advanced Air Mobility and an automation workstream; EASA's public position has been that eMCO could be feasible on a case-by-case basis with substantial mitigations while SiPO in commercial passenger transport is not currently supportable. IFALPA, ECA and ALPA are uniformly opposed. The realistic path is: eMCO on long-haul in the 2030s, single-pilot cargo before single-pilot passenger, and full autonomy in commercial passenger transport not this generation.
