# Becoming a cartographer — education, programmes and registration

**Summary.** There is no single "cartographer" qualification. The route splits early into three legally and commercially distinct tracks: the **regulated land-surveying track** (cadastral survey, statutorily reserved, requires registration with a national council), the **geoinformatics / GIS track** (unregulated in most countries, credential-optional, portfolio-driven), and the **remote-sensing / Earth-observation track** (usually postgraduate, research-flavoured). In southern Africa the regulated track matters more than most people expect: in Namibia only a **professional land surveyor registered under Act 32 of 1993** may sign a survey diagram, and in South Africa the equivalent gate is the **South African Geomatics Council**. Everything else — web mapping, thematic design, EO analytics — is open to anyone who can demonstrate the work.

> ⚠️ **[NA]** Do not describe yourself as a "land surveyor", "technical surveyor" or "survey technician", or sign anything as one, unless registered under Act 32 of 1993. Section 14 prohibits unregistered practice. Producing an unsigned site plan or a GIS map is not surveying; producing a diagram for lodgement with the Surveyor-General is.

## The three tracks, and choosing between them

### Track A — regulated land surveying (cadastral)

This is the track with a legal monopoly and therefore the clearest earnings floor. It leads to the right to survey and subdivide land, to fix beacons, and to sign diagrams that the Surveyor-General accepts and the Deeds Office registers. It is also the most rigid: an accredited degree, a structured articles/candidacy period under a registered practitioner, and a professional examination.

Choose this if you want to work in property development, subdivision, mining rights, engineering setting-out, or public-sector survey.

### Track B — geoinformatics / GIS / spatial data engineering

Unregulated almost everywhere. Employers hire on demonstrated ability: a portfolio, a GitHub profile, a functioning pipeline. Degrees in **geography, geoinformatics, geomatics, computer science, environmental science or civil engineering** all feed into it. The **GISP** credential exists and is valued in parts of the United States public sector and in some consultancies; elsewhere it is optional.

Choose this if you want the widest job market and the fastest self-taught entry.

### Track C — remote sensing / Earth observation

Usually postgraduate. The employers are agricultural analytics firms, mining and exploration, insurance and catastrophe modelling, environmental agencies, defence and intelligence, and increasingly the geospatial-AI startups. Requires more mathematics and physics than Track B — radiometry, statistics, signal processing, and now deep learning.

## Undergraduate degrees — what the names mean

The naming is inconsistent internationally, and this matters when checking accreditation.

- **Geomatics / Geomatics Engineering / Surveying** — the survey-led degree. Geodesy, adjustment computations, cadastral law, engineering survey, photogrammetry, GIS. This is the accreditable degree for Track A.
- **Geoinformatics / Geographic Information Science / Geospatial Science** — the data-led degree. Spatial analysis, databases, programming, cartography, remote sensing. Rarely accredited for cadastral practice.
- **Geography (BSc, with a GIS stream)** — broadest, most variable. Excellent if the department has a strong technical GIS group; weak if it is a human-geography department with one GIS module.
- **Civil engineering with a survey stream** — feeds engineering survey and setting-out, not cadastral survey.
- **Cartography (as a named degree)** — rare, and mostly a German/Central European tradition (TU Dresden, TU Munich, TU Wien, University of Twente jointly ran the Erasmus Mundus *Cartography M.Sc.*). If you specifically want design-led cartography, this family is where it lives.

## The southern African route

### **[ZA]** South Africa

| Institution | Offering |

| **Others** | University of Johannesburg, Nelson Mandela University, Tshwane University of Technology and Central University of Technology run survey technician / technologist qualifications feeding the SAGC technician and technologist categories. |

**Registration** is with the **South African Geomatics Council (SAGC)**, the successor to PLATO. All qualified Professional Surveyors, Technologist Surveyors, Technician Surveyors and **GISc Practitioners** must be registered with it; there are corresponding **Surveyor-in-Training** categories for the candidacy period. The GISc Practitioner category is significant because it makes South Africa one of the few countries where GIS practice itself has a statutory professional register — relevant to anyone doing paid GIS work for South African public bodies.

### **[NA]** Namibia

**NUST (Namibia University of Science and Technology)** is the centre of gravity. Two departments matter:

- **Department of Geo-Spatial Sciences and Technology** — Diploma in Geomatics; **Bachelor of Geomatics**; **Bachelor of Geomatics Honours**; **Bachelor of Geoinformation Technology**; **Bachelor of Geoinformation Technology Honours**; **Master of Geoinformation Science and Earth Observation**.
- **Department of Land and Spatial Sciences** — Diploma in Land Administration; **Bachelor of Land Administration** and Honours; Master of Spatial Science (Land Administration) and (Property Studies); PhD in Spatial Sciences. NUST also hosts the **Integrated Land Management Institute (ILMI)**, which is the main publisher of Namibian land-tenure and land-administration research.

The Geomatics stream is the survey-led one; Geoinformation Technology is the data-led one; Land Administration is the tenure/cadastre-policy one — important in Namibia because a very large share of the country is **communal land** administered through Traditional Authorities and Communal Land Boards rather than freehold title.

**UNAM (University of Namibia)** offers geography, environmental science and geoinformation-adjacent programmes; it is not the principal route into professional survey registration. *Needs verification for current programme names.*

**Registration** is with the **Namibian Council for Professional Land Surveyors, Technical Surveyors and Survey Technicians**, established by the **Professional Land Surveyors', Technical Surveyors' and Survey Technicians' Act 32 of 1993** (GG 769; in force 1 June 1994; amended by Act 16 of 1995). The Act:

- establishes the Council and its registrar (Parts II–III);
- maintains a **register of practitioners** and prohibits unregistered practice (s.13–14);
- prescribes qualifications for registration by ministerial regulation — courses of instruction and examinations (s.15);
- requires apprentices to work **under supervision** (s.18);
- gives the Council disciplinary powers over professional conduct (Part V);
- recognises an **approved society** — the **Institute of Land Surveyors of Namibia** (s.7, s.32);
- requires the registrar to notify the **Surveyor-General** of registrations (s.23).

The companion statute governing the *survey work itself* — beacons, diagrams, general plans, approval by the Surveyor-General — is the **Land Survey Act 33 of 1993**.

> ⚠️ **[NA]** The specific prescribed qualifications, articles duration, examination structure and fees are set by **regulation** under s.31 of Act 32 of 1993, not in the Act itself. Those regulations were not retrieved during this research. ## GIS certification — GISP

The **GISP** is administered by the **GIS Certification Institute (GISCI)**, a US non-profit incorporated in **2002**. The current pathway is a **portfolio** assessed across four areas — *educational achievement* (credentials, coursework, conference attendance), *professional experience*, *contributions to the profession*, and *supplemental points* — **plus a proctored exam** covering the GIS body of knowledge. GISCI also runs a **PreGISP (GISP-E)** pathway for emerging professionals.

**Honest assessment:** GISP is close to mandatory for some US state and federal GIS roles, useful as a CV differentiator in consultancies, and largely irrelevant in Europe and southern Africa, where the SAGC GISc Practitioner register (**[ZA]**) carries more weight. Exam and application fees exist but the current amounts were not retrievable — `needs-verification`.

## The self-taught route — a concrete curriculum

This assumes zero background and a target of "employable as a GIS/geospatial analyst, and able to produce a defensible map". It will **not** make you a registered land surveyor — that path is statutory.

**Phase 0 — foundations (2–4 weeks)**
- Install QGIS LTR (3.44.x) — not the bleeding-edge release, until you know why you'd want it.
- Work the official **QGIS Training Manual** end to end. It is free, and it is the single best structured start.
- Learn the vocabulary in `00_overview.md`: datum vs CRS, projection vs transformation, DEM vs DSM vs DTM.

**Phase 1 — coordinate literacy (2–3 weeks)** — *do not skip this; it is the differentiator*
- Read Iliffe & Lott, *Datums and Map Projections* (see `11_books-courses-and-resources.md`).
- Work `02_geodesy-and-coordinate-systems.md` in this domain, and reproduce every PROJ command in it against real data for your own country.
- Exercise: take a GNSS point in WGS 84, transform it to your national grid **both** correctly and with a deliberately wrong datum, and measure the error. Internalise the magnitude.

**Phase 2 — data and formats (3–4 weeks)**
- Download an OSM extract (Geofabrik), a Sentinel-2 scene, Copernicus DEM GLO-30 tiles and a Natural Earth pack for your area.
- Learn `ogr2ogr` and `gdal_translate` / `gdalwarp` from the command line, not the GUI.
- Convert everything to **GeoPackage** and **Cloud-Optimized GeoTIFF**. Stop using Shapefile.

**Phase 3 — analysis and databases (6–8 weeks)**
- PostgreSQL + PostGIS locally. Load the OSM extract. Write spatial SQL: `ST_Intersects`, `ST_Buffer`, `ST_Union`, `ST_DWithin`, `ST_Transform`, and a nearest-neighbour query with a GiST index.
- Python: GeoPandas, Shapely, Rasterio, pyproj. Reproduce three PostGIS queries in GeoPandas and understand which is faster and why.

**Phase 4 — cartographic design (4–6 weeks)** — *the part that makes your output look professional*
- Brewer, *Designing Better Maps*, cover to cover, applying each chapter to one of your own maps.
- Monmonier, *How to Lie with Maps*, for the failure modes.
- Make five maps: a choropleth (correctly normalised), a proportional-symbol map, a hillshaded terrain map, a thematic map with a diverging palette, and a printable A3 site plan at a stated scale.
- Get every one of them critiqued (r/cartography, the Cartography Guild, the ICA Map Design Commission's material).

**Phase 5 — web and cloud (6–8 weeks)**
- Build vector tiles with `tippecanoe`, package as **PMTiles**, host on object storage, render with **MapLibre GL JS** and a hand-written style JSON.
- Read a COG from a public STAC catalogue with `rioxarray` without downloading the whole file.

**Phase 6 — remote sensing (6–8 weeks)**
- Google Earth Engine or the Microsoft Planetary Computer. Compute NDVI, NDWI and NBR time series for an area you know.
- Do one supervised classification and one change-detection job, and report accuracy honestly with a confusion matrix.

**Phase 7 — portfolio and proof (ongoing)**
- Three finished, documented projects with data provenance, licence notes and reproducible code. One should be local and useful to someone.
- Contribute to OpenStreetMap in your own area — it is public, verifiable evidence of spatial data competence.
