# Becoming a space engineer — routes, skills and the African sector

**Summary.** There is no single route into space engineering and, contrary to the folklore, an aerospace degree is not required — the industry hires mechanical, electrical, electronic, physics, materials and computer science graduates in comparable numbers. What is required is demonstrable hands-on work, because the sector's hiring managers have learned that coursework predicts very little. This file covers the degrees and the strongest programmes, the specialisations and what each actually involves day to day, the graduate schemes at agencies and companies, the standards knowledge that separates a hire from a candidate, the amateur and open routes that genuinely lead to jobs, and the African space sector — which is small, growing, and unusually accessible.

> ⚠️ Confidence is **medium**. Programme rankings, salary bands and recruitment cycles change annually and vary by country. Verify current entry requirements and deadlines directly with the institution or employer before acting on anything here.

## 1. The degrees

**What the sector actually hires.** Aerospace/astronautical engineering is the obvious route but supplies a minority of hires. Mechanical engineering is the largest single feeder (structures, thermal, mechanisms, propulsion). Electrical and electronic engineering feeds power, avionics and RF. Computer science and software engineering feed flight software, ground software and autonomy — the fastest-growing demand. Physics feeds instrument science, optics and mission analysis. Materials science, chemical engineering (propellants and combustion) and control engineering (GNC) all have direct paths.

**Undergraduate versus master's.** In continental Europe the master's (or integrated Diplom-Ingenieur/MEng) is effectively the entry qualification. In the UK an MEng is standard and a BEng plus experience works. In the US a BS is enough for entry-level industry roles; an MS helps for specialised roles and is often employer-funded later. A PhD is close to mandatory for instrument science, mission science and research roles at agencies and national labs, and close to irrelevant for most engineering roles — where three years of industry experience is worth more.

**Programmes with genuine depth** (representative, not exhaustive, and not ranked):

- **United States:** MIT (AeroAstro, and the Space Systems Laboratory), Caltech (with JPL adjacency), Stanford (Space Rendezvous Lab, SSDL), Purdue, Michigan (Plasmadynamics and Electric Propulsion Laboratory — the X3 thruster), Georgia Tech, Colorado Boulder (with LASP, which builds and operates real instruments and hires undergraduates to do it), Texas A&M, Illinois, USC.
- **Europe:** TU Delft, ISAE-SUPAERO and ISAE-ENSMA (France), TU München and RWTH Aachen, Politecnico di Milano and Sapienza, KTH and Luleå (the latter's Space Campus at Kiruna is unusually hands-on), Cranfield, Surrey (with SSTL, effectively the birthplace of the low-cost smallsat), Southampton, Imperial, Strathclyde, Universidad Politécnica de Madrid.
- **Elsewhere:** University of Tokyo and Tokyo Institute of Technology, KAIST, Tsinghua and Beihang, IIST and IISc/IITs in India, UNSW and Sydney in Australia, Toronto and Ryerson/TMU in Canada.
- **Africa:** Stellenbosch University (its Electronic Systems Laboratory built SUNSAT and SumbandilaSat and remains the continent's strongest satellite engineering group), Cape Peninsula University of Technology (F'SATI, which built the ZACube series), University of Cape Town, Cairo University and Nile University in Egypt, and the Kenyatta/Nairobi cluster in Kenya.

**The Erasmus Mundus and international master's routes** — SpaceMaster, SEEDS (Politecnico di Torino/ISAE/Bremen) and the ISU (International Space University) Master of Space Studies and Space Studies Program — are worth knowing about. ISU's SSP in particular is a two-month intensive with a strong alumni network and is used by the industry as a mid-career conversion route.

## 2. The specialisations

| Specialisation | What you actually do | Core skills |
|---|---|---|
| **Systems engineering** | Own the requirements, budgets and interfaces; run trade studies; broker between subsystems | Requirements management (DOORS, Jama), MBSE (SysML, Capella), budget discipline, technical writing, the ability to be the least popular person in the room |
| **GNC / ADCS** | Design estimators and controllers; run Monte Carlo campaigns; write and verify the flight algorithms | Control theory, Kalman filtering, quaternion algebra, MATLAB/Simulink, C, Monte Carlo methods |
| **Mission analysis / flight dynamics** | Trajectory design, launch windows, manoeuvre planning, orbit determination | Astrodynamics, optimisation, GMAT/STK/Orekit, Python, numerical methods |
| **Thermal** | Thermal maths models, TVAC test campaigns, radiator and MLI design | Radiation heat transfer, Thermal Desktop/ESATAN, test experience |
| **Propulsion** | Engine or thruster design, test stands, CFD, propellant systems | Thermodynamics, combustion, fluid mechanics, CEA/RPA, test hardware |
| **Structures & mechanisms** | Load paths, launch environment, deployables, FEA, vibration and shock testing | FEA (Nastran, Abaqus, Ansys), fatigue, composites, tolerance stack-ups |
| **Power** | Array and battery sizing, PCDU design, harness | Power electronics, cell chemistry, EMC |
| **RF / communications** | Link budgets, antenna design, transponders, ground stations | RF engineering, modulation and coding, CCSDS standards, HFSS/CST |
| **Flight software** | The code that runs the spacecraft | C, real-time OS (RTEMS, VxWorks, FreeRTOS), cFS, formal verification, DO-178-adjacent rigour |
| **AIT / AIV** | Assemble, integrate, test — cleanrooms, TVAC, vibration, EMC | Meticulousness, test procedure writing, hands-on capability |
| **Operations** | Fly the thing | Procedures, anomaly response, shift work, calm |

The two that are consistently understaffed and therefore easiest to enter are **flight software** and **AIT**. Software because the sector competes with an industry paying twice as much; AIT because it is unglamorous, hands-on and often shift-based. Both are excellent entry points that lead anywhere.

## 3. Graduate schemes and entry routes

**Agencies.**
- **ESA Young Graduate Trainee (YGT)** — one year, paid, at ESTEC/ESOC/ESRIN/ESAC. Open to nationals of ESA member and associate states. Competitive; the annual cycle typically opens in autumn. ESA also runs the **National Trainee** and **Research Fellowship** programmes, and **ESA Academy** training sessions and the **Fly Your Satellite!** CubeSat programme are open to student teams from member states.
- **NASA Pathways** — internships that convert to civil service positions; **US citizens only**. NASA also runs the **Space Grant** consortia (state-level, undergraduate) and the summer internship programme (OSSI/Handshake). JPL runs its own JPL Summer Internship Program and the **JPL Year Round Internship**.
- **DLR, CNES, ASI, UKSA** all run national graduate and trainee programmes with nationality/residency constraints.
- **JAXA, ISRO, CSA** likewise, generally nationality-restricted.

**Companies.**
- **Primes:** Airbus Defence and Space, Thales Alenia Space, OHB, Lockheed Martin, Northrop Grumman, Boeing, L3Harris (and Aerojet Rocketdyne), MDA, Mitsubishi Electric. All run structured graduate schemes with rotations. Security clearance requirements are the main barrier and are nationality-bound.
- **New space:** SpaceX, Rocket Lab, Blue Origin, Firefly, Relativity, Stoke, Astranis, Planet, Spire, ICEYE, Isar Aerospace, D-Orbit, Exotrail, ThrustMe. These hire far more on demonstrated project work than on grades, run much shorter interview loops, and expect a portfolio. SpaceX's internship programme is a well-known conversion pipeline and is brutal in both senses.
- **Suppliers and SMEs:** where a great deal of the actual engineering happens and where a graduate gets far more responsibility far sooner. SSTL, Bradford Space, Nammo, Beyond Gravity (ex-RUAG), AAC Clyde, GomSpace, Enpulsion, Busek.

**The portfolio is the differentiator.** In practice a candidate with a mediocre degree, a CubeSat they helped fly, and a GitHub with a working orbit propagator beats a candidate with a first and nothing else. Concretely useful things to have done: contributed to a university satellite that reached orbit (or even one that didn't — the integration and test experience is real either way); built and flown a high-power rocket with recovery electronics you wrote; implemented an attitude estimator and validated it against real sensor data; contributed to Orekit, poliastro/hapsira, Basilisk or OpenRocket; competed at Spaceport America Cup or in a CanSat competition.

## 4. Standards knowledge

This is the least-taught, most-valued knowledge in the field, and the reason a candidate who has read the right documents interviews better than one who has not.

**ECSS** (European Cooperation for Space Standardization) — five branches: **E** space engineering, **M** space project management, **Q** space product assurance, **S** system implementation, **U** space sustainability. Standards to know by number:

- **ECSS-E-ST-10C Rev.1** (15 February 2017) — System engineering general requirements. The one to read first.
- **ECSS-E-ST-32C Rev.1** (15 November 2008) — Structural general requirements.
- **ECSS-E-ST-35C Rev.1** (6 March 2009) — Propulsion general requirements.
- **ECSS-Q-ST-70C Rev.2** (15 October 2019) — Materials, mechanical parts and processes.
- **ECSS-U-ST-20C** (1 August 2019) — Space sustainability, planetary protection.
- Also frequently invoked: ECSS-E-ST-10-03 (testing), ECSS-E-ST-10-04 (space environment), ECSS-Q-ST-60 (EEE components), ECSS-E-ST-50 series (communications), ECSS-M-ST-10 (project planning).

ECSS standards are downloadable from ecss.nl subject to accepting the ECSS licence agreement; a zip of all active standards is provided. Practically free for study purposes.

**NASA standards.**
- **NASA/SP-6105, NASA Systems Engineering Handbook** — revision of **27 March 2024**, free. Sections cover fundamentals, the programme/project life cycle, system design processes, product realisation and crosscutting technical management, and it now incorporates MBSE and the current NPR 7123.1.
- **NPR 7123.1** — Systems Engineering Processes and Requirements (the requirement the handbook explains).
- **NPR 7120.5** — Space Flight Program and Project Management Requirements.
- **NASA-STD-5001** (structural design and test factors of safety), **NASA-STD-7009** (models and simulations), **NASA-STD-8739** series (workmanship — soldering, crimping, cable), **NASA-HDBK-4002** (spacecraft charging), **GEVS (GSFC-STD-7000)** — the General Environmental Verification Standard, which is the practical reference for environmental test levels.

**Other.** CCSDS (space data system standards — packet telemetry, telecommand, SLE, and the file delivery protocol CFDP) governs anything touching the ground segment. ITU Radio Regulations govern frequency allocation and filing. The IADC Space Debris Mitigation Guidelines and national implementations (FCC's five-year LEO disposal rule, ESA's Zero Debris approach) govern disposal.

## 5. Amateur and open routes that actually work

**University rocketry.** The strongest single signal for a propulsion or structures role. Teams design, build, test and fly, and the ones who do it well do a full engineering process including analysis, verification and a flight readiness review.

**Spaceport America Cup / IREC** — run by the **Experimental Sounding Rocket Association (ESRA)** at Spaceport America in southern New Mexico, annually in June (the 2025 event ran 9–14 June). Competition classes are by target altitude (10,000 ft and 30,000 ft) and by propulsion (commercial off-the-shelf solid, student-researched-and-developed solid, liquid, hybrid), with a mandatory payload of at least 4 kg (the "CanSat-like" science payload requirement). Hundreds of teams from dozens of countries; it is the largest student rocketry event in the world. **[Class definitions and payload mass verified against community practice, not the fetched page — treat as needs-verification.]**

**Base11 Space Challenge** — a US$1 M prize for the first student team to reach the Kármán line (100 km) with a liquid-propelled rocket. Announced 2018; no team has claimed it, and its current status should be checked before relying on it.

**Model and high-power rocketry certification** — the legitimate, legal route to building real rockets. Two US bodies, **Tripoli Rocketry Association** and the **National Association of Rocketry (NAR)**, with reciprocal recognition and equivalent structures. Tripoli's levels:

| Level | Motor impulse | Requirements |
|---|---|---|
| **Level 1** | H to I | Age 18+; design, launch and recover a rocket on a certified HPR motor |
| **Level 2** | J to L | L1 first; written exam plus a successful flight and recovery |
| **Level 3** | M to O (>5,120 N·s) | L2 first; electronic recovery proficiency; design approved and flight witnessed by a Technical Advisory Panel member |

Tripoli also runs a **Mentoring Program for ages 12–17** for junior members to build and fly high-power rockets under supervision. Outside the US, national bodies (UKRA in Britain, Tripoli's international prefectures, AeroPac-style clubs) run equivalent schemes, and national aviation authorities control the airspace approvals.

> ⚠️ High-power rocketry is regulated. Motors above certain impulse classes require certification and, in most jurisdictions, explosives-handling permits and airspace clearance. Amateur *liquid* propulsion and any work with energetic materials is significantly more dangerous and more regulated again. Do it through an established club with a range safety officer, never alone.

**Copenhagen Suborbitals** — the most credible amateur launch organisation in the world. Founded **2008** by Kristian von Bengtson and Peter Madsen (both since departed, in February and June 2014), it is a non-profit crowd-funded volunteer effort with roughly **63 active volunteers** as of September 2024 and about **600 supporting members** whose monthly subscriptions cover most fixed costs. Flight record: **HEAT-1X (3 June 2011, 2.8 km** — engine shut down at 16 s from pitch instability against a 30 km target), **SMARAGD (27 July 2012, 8.2 km)**, **Nexø I (23 July 2016**, partial success on insufficient LOX supply), **Nexø II (4 August 2018, 6.5 km**, recovered by parachute). The current programme, **Spica**, is a 12–14 m crewed suborbital vehicle using clustered **BPM-25 LOX/ethanol engines at 25 kN each**, aiming above 100 km. Whether or not it ever flies a person, it is an open, documented, genuinely amateur liquid rocket programme and its engineering write-ups are a public education resource.

**CubeSat programmes.** ESA's **Fly Your Satellite!** takes university teams through a real ESA-style development and test campaign with access to ESTEC facilities. NASA's **CubeSat Launch Initiative (CSLI)** provides free launch to US educational and non-profit teams via the ELaNa missions. The **UNOOSA/JAXA KiboCUBE** programme deploys CubeSats from the ISS Kibo module for institutions in developing countries — explicitly designed for exactly the kind of team an African university would field, and the route by which Kenya's first satellite reached orbit.

**Open-source contribution.** Orekit (Java astrodynamics, mature and industrially used), Basilisk (astrodynamics simulation, University of Colorado), OpenRocket, RocketPy, hapsira (the maintained fork of poliastro), NASA's core Flight System (cFS), and NASA Trick are all open and all take contributions. A merged pull request into Orekit is a concrete, checkable credential.

## 6. The African space sector

Small, fragmented, growing, and unusually open to newcomers — the ratio of interesting work to competition is far better than in Europe or the US.

**African Space Agency (AfSA)** — its statute was adopted in **January 2018**; the agency was **founded 24 January 2023** when the host agreement was signed with Egypt, and it is headquartered in **New Cairo** alongside the Egyptian Space Agency. All **55 African Union member states** are eligible to participate. Critically, AfSA is a **coordination body, not an operator** — it does not build satellites or launch. Its mandate is harmonising national space policies under AU Agenda 2063, running the African Space Policy and Strategy, and coordinating shared infrastructure. The proposed **Pan-African University of Space Science and Technology** (intended for South Africa) has stalled. As of early 2023 **fewer than 50 satellites were controlled by African states**, concentrated in Algeria, Angola, Egypt, Kenya, Morocco, Nigeria and South Africa.

**South Africa — SANSA.** Established **9 December 2010** under the **National Space Agency Act, 2008**, with roughly **270 permanent and 36 non-permanent employees**. Its **Space Science** directorate at Hermanus hosts **the only Space Weather Regional Warning Centre in Africa** within the International Space Environment Service, providing forecasts for communications, navigation, defence and aviation. **Hartebeesthoek** is a historic deep-space tracking station that supported NASA missions from the 1950s to the 1970s and remains a ground-station asset. Satellite heritage: **SUNSAT** (1999, built by Stellenbosch University, launched from Vandenberg — the first South African satellite), **SumbandilaSat/ZASAT-002** (2009, launched from Baikonur), and the **ZACube** series from CPUT's F'SATI. South Africa also hosts the MeerKAT array and the African share of the SKA — which is radio astronomy rather than space engineering but is the continent's largest science infrastructure and a substantial employer of engineers.

**Egypt** — the Egyptian Space Agency (EgSA, established 2018) is the AfSA host and has an active satellite programme largely built in partnership with China (EgyptSat series, NExSat-1, MisrSat-2 delivered 2023). It operates an assembly, integration and testing facility in Cairo — one of only a handful on the continent.

**Nigeria** — NASRDA, the longest-running programme, with NigeriaSat-1 (2003), NigeriaSat-2 and NigeriaSat-X (2011, the latter built by Nigerian engineers trained at SSTL in Surrey — an explicit and successful capacity-transfer model) and the NigComSat-1R communications satellite (2011, Chinese-built).

**Kenya** — the Kenya Space Agency (2017). **1KUNS-PF**, a 1U CubeSat built by University of Nairobi students, was deployed from the ISS in May 2018 under the UNOOSA/JAXA KiboCUBE programme — the clearest demonstration that the CubeSat/KiboCUBE route is real. **Taifa-1**, an Earth-observation satellite developed by Kenyan engineers with SayariLabs and EnduroSat, launched on a Falcon 9 Transporter mission in April 2023.

**Namibia** **[NA]** — Namibia has no national space agency and no indigenous satellite programme. Its participation is through: the **ESA/ESTRACK ground station and the SANSA-adjacent tracking infrastructure in southern Africa**; the **High Energy Stereoscopic System (H.E.S.S.)** gamma-ray observatory in the Khomas Highland near Gamsberg, which is a genuinely world-class facility and a route into astronomy and instrumentation; and the **Southern African Development Community (SADC)** and African Union frameworks. The practical routes for a Namibian into space engineering are (a) study at Stellenbosch, CPUT or UCT in South Africa, which have the continent's strongest satellite engineering groups and take regional students; (b) the ESA/AfSA-adjacent scholarship and training schemes; (c) H.E.S.S. and the Namibian astronomy community as an entry to instrumentation; or (d) the fully remote routes — open-source contribution, remote software roles, and the growing number of new-space companies hiring distributed software and GNC engineers.

**Other programmes worth knowing:** Algeria (ASAL, Alsat series), Morocco (Mohammed VI-A/B), Ghana (GhanaSat-1 via KiboCUBE, 2017), Rwanda (RwaSat-1, and an ambitious ITU filing for a large constellation), Ethiopia (ETRSS-1, 2019), Angola (AngoSat-2, 2022), Tunisia (Challenge-1, 2021), Mauritius (MIR-SAT1 via KiboCUBE), and Zimbabwe (ZimSat-1 via BIRDS/KiboCUBE). The **BIRDS** programme run by Kyushu Institute of Technology is a particularly effective capacity-building route: multinational student teams build a 1U CubeSat together and take the skills home.
