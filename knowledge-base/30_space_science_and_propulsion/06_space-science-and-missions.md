# Space science and the significant missions

**Summary.** This is the catalogue of what space has actually been used for. Each entry gives launch date, agency, the instruments that mattered, and the headline result. The organising insight is that mission classes have distinct engineering signatures: space telescopes are pointing-and-thermal problems; planetary flybys are navigation-and-communication problems; landers are entry-descent-and-landing problems; sample returns are all three plus planetary protection; and human spaceflight is a life-support and abort problem that dominates everything else.

## 1. Astronomy from space

Why leave the ground at all? Three reasons: the atmosphere is opaque to most of the spectrum (all of X-ray and gamma, most of UV and infrared); it blurs images (seeing limits ground telescopes to ~0.5–1 arcsec without adaptive optics); and it glows in the infrared, drowning faint sources.

**Hubble Space Telescope** — launched 24 April 1990 on STS-31; 2.4 m primary, LEO at ~540 km. Its spherical-aberration flaw (the primary was polished 2.2 μm too flat because of a mis-assembled null corrector) was corrected by COSTAR on Servicing Mission 1 in December 1993, and five servicing missions in total replaced instruments and gyros. Headline results: the extragalactic distance scale and the Hubble constant to a few percent, the Hubble Deep/Ultra-Deep Fields, direct evidence for supermassive black holes in galactic nuclei, and (with ground-based supernova work) the accelerating expansion of the universe.

**Chandra X-ray Observatory** — launched 23 July 1999 on STS-93 into a highly elliptical 64-hour orbit reaching a third of the way to the Moon (to escape the belts and get long uninterrupted exposures). Grazing-incidence Wolter Type I nested mirrors give 0.5 arcsec resolution — a hundred times better than any prior X-ray telescope. Headline results: the Bullet Cluster observation separating baryonic gas from the gravitational mass, effectively direct evidence for dark matter; imaging of supernova remnant structure; and cluster cooling flows.

**Spitzer Space Telescope** — launched 25 August 2003, Earth-trailing heliocentric orbit, 0.85 m mirror cooled to 5.5 K by liquid helium (exhausted 2009, after which the "warm mission" ran the two shortest IRAC bands until retirement in January 2020). Pioneered exoplanet secondary-eclipse and phase-curve spectroscopy; discovered the seven-planet TRAPPIST-1 system's full architecture.

**Kepler and TESS.** Kepler (launched 7 March 2009) stared at a single 115 deg² field in Cygnus in an Earth-trailing orbit, monitoring ~150,000 stars photometrically for transits; after two of four reaction wheels failed it flew the K2 extended mission using solar radiation pressure as a virtual third axis. It confirmed over 2,600 planets and established that planets are common and that small planets are the most common. **TESS** (launched 18 April 2018 into a 13.7-day 2:1 lunar-resonant orbit) surveys nearly the whole sky in sectors for planets around bright, nearby stars suitable for follow-up characterisation.

**Gaia** — launched **19 December 2013** on a Soyuz ST-B/Fregat-MT from Kourou to a Lissajous orbit around Sun–Earth L2, reached 8 January 2014. Three instruments: astrometry (Astro), blue/red photometers (BP/RP), and the Radial Velocity Spectrometer. It measured positions, parallaxes and proper motions for about **1.3 billion stars**, with standard deviations of **6.7 microarcseconds or better for bright stars (magnitude 3–12)**. Data releases: DR1 14 September 2016, DR2 25 April 2018, EDR3 3 December 2020, DR3 13 June 2022, with DR4 expected no earlier than December 2026 and the final DR5 no earlier than end of 2030. **Last targeted observation 10 January 2025; spacecraft decommissioned 27 March 2025.** Gaia is arguably the highest-impact-per-euro science mission ever flown: it re-founded the cosmic distance ladder, mapped Milky Way structure and its merger history, and its catalogue underpins a large fraction of all current astronomy.

**JWST** — launched **25 December 2021 on Ariane 5** from Kourou; halo orbit around Sun–Earth L2 with radius varying between 250,000 and 832,000 km. **6.5 m primary of 18 gold-plated beryllium hexagonal segments, 25.4 m² collecting area** — about six times Hubble's. Instruments: NIRCam (0.6–5 μm imaging plus wavefront sensing), NIRSpec (0.6–5 μm spectroscopy), MIRI (5–27 μm), FGS/NIRISS (0.8–5 μm). Five-layer aluminium-coated Kapton E sunshield, each layer ~0.1 mm, holding the cold side **below 50 K** with MIRI required to stay **under 6 K**. NASA lifetime cost **US$9.7 billion** (US$8.8 bn development, US$861 M for five years of operations), plus ESA ~€700 M and CSA ~CA$200 M. First images 12 July 2022. Early results: galaxies observed as they appeared 13.1 billion years ago, transmission spectroscopy detecting water around WASP-96b, and instrument spectral precision better than 1,000 ppm — exceeding requirements.

**Euclid** — launched **1 July 2023 at 15:12 UTC on a Falcon 9**, Lissajous orbit at Sun–Earth L2 reached in about 30 days. **1.2 m primary**; VIS is a 600-megapixel visible imager (530–920 nm), NISP a 65-megapixel near-infrared spectrometer/photometer (920–2,020 nm). Six-year nominal survey of **≈15,000 deg² (4.6 sr, about a third of the sky)** measuring ~10 billion sources, targeting dark energy and dark matter through weak gravitational lensing and galaxy clustering. Early Release Observations May 2024; Quick Data Release 1 on **19 March 2025**; releases planned through October 2031.

**Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope** — **2.4 m primary** (a repurposed NRO mirror), targeting launch **30 August 2026 on a Falcon Heavy** (launch cost ≈US$255 M) to a Sun–Earth L2 halo orbit. The Wide-Field Instrument is a 300.8-megapixel camera with a **0.28 deg² field of view — 100× the area of Hubble's imagers** at 0.11 arcsec resolution across 0.48–2.30 μm. The Coronagraph Instrument (575–825 nm) is a technology demonstrator for part-per-billion starlight suppression. Objectives: dark energy by multiple independent techniques, an exoplanet census by gravitational microlensing (sensitive to cold and free-floating planets that transit surveys miss), and direct imaging. Development cost US$3.2 bn; maximum total US$3.934 bn.

## 2. Planetary science

**Voyager 1 and 2** — launched **5 September 1977 and 20 August 1977** on Titan IIIE. Voyager 2 remains the only spacecraft to have visited Uranus (1986) and Neptune (1989), using the once-per-176-year Grand Tour alignment. Discoveries: volcanism on Io, the first evidence for a subsurface ocean on Europa, Jupiter's ring, Titan's thick nitrogen atmosphere, Neptune's Great Dark Spot and Triton's nitrogen geysers. Voyager 1 crossed the **heliopause on 25 August 2012 at 121 AU**; as of **March 2026 it is at 172.59 AU (25.8 billion km)**, with **two instruments still operating** (plasma wave subsystem and magnetometer) on RTGs that produced 470 W at launch and may support engineering telemetry until about 2036.

**Galileo** — launched 18 October 1989 from STS-34; arrived Jupiter December 1995. Its high-gain antenna failed to deploy, forcing the entire mission onto the low-gain antenna with aggressive data compression — a masterclass in operational recovery. Delivered the only atmospheric entry probe into a giant planet, and the gravity and magnetometer data establishing subsurface oceans at Europa, Ganymede and Callisto.

**Cassini–Huygens** — launched 15 October 1997, Saturn orbit insertion 1 July 2004, and 13 years of operations ending in the deliberate atmospheric entry of 15 September 2017 (chosen to protect Enceladus and Titan from contamination). ESA's **Huygens probe landed on Titan on 14 January 2005** — the most distant landing ever performed. Headline results: the Enceladus south-polar plumes and the subsurface ocean they vent from, with Cassini's mass spectrometer detecting molecular hydrogen and silica grains implying hydrothermal activity; Titan's methane lakes and hydrological cycle; and the structure and age of the rings.

**Juno** — launched 5 August 2011, Jupiter orbit insertion 4 July 2016 into a highly elliptical polar orbit that minimises time in the radiation belts. Solar-powered at 5.2 AU with ~60 m² of arrays — a deliberate demonstration that RTGs are not mandatory in the outer system. Electronics in a titanium radiation vault. Results: a deeper-than-expected zonal wind structure (down ~3,000 km), a "fuzzy" dilute core rather than a compact one, and polar cyclone arrays.

**New Horizons** — launched 19 January 2006 on an Atlas V 551, the fastest Earth departure ever at the time (C3 ≈ 158 km²/s²). Pluto flyby **14 July 2015**; Arrokoth (2014 MU69) flyby 1 January 2019 — the most distant object ever visited. Pluto proved geologically active, with the nitrogen-ice Sputnik Planitia convecting, water-ice mountains, and a layered haze atmosphere.

**Mars, Viking to Perseverance.** Viking 1 and 2 (landed 20 July and 3 September 1976) performed the first successful surface operations and the first — inconclusive — life-detection experiments. Mars Pathfinder/Sojourner (1997) demonstrated airbag landing and roving. Spirit and Opportunity (landed January 2004) found abundant aqueous mineralogy; Opportunity ran for 14 years against a 90-sol design life. Curiosity (landed 6 August 2012 via the sky crane) confirmed a long-lived habitable lacustrine environment in Gale crater and detected seasonal methane variation. **Perseverance** (landed 18 February 2021, Jezero crater) is a caching rover; **Ingenuity** flew 72 times over three years, the first powered flight on another world. Orbiters — Mars Odyssey (2001), Mars Express (2003), MRO (2005), MAVEN (2013), the ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter (2016) and the UAE's Hope (2020) — supply relay and long-baseline monitoring; India's Mangalyaan (2013) and China's Tianwen-1 with the Zhurong rover (2021) broadened the field.

**Mars Sample Return — the programme's central problem.** As of July 2025 Perseverance has filled **33 of 43 sample tubes** (8 igneous, 13 sedimentary, 3 igneous/impactite, 1 serpentinite, 1 silica-cemented carbonate, 2 regolith, 1 atmospheric, 3 witness), with **ten additional samples cached at the Three Forks depot** as a backup. The NASA–ESA return architecture requires a lander carrying a Mars Ascent Vehicle and sample transfer arm plus two Ingenuity-class helicopters, and an ESA Earth Return Orbiter. The cost trajectory: a **2023 estimate of US$7 bn with samples back by 2033**; NASA paused the programme in November 2023 for an independent review; an **April 2024 update found an US$11 bn cost infeasible with return slipping to 2040**. NASA subsequently solicited cheaper architectures from industry and JPL. Meanwhile **China's Tianwen-3** targets a late-2028 launch and **sample return in July 2031**, and Japan's **MMX** will return a sample from Phobos (launch 2026, return 2031). If those hold, the first Martian material on Earth may be Chinese.

**Small-body sample return.**
- **Hayabusa** (2003) returned ~1,500 microscopic grains from asteroid Itokawa in **June 2010** — the first asteroid sample return, achieved despite ion engine failures, a lost rover, a fuel leak and a three-year delay.
- **Hayabusa2** — launched **3 December 2014 on an H-IIA 202**, rendezvoused with **162173 Ryugu on 27 June 2018**. Deployed four rovers (HIBOU and OWL on 21 September 2018, DLR/CNES **MASCOT** on 3 October 2018, MINERVA-II-2 on 2 October 2019), fired a Small Carry-on Impactor on 5 April 2019 to expose subsurface material, and performed surface sampling on 21 February 2019 and subsurface sampling on 11 July 2019. **5.4 g returned to Woomera on 6 December 2020.** Key finding: C-type asteroid material is far more porous than assumed, explaining why such meteorites rarely survive entry. Extended mission: flyby of 98943 Torifune July 2026, rendezvous with the fast-rotating 1998 KY26 in July 2031.
- **OSIRIS-REx** — launched **8 September 2016 on an Atlas V 411**, target **101955 Bennu** (480–511 m mean diameter). TAG sample collection at the "Nightingale" site **20 October 2020**; **121.6 g collected**; capsule landed at the Utah Test and Training Range **24 September 2023**. Instruments: OCAMS, OVIRS, OTES, OLA, REXIS, TAGSAM. Analysis found **14 of the 20 biological amino acids and all four DNA/RNA nucleobases**, plus evidence of aqueous alteration on the parent body. The extended mission, **OSIRIS-APEX**, rendezvouses with 99942 Apophis in April 2029 for an 18-month study following its close Earth approach.
- **Rosetta** — launched 2 March 2004, arrived at comet 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko August 2014, deployed the **Philae** lander 12 November 2014 (which bounced, landed in shadow and operated ~60 hours on batteries). Rosetta itself operated until a controlled descent on 30 September 2016. Results: the D/H ratio in 67P's water is ~3× terrestrial, arguing against Jupiter-family comets as the main source of Earth's oceans; detection of molecular oxygen and glycine in the coma.

**Currently en route or planned.**
- **BepiColombo** (ESA/JAXA) — launched **20 October 2018 on Ariane 5 ECA**. Two orbiters, the 1,150 kg MPO (11 instruments) and the 285 kg Mio magnetospheric orbiter (5 instrument groups), carried by the 2,615 kg Mercury Transfer Module with four QinetiQ T6 ion engines. Nine gravity assists; **Mercury orbit insertion targeted November 2026**, slipped from December 2025 after a September 2024 thruster power anomaly.
- **Europa Clipper** — launched **14 October 2024 on a fully expendable Falcon Heavy**. 6,065 kg launch mass (2,750 kg propellant), 3,241 kg dry, 30.5 m solar array span producing ~600 W at Jupiter. Mars gravity assist 1 March 2025 (884 km), Earth gravity assist 3 December 2026, **Jupiter orbit insertion 11 April 2030**, then **49 Europa flybys from 25 to 2,700 km** over a four-year science phase. Nine instruments: E-THEMIS, MISE, EIS, REASON (ice-penetrating radar), ECM, PIMS, MASPEX, Europa-UVS, SUDA. Cost ≈US$4.25 bn.
- **Dragonfly** — a **450 kg, eight-rotor (1.35 m diameter) nuclear-powered rotorcraft for Titan**, launching on a **Falcon Heavy in a window from 5 to 25 July 2028** and arriving in **2034**. Lithium-ion battery recharged by an MMRTG (≈70 W desired) during Titan's long nights; each flight covers up to 16 km with ~30 minutes aloft. First landing in dunes southeast of the Selk impact structure in Shangri-La, working toward Selk crater 90 km away. Instruments: DraMS (mass spectrometer), DraGNS (gamma-ray and neutron spectrometer), DraGMet (geophysics and meteorology), DragonCam. 3.3-year science phase within a 10-year mission; lifecycle cost **US$3.35 bn**.
- **JUICE** (ESA, launched April 2023) targets Ganymede orbit in the 2030s — the first spacecraft to orbit a moon other than our own.

## 3. Heliophysics

**Parker Solar Probe** — launched **12 August 2018 at 07:31 UTC on a Delta IV Heavy with a Star 48BV upper stage**. Seven Venus gravity assists over nearly seven years walked perihelion down to **9.86 solar radii (6.1 million km from the surface) on 24 December 2024**, at **190 km/s (690,000 km/h)** — the fastest human-made object. The heat shield is two reinforced carbon–carbon panels around an 11 cm carbon foam core, 2.3 m diameter, withstanding **1,370 °C** on the outside while the spacecraft stays at 29 °C. Four instrument suites: FIELDS, IS☉IS, WISPR, SWEAP. Results: **magnetic switchbacks** in the solar wind, Alfvén waves consistent with coronal heating, a dust-free zone extending 5.6 million km from the Sun, Kelvin–Helmholtz instability in a CME, and 20 sungrazing comets found in WISPR imagery.

**Solar Orbiter** (ESA/NASA, launched 10 February 2020) complements Parker: it goes less close (0.28 AU perihelion) but carries remote-sensing instruments and, crucially, inclines its orbit using Venus gravity assists to reach heliographic latitudes above 30° — the first good look at the Sun's poles, which matter for understanding the solar dynamo.

**SOHO** (1995, Sun–Earth L1, still operating after 30 years), **ACE** and **DSCOVR** (L1, real-time solar wind warning ~15–60 minutes ahead of geomagnetic storms), **SDO** (2010, full-disc EUV imaging every 12 seconds), and **IRIS** complete the working fleet. Space weather is the applied output: geomagnetically induced currents in power grids, HF radio blackouts, satellite drag increases, and radiation dose to aviation crews and astronauts.

## 4. Earth observation science

The scientific rather than commercial half of remote sensing. **Landsat** (continuous since 1972, now Landsat 9) is the longest civilian Earth-imaging record and the reference for land-cover change. **Sentinel-1/2/3/5P** under Copernicus provide free, open, systematic radar and optical coverage — the largest openly-licensed EO archive in existence. **GRACE and GRACE-FO** measure time-variable gravity by inter-satellite ranging, yielding ice-sheet and groundwater mass loss directly — Greenland and Antarctic mass balance figures come from here. **ICESat-2** measures ice-sheet elevation by photon-counting laser altimetry. **CloudSat**, **CALIPSO**, **OCO-2/3** (CO₂ column), **SMAP** (soil moisture), **Aeolus** (the first space-based wind lidar, 2018–2023) and the **TRMM/GPM** precipitation missions round out the climate observing system.

Two structural points. First, Earth observation is where space science most directly feeds public policy — and consequently where budget volatility is highest. Second, the ground segment is the science: calibration, validation, reprocessing and open data distribution are what turn photons into a usable record, and they cost as much as the satellites.

## 5. Human spaceflight

**ISS** — continuously occupied since **2 November 2000**, the longest continuous human presence in space. **1,005 m³ pressurised volume**, roughly 109 m across including solar arrays by 73 m, orbiting at about **400 km and 51.64° inclination**, one revolution every ~93 minutes at 7.67 km/s. It is expected to remain operational **to the end of 2030**; NASA awarded SpaceX a contract in **June 2024** for the **US Deorbit Vehicle** that will perform the controlled destructive re-entry. ESA's Columbus module alone cost about €1.4 bn (≈US$1.6 bn), which gives a sense of the whole programme's scale.

Its scientific output is genuinely mixed — microgravity materials and combustion science, the Alpha Magnetic Spectrometer's cosmic-ray measurements, and above all human physiology data (bone density loss ~1–1.5% per month without countermeasures, fluid shift and spaceflight-associated neuro-ocular syndrome, immune and microbiome changes) that is the only dataset relevant to long-duration exploration.

**Artemis.** **Artemis I** launched **16 November 2022** uncrewed, sending Orion around the Moon for six days and travelling **more than 2.3 million km** before a Pacific splashdown — with greater-than-expected heat shield char erosion that delayed the follow-on. **Artemis II launched 1 April 2026** and completed successfully on **11 April 2026** with Victor Glover, Christina Koch, Jeremy Hansen and Reid Wiseman, reaching a maximum of 252,756 miles from Earth — **the first crewed flight beyond low Earth orbit since Apollo 17 in December 1972**. **Artemis III** is scheduled for **late 2027** as an Earth-orbit rendezvous and docking test with commercial landers (SpaceX Starship HLS and Blue Origin's Blue Moon) and a trial of the Axiom EMU suit; **Artemis IV in early 2028** is targeted as the first crewed landing, with **Artemis V in late 2028**. **The Lunar Gateway was cancelled in 2026**, with NASA shifting to a lunar surface base and elements repurposed. SLS Block 1 produces **39 MN at liftoff** and delivers **27 t to trans-lunar injection** (against Saturn V's 48.6 t). Programme budget: **US$53 bn allocated FY2021–2025, US$93 bn total through FY2025** including US$40 bn spent FY2012–2020.

**Commercial LEO destinations.** With ISS retirement scheduled for 2030, NASA's Commercial LEO Destinations programme funds Axiom Station (initially modules attached to the ISS, later free-flying), Orbital Reef (Blue Origin/Sierra Space), Starlab (Voyager Space/Airbus) and Vast's Haven-1. None has flown a crewed station module as of this writing, and the gap risk between ISS deorbit and commercial availability is the programme's central concern.

**Tiangong** — three modules: **Tianhe core (29 April 2021), Wentian lab (24 July 2022), Mengtian lab (31 October 2022)**. **340 m³ pressurised volume**, orbit 340–450 km at **41° inclination**, roughly a third the size of ISS. Normally three crew, up to six during handovers, with ~180-day missions and **continuous occupation since November 2022**. On **17 December 2024** its crew performed the longest spacewalk ever, over nine hours. It has hosted experiments from 17 countries and in 2022 completed the first full rice life cycle in microgravity, seed to harvest to replanting. The **Xuntian** space telescope — a 2 m-class survey instrument with a field of view hundreds of times Hubble's — is scheduled to launch in **2027** and will co-orbit, docking periodically for servicing. China has announced expansion toward six modules by 2030.

## Open questions

- The final Mars Sample Return architecture and its cost were still unresolved at the time of writing; the figures given are the last officially published estimates.
- Roman's 30 August 2026 launch date is a target as of the source; confirm before citing.
- Commercial LEO destination schedules are highly volatile and no firm dates are given here.
- Qianfan, Xuntian and Chinese crewed lunar programme milestones rest on state announcements without independent confirmation.
