# Concrete block paving — structure, materials, patterns and installation method

**Summary.** Concrete block paving (CBP) is a flexible pavement whose base course and wearing course are the same thing: discrete concrete units, 50–80 mm thick, bedded on 25 ± 10 mm of compacted sand, with 2–6 mm joints filled with a finer sand. Load is carried by *interlock* — the sand-filled joints transfer shear between adjacent blocks so a wheel load is spread over many units rather than punching one. Get seven things right and it lasts decades with no maintenance: subgrade, subbase, bedding sand, the blocks, the jointing sand, the edge restraints, and drainage. Get the bedding sand or the edge restraint wrong and it will fail regardless of how good the blocks are.

> ⚠️ **Never add cement to the jointing sand.** It converts a flexible pavement into a rigid one that cannot accommodate flexural or thermal movement, cracks, admits water, and discolours the paving permanently. The one exception the CMA allows: a little cement in the sand immediately around a downpipe or stormwater outlet where wash-out is likely.

## How interlock actually works

Blocks are manufactured with vertical side faces and are shaped in plan so that adjacent units bear on each other. Clifford's 1984 analysis is the clearest description: blocks interlock through **compressive forces** that keep individual units in place; some **shear** interlock occurs through the sand-filled joints; there is essentially **no tension** interlock (it is not a jigsaw). Interlock in the horizontal plane exists only if the field is contained by kerbing or an equivalent restraint.

Three interlock mechanisms are conventionally distinguished:

- **Vertical interlock** — shear transfer between adjacent blocks through the jointing sand, so a loaded block drags its neighbours down with it and the load is distributed. This is what a properly filled joint buys you, and it is why an empty joint is a structural defect, not a cosmetic one.
- **Rotational interlock** — the block's resistance to tilting under an eccentric wheel load, resisted by the surrounding blocks and the joint sand. Favoured by thicker blocks (higher aspect ratio of thickness to plan dimension) and by narrow joints. This is the main structural argument for 80 mm over 60 mm.
- **Horizontal interlock** — resistance to the blocks spreading apart or creeping in plan under braking and turning forces. Depends on plan shape, laying pattern and above all on the **edge restraint**.

**Lock-up** is different and often confused with interlock. Under traffic, block pavements progressively stiffen as joints fill with detritus, sand densifies and blocks rotate into contact, until a quasi-equilibrium is reached beyond which no further deformation occurs. The stiffness increase can be substantial. Lock-up is contingent on construction quality and on the layerworks: a subbase of low bearing value prevents interlock developing in the early life of the pavement. Where conditions are favourable, lock-up develops gradually under traffic, or can be induced by proof-rolling.

The practical consequence: block pavements deflect a lot (up to 2 mm or more under truck traffic — deflections that would crack an asphalt surfacing) but accumulate less permanent deformation than asphalt of similar thickness, and the block layer's load-spreading means the unbound granular subbase under a block pavement is normally *thinner* than under an asphalt-surfaced pavement.

## Block types by plan shape (SANS 1058 / SANS 1200 MJ)

| Type | Definition | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **S-A** | Geometric interlock between **all** vertical faces of adjacent blocks; resist joint spread by plan geometry; can be laid herringbone parallel to both longitudinal and transverse joint axes | Roads and heavy-duty pavements; recommended for **all industrial and heavy-duty applications** — best resistance to both vertical and horizontal creep |
| **S-B** | Geometric interlock on **some** faces; resist joint spread parallel to the blocks' longitudinal axes | Chosen mainly for aesthetics; acceptable for light duty |
| **S-C** | **No** geometric interlock between adjacent faces; relies entirely on dimensional accuracy and accuracy of laying | Rectangular pavers, clay-brick-shaped units; aesthetic choice, light duty |

## Thickness selection

| Thickness | Where |
|---|---|
| **50 mm** | Pedestrian areas, patios, garden paths, light domestic driveways; the cheapest option and adequate where no truck ever runs |
| **55 mm** | Common size for cobble-format products (Technicrete Concobble, Conleaf) |
| **60 mm** | The default for domestic driveways, residential streets, car parks and municipal sidewalks; the "specify this unless there's a reason not to" thickness |
| **80 mm** | Industrial pavements, heavy commercial, roads, bus stops, container yards, petrol forecourts |
| **> 80 mm** | Available for special applications, but "experience has shown that very little benefit is gained from pavers which are thicker than 80 mm" |

Thicker blocks resist both vertical deformation and horizontal creep better, and a thicker paver also develops more side friction. Blocks of 50, 55, 60 and 80 mm are readily available ex stock. Specifying unnecessarily thick blocks only increases cost.

## Laying patterns and their structural consequences

Three basic patterns:

- **Herringbone** (45° or 90° to the direction of traffic) — the only pattern with genuine structural merit. It "ensures the best resistance to both horizontal and vertical deformation and is generally recommended for industrial pavements". SANS 1200 MJ makes it the default: if the pattern is not specified, blocks "shall be laid in a herringbone pattern if the block shape permits". The 45° version orients no continuous joint line with the traffic direction, which is what resists creep. Its cost is cutting: every edge needs a cut block.
- **Stretcher (running) bond** — blocks laid in rows with staggered joints, long axis across the traffic direction. If herringbone is impossible, SANS 1200 MJ requires the long axis at right angles to the line of traffic. Cheap to lay, minimal cutting, but continuous joint lines parallel to traffic permit creep and tramlining.
- **Basketweave** — pairs of blocks alternating at 90°. Purely decorative; poorest structural performance of the three; acceptable for patios and pedestrian areas only.

The CMA states plainly: "pavements laid in herringbone bond do perform better under traffic loads than pavements laid in stretcher bond or basket weave." Clifford notes that the advantage of one bond over another is largely aesthetic *except* that laying joints at 45° to the principal direction of trafficking reduces the effects induced by traffic.

**Pattern at edges.** Where herringbone meets an edge restraint, reorient the pattern along the restraint (a soldier course) rather than cutting a longitudinal sliver on every block. This eliminates small cut pieces and makes the edge look deliberate. The gap between the blocks and the edge restraint must still meet the 2–6 mm joint tolerance. Cut units smaller than about **25 % of a full block** are not permitted — they dislodge under traffic.

## Edge restraints

Every block pavement must have an edge restraint. Its function is to retain the bedding sand and to stop the perimeter units creeping outward or rotating under load, which opens joints and destroys interlock across the whole field.

- Restraints must be built **before any blocks are laid**, positioned on the subbase, securely supported with an adequate concrete backing. They then double as the screeding datum: the screed board is cut to the required depth below the top of the restraint.
- Options: precast kerbs and channels to SANS 927 / SANS 1200 MK; cast in-situ concrete edge strips; pavers set on end in a concrete haunch (a "soldier" restraint); or an existing structure. Common kerb sections: half-battered, mountable, and rectangular/tapered channels.
- A **separate** edge restraint is unnecessary where the block paving abuts an existing concrete pavement, or an asphalt pavement whose surfacing is not less than 100 mm thick.
- On slopes, the restraint becomes an **anchor beam**. Anchor beams are required where the slope exceeds **12 %**, and at the engineer's discretion between **8 % and 12 %**. Indicative spacing of intermediate anchor beams: 12 % slope → 30 m; 15 % → 20 m; 20 % → 15 m. A typical beam is ~150 mm × 150 mm with 25 mm diameter weepholes at 1,0 m spacing. Cast the beam so its top is 7–10 mm below the surrounding blockwork to allow for settlement, and interlock it with the pavers rather than cutting small pieces.

## Bedding sand and jointing sand — the two sands are different

They must never be the same material.

**Bedding sand** — coarse, clean, non-plastic, free of silt and deleterious salts. Coarse river sand is the classic choice; crusher sand may be suitable but must be evaluated. Where several conforming sands are available, prefer **sharp, angular particles with a high internal angle of friction** — tests show these perform better under traffic. If the angle of shearing resistance is unknown, prefer crusher sands or dune sands over river sand. Mine sand is generally acceptable in mining areas; certain fine Cape Flats and Port Elizabeth sands are proven acceptable.

**Jointing sand** — finer plaster sand passing 1,18 mm with **10–50 % passing 0,075 mm**. It should ideally contain a little clay or silt: that helps seal the joints against water ingress. Where the bedding sand contains more than about 10 % clay, water infiltration produces substantial increases in deformation, so the clay belongs in the joint, not the bed.

Both sands must be free of soluble salts, which cause "halo efflorescence" — unsightly, and it can reduce the skid resistance of the pavement.

## Layerworks under the blocks

- The **subgrade** is prepared and compacted, and preparation must extend to the rear face of all new edge restraints.
- A **subbase** is required where the subgrade is of low strength (usually CBR < 15) or the traffic is heavy; the subgrade may alternatively be stabilised. Because the blocks perform a structural function, layerworks requirements are less onerous than for asphalt, and **in many cases the subbase can be eliminated** on good ground under light traffic.
- Depressions must be filled with **subbase material** compacted to 98 % modified AASHTO — never with bedding sand. Any shallow depression needs the subbase scabbled to give a minimum 50 mm fill. Depressions deeper than 50 mm in a stabilised subbase may be filled with concrete of at least 5 MPa at 7 days.
- Cement treatment: typically CEM I 42,5, CEM II/A-S/V/W 32,5 or higher, CEM II/B-S/V/W 32,5, CEM III/A 32,5, or a 50/50 blend of CEM I 42,5 with milled granulated slag (SANS 1491-1) or fly ash (SANS 1491-2). Proportion determined by laboratory test.
- A representative UTG 2 catalogue entry for a residential-category road in a wet climatic region: 60 mm S-A/S-B/S-C blocks + 20 mm sand over 150 mm G5 or 125–150 mm C4, depending on traffic class E0 to E4 (subgrade CBR minimum 10 % or 15 %). At E3–E4 the blocks step to 60–80 mm S-A and 80 mm S-A respectively.

## Installation method, step by step

1. **Set out and square up.** Work from one point only. Align the pattern with the roadway direction or parallel to a wall or edge restraint. Because the kerb line itself may not be true, set **two string lines at right angles** and check alignment regularly.
2. **Confirm the layerworks.** The paviour should check that earthworks levels conform to the drawings before starting; the main contractor remains responsible for the strength, line and level of the layerworks. Note that the standard SANS 1200 tolerance on subgrade and subbase is *not* tight enough for good paving — the engineer must amend it (SANS 1200 MJ table 6.2).
3. **Install edge restraints** and let the concrete backing gain strength.
4. **Determine the surcharge.** Lay a test area on 30 mm of loose sand and compact with three passes of the plate vibrator to be used on the job. Measure the settlement; that gives the loose depth needed to achieve 25 mm compacted. Recheck whenever sand type or moisture changes.
5. **Screed the bedding sand** loose and even, at 6 ± 2 % moisture, only as far ahead as can be paved that day. Protect stockpiles from drying and wetting under plastic. If the bed is accidentally compacted, rake and re-screed loose.
6. **Lay the blocks.** Start in a right-angled corner and work outwards in both directions on a **diagonal laying face** — this is the productive geometry. On a slope, **lay uphill**, so the crew's movement consolidates joints rather than opening them. Over undulating ground, start at the low point of the dip and work outward in both directions. Place blocks with the two-handed method, holding firmly against the units already laid; bounce them off their neighbours to approximate the 3 mm gap. Spacer nibs are a guide, not a substitute for judgement. Whole units first; closures later.
7. **Adjust and line up.** Check squareness with a string line as each area is completed and lever sections back into line **before** compaction and jointing. After joint filling this is effectively impossible.
8. **Cut closures.** Measure, mark with a chalk line, cut with a hydraulic/mechanical guillotine (block splitter) or a power saw, tap into place. Defer cutting until enough work is complete for a continuous cutting operation. Avoid infill concrete; where unavoidable it must be full depth, at least 15 MPa at 24 hours with 9,5 mm maximum aggregate, cured 24 hours under moist sand or plastic, and no compaction within 1 m for 24 hours or until 15 MPa is reached.
9. **Compact.** At least two passes of the plate compactor before any jointing sand. Plate compactor requirements: for units **≤ 80 mm**, 7–16 kN centrifugal force, 65–100 Hz, plate area 0,2–0,4 m² (covering ≥ 10 units); for units **> 80 mm**, 16–20 kN, 65–100 Hz, plate area 0,35–0,5 m² (covering ≥ 12 units). Rollers must be light (2–4 t) vibratory or heavy pneumatic-tyred; **never** a heavy steel roller, which breaks blocks. Replace damaged units before joint filling. Install a temporary restraint against any free edge before compacting.
10. **Fill the joints.** Only after all closures are in, levels adjusted and at least two compaction passes made. Broom (do not wash) dry jointing sand into the joints until full; run further compactor passes to settle it; repeat until the joints stay full. Both the sand and the blocks should be as dry as possible — damp sand bridges narrow joints and resists compaction invisibly.
11. **Lock up.** Where wheel loads exceed 30 kN, finish with at least five passes of a heavy pneumatic-tyred roller over the whole area. Heavy-duty plate compactors for this stage weigh 300–600 kg with 0,5–0,6 m² plates and 30–65 kN centrifugal force.
12. **Clean off and hand over.** Broom off excess sand. **No vehicular traffic until all joints are filled.** Top up joints at about three months.

## Quality control on the finished work

A **trial section** — at least 20 m long and about 6 m wide, starting from a permanent edge restraint, laid as part of the permanent work — is used to assess the contractor's ability. Full-scale laying does not start until it is approved. (Not mandatory for experienced contractors on small jobs; strongly recommended on labour-based training projects, where it should be on a minor road.)

Permissible deviations (Degree of Accuracy I), measured 3 months after opening to traffic:

| Item | Tolerance |
|---|---|
| Unit length / width | ±2 mm |
| Unit thickness | ±3 mm |
| Unit squareness | ±2 mm |
| Top of subbase from designated level | ±10 mm |
| Smoothness of subbase on a 3 m straightedge | ±10 mm |
| Thickness of compacted sand bedding | ±10 mm |
| Line of pattern, deviation from a 3 m straight line | 10 mm max |
| Line of pattern, deviation from a 20 m straight line | 20 mm max |
| Vertical deviation from 3 m straightedge, at kerbs/channels/gullies/manholes/edge restraints | +3, −0 mm |
| Vertical deviation from 3 m straightedge, elsewhere | +10, −15 mm |
| Difference in surface level between adjacent units | 3 mm |
| Finished surface level from designated level | +10, −15 mm |

Note that the permissible deviation on the top of the subbase is the **same** as on the finished paving — proof that the bedding sand cannot be used to correct subbase inaccuracy. Smoothness checks: for roads, per SANS 1200 M 6.3; elsewhere, one check per 300 m² maximum. If the engineer suspects ponding, the surface may be flooded; the contractor rectifies at his own cost if ponding occurs, the employer pays if it does not. Tolerances on the units themselves and other SANS 1058 requirements must never be relaxed.

## Maintenance

Jointing sand top-up (three months, then as needed); weed control; abrasion is normally negligible on Class 25 blocks; settlement is corrected by lifting, correcting the bed and relaying the same blocks; trench reinstatement is straightforward and is one of block paving's structural advantages; efflorescence is temporary (see `05_paving-manufacture.md`); oil stains are treated locally or by replacing individual units.
