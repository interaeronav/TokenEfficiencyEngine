# Paving and roads — domain map for southern Africa and Namibia

**Summary.** "Paving" in southern Africa covers three activities that share a vocabulary but almost nothing else: residential hard landscaping (a driveway, a patio, a pool surround — typically 50–60 mm concrete blocks on 25 mm of bedding sand over a thin subbase, laid by a small team with a plate compactor); access roads and yards (60–80 mm blocks or a light bitumen seal over a proper subbase, designed to a traffic class); and engineered highway pavements (a designed multi-layer flexible, rigid or block pavement carrying millions of equivalent standard axles, specified to the TRH/COTO documents and built by a civil contractor with a laboratory). Confusing them is the single most expensive error in the domain — a driveway built to highway rules costs three times what it should, and an access road built to driveway rules fails in one wet season. This folder maps the engineering, the standards, the materials, the manufacturers, the trades and the Namibian context, with the residential block-paving case treated in most depth because that is the live work package in Okongo, Ohangwena Region.

## Key facts

| | Residential hard landscaping | Access road / yard | Engineered pavement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical design traffic | < 0,003 × 10⁶ E80 (a few cars) | 0,003–0,8 × 10⁶ E80 | 0,8–100 × 10⁶ E80 |
| Design basis | Rule of thumb, supplier detail | Catalogue (UTG2 / TRH4 category D–C) | Catalogue + mechanistic check (SAMDM) |
| Block thickness | 50–60 mm | 60–80 mm | 80 mm S-A only |
| Subbase | 0–100 mm G5 or none on good ground | 100–150 mm G5/C4 | 150 mm C4 + selected layers |
| Bedding sand | 25 ± 10 mm compacted | 25 ± 10 mm compacted | 25 ± 10 mm compacted |
| Who designs it | Nobody, or the landscaper | Technician or engineer | Registered civil engineer |
| Who signs it off | Owner | Client's agent | Engineer, with test results |
| Governing document | CMA Books 1–5, SANS 1058 | UTG 2, SANS 1200 MJ | TRH 4, TRH 14, COTO 2020 |

> ⚠️ Bedding sand is **never** a levelling layer. The single most common failure in residential paving in southern Africa is using 60–80 mm of sand to bury an uneven subbase. The CMA specification is unambiguous: 25 mm ± 10 mm compacted, and depressions in the subbase must be filled with subbase material compacted to 98 % modified AASHTO, never with sand.

## The three worlds of paving

### 1. Residential hard landscaping
Driveways, parking aprons, walkways, patios, pool surrounds, courtyards, entrance thresholds. The loads are light (a bakkie at 15–20 kN per wheel is the design case, occasionally a delivery truck or a ready-mix truck during construction). The failure modes are aesthetic before they are structural: dishing, tramlining along wheel paths, joint sand washout, weed growth, efflorescence, edge blocks creeping outward because there is no edge restraint. Cost is dominated by the block and by labour, not by layerworks. Treated in `09_residential-hard-landscaping.md` and, for the block itself, `03_concrete-block-paving.md`.

### 2. Access roads, estate roads and industrial yards
The middle ground: a farm access, a lodge entrance, a township street, a workshop yard, a fuel forecourt. Here the pavement is a real structure and the layerworks matter more than the surfacing. Design is done by catalogue — pick the traffic class and subgrade CBR, read the layer thicknesses off a table. **[ZA]** and **[NA]** both use UTG 2 (for segmental block pavements) and TRH 4 (for flexible pavements). Concrete block paving is unusually competitive in this band because the blocks themselves are a load-bearing course: the CMA notes that a block pavement's unbound granular subbase is normally thinner than the equivalent asphalt pavement's.

### 3. Engineered highway pavements
Trunk, main and district roads, freeways, ports, airfields. Multi-layer flexible pavements with cemented or granular bases, surfaced with a seal or asphalt; or continuously reinforced concrete; or, at ports and heavy intersections, 80 mm block paving. Designed with a catalogue plus a mechanistic-empirical check, built to COTO/SANS 1200 specifications with density and strength testing at defined frequencies, and managed with a pavement management system over a 20–30 year life. In Namibia this is the Roads Authority's world.

## Where Namibia differs **[NA]**

- Namibia has **no national building act** and no national pavement standard of its own for private work. The Roads Authority (established under the **Roads Authority Act 17 of 1999**) manages the national road network — every trunk, main and district road proclaimed under Chapter III of the Roads Ordinance — and issues its own **Road Maintenance / Design Manual set dated October 2014**: Construction, Drainage, Economic, Environmental, Geometrics, Materials, Procedures, Standard Drawings, Structures and Survey manuals, plus a Bridge Routine Maintenance Manual (March 2003). These are listed on the RA downloads page but the attachment links resolve to an internal server and were not publicly downloadable on the access date.
- For everything the RA manuals do not cover, Namibian practice defaults to the South African TRH/TMH/COTO/SANS stack. This is documented in the road-safety literature: Namibian rural road classes are assigned using **TRH 26 (Road Classification and Access Management, CSRA 1988)** and geometry is checked against **TRH 17 (Geometric Design of Rural Roads, CSRA 1988)**.
- The network is 48 875 km (2017): ~6 664 km standard bitumen, 412 km low-volume bitumen, 25 710 km gravel, 11 460 km earth-graded and 288 km **salt roads** — a coastal construction using concentrated salt water and gypsum-rich material that exists essentially nowhere else at scale.
- Materials are the constraint, not labour or standards. Crushed stone is expensive and far away; calcrete, Kalahari sand and gypseous gravels are what is actually available. Long-term Namibian road experiments (Lüderitz and Haalenberg, 1976–2012) exist precisely because the local materials break the South African specifications.

## What this means for a residential job in Okongo

Okongo lies about 120 km east of Eenhana on the tarred road to Nkurenkuru, in Ohangwena Region (337 729 people over 10 703 km², 85,5 % rural, 2023 census). Practical consequences that run through every file in this folder:

1. **Haul distance dominates cost.** Concrete blocks are 2,4–2,6 kg each and roughly 100–130 kg/m² for a 50 mm block, 150–200 kg/m² at 80 mm. Trucking from Windhoek (~700 km) or Ondangwa (~200 km) can exceed the ex-works block price. Local manufacture — a small block machine on site — becomes rational at surprisingly small quantities; see `05_paving-manufacture.md`.
2. **Subgrade is Kalahari/Cuvelai sand, not gravel.** Deep uniform sands drain well but have low soaked CBR and no cohesion; the design question is not drainage but whether the subbase can develop the confinement the blocks need. Sand-only subgrades typically need an imported or stabilised subbase even at light traffic.
3. **Rainy season is the design event.** The far north gets its rain in a short, intense December–March season, and the Cuvelai drainage system floods shallow oshanas. Falls, edge restraints and stormwater outfalls matter more here than layer thickness.
4. **There is no building control.** On communal land there is no plan approval, no building inspector and no NHBRC equivalent. Quality is entirely a matter of specification and inspection by the builder — which is why `10_specifying-and-costing-paving.md` carries a full specification template.

## Vocabulary that trips agents up

- **Paver / paving block / paving brick** — a concrete or clay unit ≤ 300 mm, laid on sand, joints filled with sand. Not a "slab" (large-format, laid on mortar or sand, minimal interlock) and not a "flag".
- **Segmental paving / CBP** — the engineering term for block paving; SANS 1200 MJ calls it "segmented paving".
- **Layerworks / earthworks** — everything below the bedding sand. Usually a different contractor from the paviour, and the source of most disputes.
- **Kerb vs edge restraint** — a kerb is one kind of edge restraint; a concrete haunch, an anchor beam or an existing slab can also serve.
- **E80** — one equivalent standard 80 kN axle. The traffic unit for all southern African pavement design.
- **Screeding** — striking the loose bedding sand to a uniform depth with a board running on rails or the edge restraints. Never done with a "float".
