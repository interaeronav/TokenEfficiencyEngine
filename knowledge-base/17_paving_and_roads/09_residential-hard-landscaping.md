# Residential hard landscaping — driveways, paths, patios, pool surrounds and stormwater

**Summary.** Residential hard landscaping is where paving decisions are made by people who will walk on the result barefoot every day for twenty years, and where the loads are trivial but the geometry, the falls and the edges are everything. The technical rules are few and non-negotiable: a minimum 2 % fall in every direction; 25 ± 10 mm of bedding sand and never more; an edge restraint on every free edge; herringbone where vehicles turn; and drainage designed before the first block is ordered. The rest is geometry — how big a car needs to turn, how wide a path must be for two people, how far a pool coping should overhang — and comfort: how hot a dark paver gets, and how slippery a wet one is.

## Driveways

**Structure.** For a domestic driveway carrying cars and the occasional delivery vehicle, 60 mm concrete blocks in herringbone bond on 25 mm of compacted bedding sand is the default. 50 mm is acceptable where nothing heavier than a car will ever use it and the subgrade is good; 80 mm is warranted where a truck will regularly stand or turn. On sandy subgrades such as those in the Cuvelai/Kalahari north, a subbase is required: 100–150 mm of G5 natural gravel, or a cement-stabilised C4 layer where good gravel is not available. Where the drive will be used by ready-mix and delivery trucks *during construction*, design for that load, not for the finished use — construction traffic destroys more residential paving than twenty years of family cars.

**Falls and shape.** Fall the driveway away from the house and away from the garage threshold. Where a driveway must fall towards a building, provide a channel and a grated drain across the full width with an outfall you have actually designed. A single crossfall is easier to build and better looking than a crown on a drive narrower than about 5 m.

**Gradients and transitions.** Long straight runs at a constant grade are comfortable; the failures are at the changes. Where a steep drive meets a level apron or the street, provide a **vertical transition** — a short length of intermediate grade — or the car will ground on its nose or tail. As a rule of thumb, limit the change of grade at any single break to about 6–8 % and use a 2–3 m transition length. `needs-verification`. Above 12 % slope the paving itself needs anchor beams (see Key facts).

**Turning.** A vehicle that must reverse into the road is a safety problem; a turning area lets it leave forward. The minimum useful turning space is a hammerhead or a T-bay sized on the vehicle's turning circle. Where turning happens, use **herringbone** and **S-A type blocks** — turning generates exactly the horizontal shear that herringbone resists and stretcher bond does not.

**Transition from the road / crossover.** In a proclaimed town the crossover (the section between the property boundary and the road channel) is usually the local authority's asset, requires a permit, and must be built to the authority's standard detail — often a concrete slab or an authority-specified kerb drop. **[NA]** In an unproclaimed area there is no such requirement, but the engineering still applies: the crossover carries the steepest change of grade and the highest loads on the whole drive, is where the road's stormwater runs, and is where a bakkie mounts the kerb. Build it thicker (80 mm blocks or a 150 mm concrete slab), with a proper edge restraint on both sides, and design where the road's runoff goes before you put a paved surface across its path.

**Edge restraint on a driveway** is not optional. The most common residential paving failure is the outer row of a driveway creeping outward into the garden until the joints open and the field unravels. Use a concrete edge beam haunched into the subbase, a precast kerb to SANS 927, or a soldier course of pavers bedded on and haunched with concrete.

## Parking bays and hardstanding

Parking bays are simple to build and easy to get wrong dimensionally. Design for the actual vehicle: a double-cab bakkie is around 5,4 m long and 2,0 m wide, so a 5,0 m bay leaves it overhanging. Provide the bay, the door-opening clearance and the aisle as one geometry, not three separate decisions.

A parked vehicle is a **static point load in the same place every day**, which is why parking bays rut and driveways do not. Where vehicles stand permanently, use 80 mm blocks or accept periodic relaying of the wheel positions. Drip-tray areas (under engines) should be a material you can clean or replace — one more argument for block paving, where you lift and swap the stained units.

## Pedestrian paths

- Width by function: 0,9–1,0 m for a single person, 1,2–1,5 m where two people walk abreast or where a wheelbarrow or wheelchair must pass.
- Falls: 2 % crossfall to one side, never a crown on a path narrower than 1,5 m. Longitudinal gradient under 5 % is comfortable; above 8 % consider steps.
- Structure: 50 mm blocks on 25 mm sand directly on a compacted, well-drained subgrade is sufficient. No subbase is required for foot traffic on sound ground.
- Edge restraint on both sides, always — a path has two free edges and no adjacent paving to confine it.
- Pattern: stretcher bond or a running bond along the path reads better than herringbone and is cheaper because there are fewer cuts; there is no structural objection at pedestrian loads.
- **Do not run a path across the line of surface flow without a crossing detail.** A path that dams sheet flow creates a pond on its uphill side.

## Patios and terraces

- Patios are the place for the large-format and wet-cast products (flagstones, large concrete slabs, cast stone) because there is no traffic to demand interlock. SmartStone and Revelstone exist for exactly this market.
- Fall away from the building at 2 % minimum, and set the finished paving at least **150 mm below the damp-proof course** of the adjacent wall, with a drainage channel where that is impossible. `needs-verification` for the DPC dimension against the applicable building regulation.
- Thermal comfort is a real design criterion in northern Namibia. Pale, matt, textured surfaces stay dramatically cooler underfoot than dark, dense, smooth ones. Specify light greys, buffs and pale sandstones for any surface people will cross barefoot, and reserve charcoals and plums for driveways and edges.
- Shade planning is part of the paving specification: a paved terrace with no shade is unusable for six months of the year, and it re-radiates heat into the adjacent rooms at night.

## Pool surrounds and slip resistance

- **Slip resistance is the governing criterion.** Never specify polished, honed, or smooth-faced units around a pool. Suitable: textured or shot-blasted concrete pavers, riven sandstone or slate, exposed aggregate concrete, and purpose-made non-slip coping.
- **Coping** should overhang the pool edge by a small, consistent amount (typically 25–40 mm) with a bullnose or rounded arris; the overhang creates a shadow line, a grip point for swimmers, and stops water tracking back under the coping. `needs-verification` for the overhang dimension.
- **Falls away from the pool**, into a channel or into planting, not into the water — the pool's chemistry is the reason. A 2 % fall away from the coping over the first 1,5 m is the usual arrangement.
- Chlorinated splash-out attacks jointing sand and some stones. Use a stabilised jointing sand or a proprietary joint stabiliser in the splash zone, or accept an annual top-up.
- Heat: this is where dark stone becomes a genuine burn hazard in a Namibian summer. Pale, matt, thermally reflective materials only.
- Set the coping level and the surround falls **before** the paving is ordered — a pool surround that ponds is the most visible defect in a garden.

## Stormwater management and permeable design

Block paving is not waterproof: 30–35 % of rainfall penetrates newly laid, untrafficked, unsealed block paving (the figure drops as joints clog with detritus). That has two implications: the layerworks must be able to drain, and a paved area is not a "hard" catchment in the way concrete is.

**Conventional (impermeable) approach:** falls to a channel, gully or kerb inlet; the water leaves via a piped or open system to a soakaway, a swale, or a legal discharge point. Detail requirements from the CMA: minimum 1 % longitudinal and 2 % crossfall; paving set minimum 5 mm higher at drains, gutters and manhole inlets; concrete infill collar at pits and manholes minimum 100 mm thick, 30 MPa; chamfer or rumbled arris; and drainage of the bedding sand layer itself where the paving sits on a concrete slab.

**Permeable approach (PICP):** wider joints filled with **angular aggregate, not sand**, over an open-graded permeable subbase designed to store water. Three configurations: **System A — full infiltration** (all water into the subgrade; needs good subgrade permeability); **System B — partial infiltration** (some infiltrates, the rest collected by outlet pipes connected to the permeable subbase and released at a controlled rate); **System C — no infiltration** (fully tanked with an impermeable membrane up the sides of the subbase; used where subgrade permeability is poor). Initial infiltration through the joints is around 4 000 mm/hour; design at 10 % of that to allow for clogging, which still leaves a very large safety factor. PICP also treats the water: 60–95 % of suspended solids, 70–90 % of hydrocarbons, 50–80 % of total phosphorus, 65–80 % of total nitrogen and 60–95 % of heavy metals. Roof runoff and adjacent impermeable surfaces can be directed onto a PICP area — a common and effective residential arrangement is 75 % conventional paving falling onto 25 % permeable paving.

Limits: PICP costs roughly a third more than conventional paving; it needs periodic suction cleaning of the joints; it is inappropriate at fuelling or vehicle-maintenance areas; and it is generally unsuitable where surrounding land draining onto it exceeds 20 % slope.

**In a sandy northern Namibian setting**, full infiltration (System A) is usually the right answer — the subgrade is highly permeable — and the real design question is not where the water goes but how to stop concentrated flows from scouring unpaved areas at the paving's edge.

**On slopes**, water travelling down the bedding sand layer accumulates at each anchor beam; provide 25 mm diameter weepholes at 1,0 m spacing through the beam, and control surface flow, which on steep paving can reach velocities high enough to erode the jointing sand and destroy the pavement's integrity.

Typical dimensions shown include 300 mm high × 180 mm and 250/300 × 125/150 mm sections with 20 mm radii; channels are typically 125 × 250 mm or 90 × 125 × 300 mm.

Residential edging options in order of cost:
1. **Soldier course of pavers** set on and haunched with concrete — cheapest, matches the field, needs a proper haunch or it fails.
2. **Precast concrete edging strip** (rectangular or half-round) — clean, quick, defines a lawn edge well.
3. **Precast kerb** (half-battered where you want to stop a wheel, mountable where you want to allow one) — the correct choice at a driveway edge that vehicles will overrun.
4. **Cast in-situ concrete edge beam** — the strongest and the most flexible for curves; the standard answer on a curved driveway edge.
5. **Steel or aluminium landscape edging** — for garden paths only; it has no structural capacity and will not restrain a vehicle-trafficked field.

All of them must be **built before the blocks are laid**, founded on the subbase (not on soil), with an adequate concrete backing, and used as the screed datum.

## Planting integration

- Keep tree pits out of trafficked paving where possible; where unavoidable, use a structural cell or a generous pit with an edge restraint around it, because root heave is the one thing block paving does *not* tolerate gracefully. (Its saving grace: heaved block paving can be lifted, the roots dealt with, and the same blocks relaid.)
- Do not run paving hard up to a lawn without an edging: mower wheels break the arrises and grass invades the joints.
- Beds adjacent to paving should sit **below** the paving level so mulch and soil do not wash across it.
- Irrigation over paving accelerates efflorescence and grows weeds and algae in the joints; keep spray patterns off the paving.
- Grass or ground cover in the joints (or a grass-through product such as Armorflex HLP or a turf paver) is a legitimate design choice for overflow parking and fire access, but requires irrigation to survive a Namibian dry season.

## Lighting

- Light the **changes**: steps, level changes, the transition from drive to path, the pool edge, and the gate.
- Low-level, downward-directed fittings (bollards, step lights, recessed wall washers) light paving better than pole-mounted floods, which flatten the texture and glare.
- Grazing light across a textured paving surface reveals it; flat frontal light makes it look like a bad photograph. This matters for exposed aggregate, split and tumbled products, where the texture is the design.
- Coordinate cable routes with the layerworks. Ducting must be laid and the trench backfilled with approved granular material compacted to subgrade level **before** subbase construction — trench settlement under paving is one of the classic defects (see `07_paving-contractors-and-craft.md`).
- Recessed in-ground fittings in paving need a drained housing and a full-depth surround; they are a permanent maintenance obligation.

## Setting-out geometry

- Establish a single origin for the whole scheme and a pair of perpendicular baselines. Every area is set out from those, not from each other.
- Set out **hard geometry first**: building lines, kerb lines, the pool coping, the garage threshold. These are fixed; the paving fits them.
- **Do not trust the building or the boundary to be square.** Check diagonals. Where the building is out of square, decide which line the eye will read — usually the longest visible edge or the line of the house wall — and align the pattern to that, absorbing the error in a cut course at the least visible edge.
- Set out curves with a centre pin and a tape, or with a garden hose laid to the line and then surveyed. Free-form curves look accidental unless they are made of arcs of a small number of radii.
- Circles and fans: buy a proprietary kit or budget for heavy sawing.
- Mark levels on pegs or on the building, not on the ground. Record the top-of-subbase levels in writing before the sand goes down.
- Plan **where the cuts fall** at setting-out stage, not at laying stage. If the field width is an awkward multiple of the block width, shift the origin so the cut lands at the least visible edge, or introduce a deliberate soldier course.
