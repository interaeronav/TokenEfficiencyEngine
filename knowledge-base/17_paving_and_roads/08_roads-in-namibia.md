# Roads in Namibia — network, institutions, gravel practice and the northern regions

**Summary.** Namibia has one of the best-regarded road networks on the continent — 48 875 km in 2017, ranked first in Africa on the quality-of-road-infrastructure index at 5,57 out of 7 in 2024 — built and maintained by a three-part institutional structure created by statute in 1999: the **Roads Authority** manages the network, the **Road Fund Administration** raises and allocates road user charges, and the **Roads Contractor Company** executes work. But 76 % of that network is gravel or earth, and the quality reputation belongs to the paved trunk routes. In the far north, where rural access roads are unpaved and the rainy season is short and intense, the practical experience of the network is very different. This file covers what the network is, who runs it, how gravel roads are actually built and maintained, the coastal salt roads, and what all of it means for a builder hauling materials to Okongo.

## The institutions

Namibia's 1999 road-sector reform separated three functions that are usually conflated:

**Roads Authority (RA)** — Roads Authority Act 17 of 1999. A juristic person established "to manage the national road network of Namibia". Functions: planning, design, construction and maintenance of the national road network, quality control, and operation of road management systems, under a board of directors with a CEO. Funded from Road Fund allocations, parliamentary appropriations, donations and other sources, with annual audit and ministerial performance oversight through performance statements and procedures agreements. Head office: corner of Mandume Ndemufayo Avenue and David Hosea Meroro Road, Southern Industrial Area, Windhoek; +264 61 284 7000; pr@ra.org.na. Also operates NaTIS (vehicle registration and licensing), abnormal-load, cross-border and domestic transport permits, and the weighbridge system.

**Road Fund Administration (RFA)** — Road Fund Administration Act 18 of 1999. A juristic person overseeing the road user charging system to secure funding for road expenditure while promoting safe and efficient infrastructure. It runs the Road Fund itself, sets and collects road user charges, and allocates funding. Amended by the Public Enterprises Governance Act, 2006. In practice the RFA collects a fuel levy, vehicle licence fees, mass-distance charges on heavy vehicles, cross-border charges and abnormal-load fees — *the specific charge instruments and current rates are* **needs-verification**, as the RFA website was unreachable during compilation.

**Roads Contractor Company (RCC)** — the commercial state-owned road contractor, over 20 years old, head office on Marien Ngouabi Street, Windhoek, regional offices in **Otjiwarongo** and **Keetmanshoop**, plus 8 satellite locations. Operating units:
- **Grading Unit (GU)** — maintenance of unpaved roads
- **Re-Gravelling Unit (GRU)** — restoring gravel wearing courses
- **Bitumen Unit (BMU)** — bituminous surfacing
- Earth dam construction and rehabilitation
- Low-volume seal roads
- Railway embankment upgrading

RCC projects revenue of N$500 million+ by 2028/29, aligned to NDP5 and Vision 2030.

**Road management systems.** The RA maintains a Road Management System (RMS) in which all proclamations and network data are deposited; network map series are published in versions ("Network 6", "Network 7", "Road Summary Network 11"). A Bridge Management System was created after the first-ever visual assessment of the country's bridge stock in 2008.

## Road classification **[NA]**

Two classification systems coexist and are often confused.

**Legal/administrative classification** — under the **Roads Ordinance**, roads are proclaimed as **trunk roads, main roads and district roads**; together these constitute the national road network managed by the Roads Authority under the RA Act. **Farm roads** and access tracks fall outside that proclaimed network. Route numbering follows an alphanumeric convention: **A** for the single freeway section (A1, Windhoek–Okahandja, 74 km), **B** for the primary trunk routes (B1 at 1 467 km is the north–south spine; B8 at 929 km runs to the north-east; B14 at 389 km), **C** for secondary/main roads, **D** for district roads, and **M** for certain main routes. The tarred road serving Okongo runs east from Eenhana toward Nkurenkuru.

**Functional classification for design and safety analysis** — Namibian rural roads are assigned to functional classes using the South African **TRH 26 (Road Classification and Access Management, CSRA, 1988)**:

| Group | Class | Description |
|---|---|---|
| High Order Rural Roads | R1 | Rural principal arterial |
| | R2 | Rural major arterial |
| | R3 | Rural minor arterial |
| Low Order Rural Roads | R4 | Rural collector road |
| | R5 | Rural local road |
| | R6 | Rural walkway |

Design compliance is assessed against **TRH 17 (Geometric Design of Rural Roads, CSRA, 1988)**. Research on Namibian crash data found lane width and ground shoulder width to be the geometric covariates most strongly associated with fatal and serious injury crashes on high-order rural roads — with the counter-intuitive finding that wider paved shoulders combined with wider lanes create perceived space that raises speed selection and in-lane deviation.

## Salt roads — the Namibian speciality

288 km of Namibia's network is **salt road**: a wearing course built from concentrated salt water (brine) and gypsum-rich material, found only near the Atlantic coast within the **mist belt**. The mechanism is that the hygroscopic salts draw moisture from the nightly coastal fog, keeping the surface damp, cohesive and dust-free, producing a hard, dark, almost bituminous-looking running surface at a fraction of the cost of a seal. Outside the fog belt the same construction dries out, disintegrates and blows away, which is why the technique is essentially confined to the coastal strip between Swakopmund, Walvis Bay and Henties Bay.

The underlying materials science is unusually well documented for Namibia because the local materials break the standard specifications. Netterberg's **Lüderitz and Haalenberg long-term road experiments**, built in 1976 on Trunk Road 4/2 between Aus and Lüderitz and monitored to 2012, tested G3 crushed-stone and G4 calcrete bases with 2–20 % added gypsum and up to 2 % added NaCl, and sections compacted with **seawater**. Findings that matter to any builder in arid Namibia:

- Gypcretes and gypseous soils occur mostly **within about 50 km of the coast** in Namibia.
- The original limit on gypsum was 1,0 % as sulfate (1,8 % gypsum), borrowed from concrete practice; it was later raised to 2,0 % sulfate (3,6 % gypsum).
- The experiments established that for a 30-year design life and at least 1,0 × 10⁶ E80 under a **19 mm Cape seal**, gypsum up to **10 % in a G3 crushed-stone base** and at least **5 % in a G4 calcrete base** is acceptable in this arid environment, and probably across the southern African arid and semi-arid zone.
- Highly soluble salts are a different problem: **more than about 0,2 % NaCl** causes surface disintegration of primed bases and blisters bituminous surfacings.
- Gypsum is reversibly unstable above 46 °C, with molar volume decreases of up to 39 % and increases of up to 63 % — temperatures routinely reached inside a Namibian pavement base.
- Climatic risk indices at the sites: Weinert N-value over 50 and Thornthwaite Moisture Index below −50 (extremely dry for pavement design); gross annual Class A pan evaporation about 3 000 mm — roughly **100 times the rainfall**, so the potential for salt concentration at the surface is enormous.

## Gravel road construction and maintenance

With 25 710 km of gravel and 11 460 km of earth road, gravel practice is the operational core of the Namibian network. The governing guideline is **TRH 20 (The Structural Design, Construction and Maintenance of Unpaved Roads, NITRR/CSIR, Pretoria)**.

**What a gravel road is.** A wearing course of imported unbound gravel over a prepared and shaped formation, cambered to shed water, with side drains and mitre drains. There is no binder holding it together beyond the cohesion of its own fines — which is why material selection is everything.

**Material specification.** Southern African unsealed-road specifications derive from a 1989 performance-related study of 110 gravel-road sections that correlated slipperiness, erosion, ravelling and corrugation with the material's **Grading Coefficient (GC)** and **Shrinkage Product (SP)**. The Tanzanian calibration of that work gives:

| Property | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Maximum size | ≤ 37,5 mm |
| Shrinkage Product (linear shrinkage × % passing 0,425 mm) | 120–400 |
| Grading Coefficient | 16–34 |
| CBR at 95 % MDD (BS heavy compaction) | ≥ 25 % |
| Field dry density | ≥ 95 % MDD |

Dust is acceptable most often when SP is held between 120 and 400. Material with GC below 16 is particularly erosion-prone.

**The four distress mechanisms:**
- **Dust** — driven by traffic volume and speed, road moisture, looseness, maintenance frequency and wind speed.
- **Ravelling** — loss of loose gravel from a deficiency of fine material and hence cohesion, poor particle size distribution, and inadequate compaction. Blending in fines and compacting at optimum moisture content reduces it.
- **Erosion** — loss to flowing water; resisted by shear strength, mitigated by controlling grade and crossfall and by minimising the length of the water flow path.
- **Gravel loss** — the cumulative product of the above plus traffic abrasion. Well-graded materials resist it better than materials deficient in either fines or coarse fraction, even when well compacted. A Plastic Factor above 500 reduces gravel loss.

**Two facts that change how you budget maintenance:**
1. Gravel roads compacted with only a nominal number of grid-roller passes can **lose up to 30 % of the constructed wearing-course thickness within a short period** simply to traffic compaction. Design the layer thicker than the "required" thickness or compact it properly.
2. Roughly **90 % of the total road network in sub-Saharan Africa is unsealed**, and gravel road management systems in the region are still largely ad hoc and condition-based rather than model-based. Regravelling cycles are therefore usually set by budget rather than by predicted gravel loss.

**Maintenance activities**, as the RCC organises them: routine blading (grading) to restore the camber and cut out corrugations; spot regravelling; full regravelling when the wearing course is exhausted; drainage clearing; and, where traffic justifies it, upgrading to a low-volume seal. The RA describes its programme as periodic maintenance including blading and gravelling of roads, clearing and developing newly proclaimed routes and existing tracks.

**Low-volume roads.** Namibia carries 412 km of low-volume bitumen road and the RCC has a dedicated low-volume seal capability. The foundational Namibian reference is **Dierks, K. (1992), "Technical Aspects for Appropriate Low-Volume Roads in Namibia", PhD thesis, Berlin / Namibia Ministry of Works, Transport & Communication** — the document that established the local case for lower-cost sealed standards using local materials. *Its content was not retrieved and is* **needs-verification**.

## The far north and the rainy-season problem

Ohangwena Region is 85,5 % rural, with major settlements strung along the good paved road running from the Angolan border down to Ondangwa and connecting to the Oshakati–Tsumeb trunk road. Away from those routes, access is unpaved. The published literature is direct about it: while Namibia's major highways are well maintained, "many rural roads, most of which are gravel, require upgrades and expansion", and "in northern Namibia, many remote areas contend with inadequate road infrastructure, restricting access to essential services, employment and economic opportunities". Farmers and small businesses face high transport costs and limited road access. The eastern part of the region has good grazing but "the shortage of water and poor communications render it uninhabitable at present".

**Why the rainy season is the design event.** The far north sits in the **Cuvelai drainage system** — a network of shallow, interlinked seasonal channels (*oshanas*) that flood from the Angolan highlands. Rain arrives concentrated in roughly December to March. The consequences for roads and for a construction programme:

- Gravel and earth roads become impassable or badly rutted; the standard consequence of gravel roads listed in the literature is "(1) Impassable during the rainy season".
- Culvert and low-point crossings on secondary routes can be under water for days.
- Borrow pits flood and gravel cannot be won or hauled.
- Compaction of layerworks becomes impossible above optimum moisture content, and the completed layers soften.
- Deliveries slip; a truck that cannot reach site is a programme risk, not a logistics inconvenience.

**Practical scheduling rule:** all earthworks, layerworks and material haulage for a northern Namibian project should be completed in the **dry season (roughly April to November)**, with paving and finishing able to continue into the wet if materials are already stockpiled on site.

## Practical implications for a builder hauling materials

1. **Plan the route on paved roads for as long as possible.** Okongo is on a tarred road; the vulnerability is the last few kilometres of local access, not the trunk route.
2. **Know the axle-load regime.** The RA operates weighbridges and requires, at weighing: driver's licence with Professional Authorisation (PA), consignment note, transportation permits, operator's card, entry fee charges and the Mass Distance Charge logbook. Overloaded delivery vehicles are a real financial exposure, and mass-distance charges are a per-tonne-kilometre cost on heavy vehicles.
3. **Permits.** Abnormal load, cross-border and domestic transport permits are issued by the RA (online services at online.ra.org.na). If any plant is being brought in from South Africa, allow lead time.
4. **Weight is the cost driver.** A 50 mm concrete paver is about 100–130 kg/m²; an 80 mm paver 150–200 kg/m². At 700 km from Windhoek, block haulage can equal or exceed the ex-works block price, and breakage in transit adds to it. Compare this directly against local manufacture (`05_paving-manufacture.md`) and against cement-only delivery (Ohorongo's plant at Otavi is much closer than Windhoek).
5. **Buy sand and aggregate locally if the quality permits.** Bedding sand should be sharp, angular, non-plastic and free of soluble salts. Kalahari/Cuvelai dune sand is fine, rounded and uniform — check the grading against SANS 1200 MJ 3.3(a) before assuming it will work; crusher sand from a local quarry is usually the better bedding material, and dune sand is explicitly *preferred over river sand* by the CMA where the angle of shearing resistance is unknown.
6. **Test for salts.** In arid Namibia, soluble salts in sand and gravel are a genuine risk: they cause halo efflorescence in paving and, in road bases, surface disintegration above about 0,2 %. TMH 1:1986 Methods A21T and A22T are the relevant tests.
7. **Aggregate quarrying is a recognised Namibian sector** and local quarries exist; a sectoral analysis of aggregate quarrying's contribution to sustainable development in Namibia has been published in the SATC literature. Where a local quarry exists, crushed stone for a subbase and crusher sand for bedding will beat anything hauled.
8. **Stockpile before the rains.** Cover bedding sand with plastic on arrival — moisture uniformity is a specification requirement (6 ± 2 %), not housekeeping.
