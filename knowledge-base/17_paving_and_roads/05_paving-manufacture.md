# How paving blocks are made — dry-cast production, machines, colour and small-plant economics

**Summary.** A concrete paving block is not cast concrete in the ordinary sense. It is a **zero-slump (dry-cast)** mix — barely damp, with a water/cement ratio around 0,30–0,38 — dropped into a steel mould on a steel or timber board, then compacted by simultaneous vibration and hydraulic pressure for a few seconds, and immediately demoulded while green. The whole cycle takes 12–25 seconds and produces 6–20 blocks. That process, not the mix design, determines the block: compaction energy sets the density, the density sets the strength and the abrasion resistance, and mould wear sets the dimensional tolerance. Understanding it explains why cheap blocks are cheap, why efflorescence happens, and what a small plant in northern Namibia would actually need to buy.

> ⚠️ "Class 25" means an **average** of 25 MPa with **no individual block below 20 MPa**. In practice the individual minimum, not the average, is what a plant fails on. Where quality control is poor, satisfying the individual-block requirement is, in the CMA's words, "virtually impossible" — which is the real reason small unregulated plants cannot claim SANS compliance.

## The dry-cast process, step by step

1. **Aggregate storage and proportioning.** Coarse aggregate (usually 6,7 mm or 9,5 mm crushed stone), crusher sand and/or fine sand, in separate bins, dosed by weight into a weigh hopper. Grading is designed to minimise voids, because in a dry-cast mix there is no paste to fill them — the aggregate skeleton *is* the block.
2. **Cement and pigment addition.** Cement dosed by weight; iron-oxide pigment dosed by weight or volumetrically as a percentage of cement mass.
3. **Mixing.** A **pan mixer** (counter-current or turbine) is standard for dry-cast, because it shears the barely-damp mix rather than tumbling it. PMSA's mixer range spans 25 ℓ to 6 500 ℓ and includes counter-current pan, turbine pan, laboratory, planetary, twin-shaft and refractory mixers. Moisture control is the critical variable: too dry and the block will not compact, too wet and it slumps on demoulding. Modern plants use microwave or conductance moisture probes with automatic water correction.
4. **Filling the mould.** A filling box (drawer) shuttles over the mould on the board and drops the mix in. Laser-controlled filling is offered on high-end machines for consistent fill.
5. **Compaction.** Vibration from a vibrating table beneath the board plus downward pressure from a tamper head above. Amplitude-controlled vibration is the modern refinement. Compaction energy and duration set the density and therefore the strength. This is where cheap blocks are made cheap.
6. **Face mix (if used).** A second, finer, richer, pigmented layer of typically 4–8 mm is added on top of the main mix and given a second short compaction. It gives a better wearing surface and better colour with far less pigment than colouring the whole block.
7. **Demoulding.** The mould rises, the tamper head holds the blocks down, and the green blocks are left standing on the board.
8. **Board handling and curing.** Boards are elevated into racks, moved by finger cars into curing chambers, held at controlled humidity and temperature (ambient, warm-moist or low-pressure steam) for typically 12–48 hours, then de-racked.
9. **Off-packing, cubing, ageing.** Blocks are off-packed, cubed and banded. Value-added finishing happens here: splitting, tumbling/ageing, grinding/polishing, shot-blasting — Besser's catalogue lists splitters, tumbling systems, stone-aging systems, grinders and polishers as separate machines.
10. **Stockpiling.** Blocks continue gaining strength. Because SANS 1058 measures strength **at despatch**, a plant can manage the age at which it releases stock to meet the specification.

## Face-mix versus through-mix

- **Through-mix (single layer):** one mix throughout. Colour must be dosed through the whole block, which is expensive with pigment, and the surface is only as good as the general mix.
- **Face-mix (dual layer):** a base layer optimised for strength and a topping layer optimised for surface quality, abrasion resistance and colour. Bosun describes exactly this trade-off on its Bevel Bond paver: "a topping layer designed for the best abrasion resistance on the surface and a base layer focused on the tensile and compressive strength." Masa's XL-R produces stones "with or without face mix layer", where the second concrete layer can have a different colour and composition; Masa also builds dedicated face-mix mixers (S 350–500) and multi-colour dosing systems.
- The common marketing claim that "colour right through" indicates superior quality is, as Bosun points out, false: what matters at the surface is abrasion resistance and colour permanence, both of which a properly designed face mix delivers better. What SANS does require is colour penetration ≥ 5 mm, which any competent face mix exceeds.

## Pigments and colour

- Pigments are synthetic or natural **iron oxides** — red (Fe₂O₃), yellow (FeOOH), black (Fe₃O₄) — plus chromium oxide green and cobalt blue for specials. Dosed as a percentage of cement mass; typical range 2–6 %, with diminishing returns above about 5 %. `needs-verification` for exact dosages.
- The CMA's basic pigment palette for South African pavers is **black, brown, red and tan**; the standard colour range sold is grey, charcoal, red, tan and brown, with **multiblends** produced by deliberately incomplete pigment mixing to give a mottled effect over large areas.
- Non-pigmented blocks vary in colour anyway, because cement and aggregate colour vary between deliveries. **Keep approved reference samples** — SANS 1200 MJ requires the purchaser to hold agreed samples showing the accepted colour range.
- Progressive **mould wear** increases the plan area of units within tolerance over the life of a mould. On large works, either stockpile matching deliveries, or separate sections of pavement with an edge restraint or a contrasting band (e.g. a row of rectangular units) so that a size step is not visible.
- Fading: conventional pigmented dry-cast concrete lightens under UV, rain and traffic. Bosun markets a "Colourfast" product line specifically against this. Clay pavers do not fade because the colour is a fired mineral property.

## Texture and surface finish

Texture depends on the mix and the process: smoother blocks are more attractive, rougher blocks give better skid resistance. SANS 1200 MJ requires texture to be consistent and reference samples approved. Post-production texturing options: split (Bosun's Split Bevel, Rio Rocoso), exposed aggregate / "organic" finishes that read as gravel, tumbled/aged edges, shot-blasted, ground and polished.

**Chamfering** improves both service performance and appearance and is standard on most concrete pavers — the chamfer prevents edge spalling under load and lets the joint sand be seen and topped up. Note the strength-test consequence: strength is computed on the wearing-face area between chamfers, provided that area is ≥ 65 % of the bed face area.

## Efflorescence — cause and cure

A white haze appearing on pavers weeks or months after laying, more visible on dark colours, disappearing when wet and returning when dry. The mechanism, as the CMA sets it out: cement produces lime (water-soluble calcium oxide); concrete contains millions of microscopic capillaries; moisture from rain, irrigation, groundwater, poor drainage or dew enters the capillaries; calcium oxide reacts with water to form calcium hydroxide; this migrates to the surface, reacts with atmospheric carbon dioxide, and deposits calcium carbonate as the water evaporates.

- **It is temporary.** Rain typically washes it away in **1 to 2 years**. It stops entirely when no further calcium hydroxide is available to migrate.
- **Quick removal:** dilute hydrochloric acid, **1 part acid to 20 parts water**, then thorough water rinsing. Side effects: it also leaches lime from the upper layers, roughens the surface and slightly changes the shade.
- **Prevention at the plant:** sufficient compaction (fewer open pores), and chemical admixtures that most manufacturers already dose. It cannot be eliminated entirely — it is a natural by-product of hardened concrete.
- **"Halo" efflorescence** is a different, avoidable problem, caused by soluble salts in the *bedding or jointing sand*. It is unsightly and can reduce skid resistance. Specify salt-free sands.

Machine architecture divides broadly into:
- **Egg-layer (mobile) machines** — the machine lays blocks directly onto a concrete casting floor and moves forward. No boards, no board handling, very low capital cost, high floor-area requirement, more variable quality. The realistic entry point for a very small operation.
- **Stationary board machines** — blocks are made on removable pallets/boards which are then racked and cured. Higher capital cost, far better dimensional control, and the only route to consistent SANS-grade product.

## What a small local plant in northern Namibia would need

This is directly relevant: Okongo is roughly 200 km from Ondangwa and 700 km from Windhoek, and haulage of a 100–200 kg/m² product over that distance can exceed the ex-works block price. Local manufacture becomes rational at surprisingly small volumes.

**The shopping list**

1. **A block machine.** Entry level: a manual or semi-automatic egg-layer or small static machine producing on the order of a few thousand blocks per shift. Serious level: a small stationary board machine in the Columbia SPM20 / Masa Economy class, ~500–1 500 products/hour.
2. **A pan mixer** matched to the machine's cycle volume, plus a moisture-control system or, at minimum, a disciplined manual water-dosing procedure with a bench moisture test.
3. **Aggregate handling** — bins or bunkers, a loader or at minimum a skip hoist, and a weigh batcher. Volumetric batching by wheelbarrow is the usual shortcut and the usual cause of strength variation.
4. **Boards/pallets** (steel, timber or composite) if using a stationary machine, plus racks and a means of moving them.
5. **Moulds** — one mould per shape and thickness. Moulds are consumables with a defined life and are a significant recurring cost; mould wear directly drives dimensional drift.
6. **A curing area** — at minimum a shaded, wind-protected, wettable slab; ideally a covered chamber. In Ohangwena the enemy is evaporation: high temperature, low humidity and wind will dry green blocks before they hydrate, producing weak, dusty product.
7. **A concrete floor** for an egg-layer, or a hardstand and stacking yard for a board machine.
8. **Water** — a reliable supply, of drinking quality or at least free of deleterious salts. This is a real constraint in the far north and interacts with the salt-damage problem.
9. **Quality control** — a compression testing machine (or a contracted laboratory), a set of sieves, a moisture oven or speedy moisture meter, a calibrated scale, callipers or a go/no-go gauge for dimensions, and a records book. Without a testing machine, no SANS claim is defensible.
10. **Cement.** Namibia has domestic supply: **Ohorongo Cement**, established 2007 by Schwenk Zement KG of Germany, plant at Sargberg near Otavi, head office Klein Windhoek, an N$2,5 billion investment built over 22 months. Otavi to Okongo is a manageable haul on tarred road.

**The economics to model, not to guess**

- Blocks per m²: a 220 × 110 mm rectangular paver is ~41/m²; a 220 × 97 mm interlocker ~48/m²; a 116 × 116 mm cobble ~74/m²; a 200 × 200 mm unit ~42/m². Mass ~1,2–2,6 kg each at 50 mm.
- Cement per block, aggregate per block and machine cycle time determine variable cost; the machine, moulds and curing shed determine fixed cost.
- Break-even against delivered blocks is a straight comparison of (ex-works cost + local margin) against (metro ex-works price + haulage + handling + breakage). Breakage on a 700 km gravel-and-tar haul is not trivial.
- The critical risk is **quality**, not cost. A plant that cannot hit the individual-block minimum strength, or that lets moulds wear past tolerance, produces a product that fails in service and destroys the paving contractor's reputation along with it.
