# Paving materials compared — concrete, clay, stone, gravel, asphalt and in-situ

**Summary.** There are about a dozen realistic ways to surface an outdoor area in southern Africa, and they differ by an order of magnitude in cost, by a factor of five in life, and completely in how they behave when it is 38 °C and barefoot children are walking on them. Concrete block paving wins most contests in this region on cost-per-year-of-life, repairability and availability. Clay pavers win on colour permanence. Natural stone wins on appearance and loses on price and slip. Gravel wins on capital cost and loses everything back in maintenance. This file compares them on the seven criteria that actually decide a specification: cost band, durability, slip resistance, hot-climate behaviour, maintenance, aesthetics and fitness for purpose.

## Key facts

The one hard, dated price comparison available is the CMA's independently commissioned July 2009 Gauteng costing of concrete block paving (CBP) against premix asphalt paving (PAP), including estimated 10-year maintenance:

| Car parks, 3 000 m² | CBR 3 | 207,10 | 293,89 |
| Car parks, 3 000 m² | CBR 7 | 175,38 | 282,08 |
| Car parks, 3 000 m² | CBR 15 | 182,19 | 266,31 |
| Urban roads, 5 000 m² | CBR 3 | 276,91 | 355,49 |
| Urban roads, 5 000 m² | CBR 7 | 196,13 | 343,45 |
| Urban roads, 5 000 m² | CBR 15 | 238,63 | 319,41 |
| Heavy duty, 10 000 m² | CBR 3 | 404,77 | 484,03 |
| Heavy duty, 10 000 m² | CBR 7 | 297,98 | 477,18 |
| Heavy duty, 10 000 m² | CBR 15 | 350,19 | 424,98 |

Design used for the car park comparison: CBP 60 mm blocks + 25 mm sand over 100 mm G5 (+125 mm G5 and 150 mm G7/G9 selected layers as subgrade worsens); premix 25 mm PAP over 100 mm G4 + 100 mm G5 + 125 mm G5 + selected layers. Urban roads: CBP 60 + 25 sand over 150 mm G5 + 125 mm C4; premix 30 mm PAP over 125 mm G2 + 150 mm G5 + 125 mm C4.

> ⚠️ These are **2009 rand in Gauteng**. They establish the *ratio* (CBP roughly 60–80 % of the cost of premix, and lower again over 10 years) but not present-day money, and certainly not Namibian money — in Okongo the delivered cost of any manufactured product is dominated by haulage. Never quote them as current prices.

## Material by material

### Concrete pavers
The southern African default, and rightly so. Dry-cast, machine-made, available in 50/55/60/80 mm, in every shape and 20-plus colours, from dozens of plants. Structurally they are the base course of the pavement (see `03_concrete-block-paving.md`). Their killer advantage is **repairability**: lift, fix the cause, relay the same blocks, and the repair is invisible. No other hard surface does this. Their weakness is colour: pigmented dry-cast concrete fades, which is why manufacturers now sell UV-stable "colourfast" and face-mix products at a premium.

### Clay pavers
Fired clay units to SANS 1575, classes PA and PB. Colour is a mineral property of the fired body, so it does not fade — the strongest argument for clay. Corobrik's current range shows what is available: Modular 220 × 108,5 × 50 mm at 41 pavers/m² and 2,6 kg each; Cobble 108,5 × 108,5 × 50 mm at 80/m²; Piazza 220 × 52 × 50 mm at 82/m²; Doppio; thicknesses 50, 65 and 73 mm. Typical published properties: average compressive strength > 33–43 MPa, average modulus of rupture > 4–5 MPa, 24-hour water absorption > 6–7 %, moisture expansion Category I, exposure zone suitability 1–4. They cost more than concrete and are heavier per m² in the thicker sizes, but on a 30-year view in a residential setting they are often the cheaper choice.

### Natural stone
Granite (hardest, most expensive, darkest — a genuine burn hazard around a pool in Ohangwena), sandstone (the most comfortable underfoot in heat, softest, most porous, needs a durable bed), slate (beautiful, thin, delaminates if under 25 mm), quartzite (extremely abrasion-resistant, naturally cleft, good value where locally quarried). In Namibia, locally quarried stone can invert the usual economics: hauling stone 50 km beats hauling concrete blocks 700 km. Specify **flamed, bush-hammered or riven** finishes outdoors; never honed or polished. Lay on a compacted bed with a mortar or a stabilised sand bed depending on unit size, and accept that the joints, not the stone, are what you will maintain.

### Cobblestone and setts
Small cubes or rectangles of granite or other hard stone, traditionally laid on sand in fan or arc patterns. Structurally they are block paving with a much better block. Their drawbacks are real: rough ride, bad for high heels, wheelchairs and trolleys, noisy, and expensive to lay because setting is slow and skilled. Their virtue is that they never wear out and they slow traffic without a hump. Concrete "cobble" products (Concobble, Classic Cobble, Village Cobble, Revelstone's 50 mm and 75 mm cobbles) exist precisely to get the look at a tenth of the price.

### Exposed aggregate and in-situ concrete
Exposed aggregate — cast in place, surface retarder applied, cement paste washed off to reveal the aggregate — gives the best slip resistance of any concrete finish and hides dirt well. In-situ concrete in general demands correct jointing (contraction joints at roughly 24–30 × slab thickness, isolation joints at every fixed element) and a properly compacted subbase; without those it cracks randomly and the crack is permanent. Broomed finish is the utility default. Stamped concrete looks like paving until the colour hardener wears through in the wheel paths, then looks like failed concrete; it needs re-sealing every 2–3 years and should be sold with that condition in writing. Polished concrete is an indoor material that kills people outdoors when wet.

### Permeable paving
Permeable concrete block paving (PCBP) uses wider, aggregate-filled joints (angular aggregate, **not sand**) over an open-graded permeable subbase that stores water. Three system types: **A — full infiltration** (all water into the subgrade, where subgrade permeability is good); **B — partial infiltration** (some infiltrates, the rest is collected by outlet pipes); **C — no infiltration** (fully tanked with an impermeable membrane, used where subgrade permeability is poor or contamination is a risk). Performance data from the CMA: initial infiltration through joints of newly laid PCBP is around **4 000 mm/hour**; American and German practice designs for **10 %** of that to allow for clogging, which still leaves a very large factor of safety. Pollutant removal: total suspended solids 60–95 %, hydrocarbons 70–90 %, total phosphorus 50–80 %, total nitrogen 65–80 %, heavy metals 60–95 %. Other permeable families: pervious concrete, porous asphalt, plastic grid systems filled with gravel or turf, resin-bound aggregate, stabilised decomposed granite. General limitations: roughly a third more expensive than impervious paving; unsuitable at fuelling stations and vehicle-maintenance "stormwater hot spots"; generally inappropriate where surrounding land draining onto the pavement exceeds 20 % slope; and maintenance (suction cleaning) is not optional.

### Gravel and decomposed granite
Cheapest to build, most expensive to own. The engineering is the same as for a gravel road (see `08_roads-in-namibia.md`): the wearing course needs a **Shrinkage Product of 120–400** and a **Grading Coefficient of 16–34**, maximum size ≤ 37,5 mm, CBR ≥ 25 % at 95 % MDD. Material with GC below 16 erodes badly. Traffic compaction alone can consume up to 30 % of the constructed thickness soon after laying. In a residential setting expect annual topping up, weeds, dust into the house, gravel migrating onto the road, and a surface that is impossible to sweep or hose. Decomposed granite with a stabiliser is a better path material than a driveway material.

### Grasscrete and turf pavers
Cellular concrete or plastic grids installed on an open-graded stone base with topsoil and grass. Genuinely the coolest hard surface available, because evapotranspiration works. Structurally adequate for occasional vehicles, not for daily parking in the same spot. In a hot dry region the grass needs irrigation or it becomes bare sand in a grid, at which point you have paid concrete prices for gravel performance. Concrete grid alternatives such as Technicrete's Armorflex HLP and Infraset/Terraforce-type products serve the same function.

### Asphalt and chip seal
Asphalt premix requires a hot-mix plant within a viable haul (typically 1–2 hours) and a paver crew — both usually absent in the far north of Namibia. It is the fastest way to surface a large area where the plant exists, and the worst thermally: a black surface in Ohangwena in November will exceed 60 °C. Chip seal (surface treatment) is the low-volume-road answer: prime, spray hot bitumen or emulsion, spread stone chips, roll. It is far cheaper, resealable, and buildable with a small distributor and a chip spreader. TRH 3 governs the design; Cape seals (a chip seal followed by slurry) are the durable version and were the surfacing on the Namibian long-term experiments.

### Stabilised earth
Cement or lime stabilisation of the in-situ or imported soil, compacted and sometimes sealed. Very relevant to Namibia: it converts an abundant, free material into a usable layer. In the Namibian context be aware of the salt problem — highly soluble salts above about 0,2 % cause surface disintegration of primed bases and blister bituminous surfacings, and gypsum interacts with lime and cement stabilisation. Netterberg's long-term experiments established that gypsum up to **10 % in a G3 crushed-stone base and at least 5 % in a G4 calcrete base** is tolerable for 30 years and 1,0 × 10⁶ E80 under a 19 mm Cape seal in an arid climate — a far more permissive limit than the historical 1,0 % sulfate rule.

## Selecting for a hot, remote, sandy site

For a residential project in Ohangwena the ranking changes from the metropolitan default:

1. **Locally made concrete blocks** — if a block machine or an existing producer is within economic haul, this beats everything on delivered cost. See `05_paving-manufacture.md`.
2. **Pale-coloured concrete blocks over dark ones** — surface temperature, not aesthetics, should drive the colour choice for pool surrounds, patios and any barefoot area.
3. **Clay pavers** — worth the premium only if the haul from a South African or Namibian kiln is affordable; the colour permanence argument is strongest in high-UV environments.
4. **Exposed aggregate or broomed in-situ concrete** — competitive where cement and local aggregate are available but manufactured units are not, and where a slab is being cast anyway.
5. **Chip seal** — the right answer for a long farm-length driveway if a contractor with a distributor can be brought in.
6. **Gravel** — the honest answer for the approach road, with a paved apron at the house so the sand stops at the threshold.

Avoid asphalt (no plant), polished anything (slip), and dark stone around water (heat).
