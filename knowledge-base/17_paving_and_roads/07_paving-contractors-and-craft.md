# Paving contractors and the craft of laying — output, setting out, cutting, defects and pricing

**Summary.** Laying block paving looks unskilled and is not. A good paviour reads levels continuously, keeps a diagonal laying face moving, holds a 3 mm joint by feel, decides in advance where the cuts will fall, and never lets the sand depth drift. A poor one buries an uneven subbase in sand, lays to a kerb line that is itself untrue, cuts slivers at every edge, and vibrates before the closures are in. The difference shows up two summers later as dishing, tramlining and creeping edges — by which time the blocks are blamed. This file covers how a paving team is organised, how fast it works, how it sets out and cuts, the defect catalogue with inspection tests for each, and how the work is quoted.

## Key facts

| Item | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Optimum joint width | 3 mm; permitted range 2–6 mm; average 3 mm over 20 blocks | SANS 1200 MJ / CMA |
| Laying face geometry | Diagonal, not straight | CMA Book 4 |
| Laying direction on a slope | **Uphill** — laying downhill opens joints | CMA Book 4 |
| Undulating ground | Start at the low point of the dip, work outward both ways | CMA |
| Minimum cut size at edges | ≥ 25 % of a full block | CMA / SANS 1200 MJ |
| Trial section | ≥ 20 m long × ~6 m wide, from a permanent edge restraint, approved before full production | SANS 1200 MJ 7.2 |
| Compaction before jointing | ≥ 2 passes; stop 1 m short of any free edge at day's end | SANS 1200 MJ 5.6.1 |
| Bedding sand moisture at spreading | 6 % ± 2 % | SANS 1200 MJ 5.3 |
| Surface level difference between adjacent units | ≤ 3 mm | SANS 1200 MJ 6.2 |
| Deviation from a 3 m straightedge (general) | +10, −15 mm | SANS 1200 MJ 6.2 |
| Anchor beams required | Slope > 12 % (8–12 % at engineer's discretion) | CMA Technical Note: Steep Slopes |

> ⚠️ **The paviour is not responsible for the layerworks, but he owns the consequences.** SANS 1200 MJ commentary is explicit: where a paviour lays only the paving, "it is advisable that he checks that the levels of the earthworks conform to the specifications and drawings and where necessary gets the contractor to take remedial action. However, the main contractor is responsible for the strength and the line and level of the layerworks." Record the layerwork levels in writing before starting, or the settlement will be your problem.

## How a paving team is organised

A typical block-paving gang splits into four functions, and the site is planned around keeping all four busy:

1. **Screeding** — one or two people preparing and striking the bedding sand ahead of the laying face, working off screed rails or off the edge restraints (the screed board is cut to the required depth below the top of the restraint).
2. **Laying** — the largest group, working the diagonal face. Where more than one person lays on a long face, **rotate the operators** — different hands produce different joint widths, and unrotated operators produce visible bands of different joint spacing.
3. **Cutting** — one person on the splitter or saw, working from marks taken off the actual gap, not from a drawing.
4. **Compacting and jointing** — the plate compactor operator and the labourer brooming in joint sand, working a safe distance behind the laying face.

Feeding all four is a materials function: blocks delivered as close to the work face as possible, moved in wheelbarrows, clamp trolleys or trolley-and-stools; bedding and jointing sand stockpiled separately and **covered with plastic** to hold a uniform moisture content. Sand that is too wet will not screed and will not compact; too dry and it will not compact either. Blocks stacked in the barrow in laying order make unloading fast.

The site sequence is: edge restraints in → screed → lay from a corner on a diagonal face → adjust to string line → cut closures → compact (2 passes) → broom in joint sand → compact again → repeat until joints stay full → sweep off → temporary restraint at the end of the day's work.

## Output rates

There is no source-backed published output table for southern African paving teams; the figures below are **planning estimates and are `needs-verification`**. They assume a competent 6–8 person team with a plate compactor, blocks delivered to the face, and layerworks already accepted.

| Work type | Indicative m²/team/day |
|---|---|
| 50–60 mm rectangular blocks, stretcher bond, large open area, few cuts | 250–400 |
| 60 mm interlocking blocks, herringbone, large open area | 180–300 |
| 60 mm herringbone, residential driveway with kerbs, curves and manholes | 80–150 |
| 80 mm blocks, industrial, herringbone | 120–220 |
| Cobble-format units (74–92 per m²) | 50–110 |
| Setts / natural stone, hand-set | 15–40 |
| Circles, fans, radial patterns, heavy cutting | 20–60 |
| Kerb laying (linear) | 60–120 m/day |

The variables that actually move these numbers, in order of magnitude: **cutting proportion** (a narrow path with a curved edge can be 40 % cut blocks); **blocks per m²** (a 92/m² cobble is more than twice the placing operations of a 41/m² modular); **pattern** (herringbone at 45° has a cut on every edge block); **distance from stockpile to face**; and **whether the layerworks are right** (rescreeding lost to a bad subbase can halve output).

## Setting out and holding line

- **Commence from one point only.** A uniform pattern across a whole area only happens if there is a single origin.
- Determine the line of paving to suit the site: align with the roadway direction, or parallel to a wall or edge restraint — whichever the eye will read as "the line".
- **Do not trust the kerb.** The kerb line itself may not be true. Set **two string lines at right angles** to each other and check alignment against them regularly, not occasionally.
- Start laying in a right-angled corner and work outwards in both directions, establishing a **diagonal laying face**.
- Check levels at regular intervals as laying proceeds. If levels change because the sand type or its moisture has changed, the surcharge changes — lift the blocks, rake and re-screed to new levels, and relay. Do not chase level by varying the sand depth.
- Adjust alignment with a lever, moving sections of blocks, **before** compaction and jointing. Common corrections: sliding a unit down into place with a change of position; the two-handed method holding firmly against the adjacent units; pushing the pavement over from the edge to straighten the lines.
- Do it in small areas as you go. Correcting a large paved area is disproportionately harder.

## Achieving falls and levels

- Falls are set in the **layerworks and the edge restraints**, not in the bedding sand. Top of subbase: minimum 1 % longitudinal, 2 % transverse, level to ±10 mm, smoothness ±10 mm on a 3 m straightedge — the *same* tolerance as the finished paving.
- Minimum 2 % fall in any direction on the finished surface. If deviations to the permitted tolerance are allowed, the design fall must be **steeper than 1 in 50**, or ponding is arithmetically certain.
- Set the paving **5–10 mm proud** of channels, gully gratings and manhole covers.
- Establish the surcharge empirically at the start of the job: lay a test area on 30 mm of loose sand, compact with three passes of the job's plate compactor, measure the settlement, and derive the loose depth that gives 25 mm compacted. Re-derive it whenever the sand changes.
- On slopes over 12 %, anchor beams are required (spacing guideline: 12 % → 30 m, 15 % → 20 m, 20 % → 15 m), cast 7–10 mm below the surrounding blockwork with 25 mm weepholes at 1,0 m centres.

## Cutting

- **Block splitter (guillotine)** — a hydraulic or mechanical guillotine that shears the block. Fast, dust-free, silent, no water, no power. The cut face is rough and slightly irregular, which is fine at an edge restraint and wrong at a visible junction. The default tool.
- **Power saw** (angle grinder or bench/table saw with a diamond blade) — clean, accurate, allows curves, mitres and precise closures around manholes. Slower, needs water suppression or dust extraction, needs power or fuel, and produces silica dust. Use for visible cuts, radii and services.
- Method: measure the actual gap, mark the block with a chalk line, cut, tap into place. For irregular edges, mark round the shape; for free-form curves, lay a garden hose to the line and mark against it.
- **Defer cutting** until enough work is complete to allow a continuous cutting operation — batching cuts is far more efficient than cutting one at a time.
- **No cut smaller than about 25 % of a full block.** Small pieces dislodge under traffic. If the geometry produces slivers, realign the blocks along the edge restraint into a soldier course so cutting produces large pieces instead.
- **Avoid infill concrete.** Where unavoidable: full depth to the subbase, at least 15 MPa at 24 hours, 9,5 mm maximum aggregate, all adjacent units pre-wetted, cured 24 hours under moist sand or plastic, and no compaction within 1 m for 24 hours or until 15 MPa is reached.

## Curves, circles and manholes

- **Curves:** with an S-A interlocker, the block orientation must be turned progressively through the curve; the CMA publishes a specific detail for block orientation through curves. Joints open on the outside of the arc — hold them within 6 mm or introduce a cut course.
- **Circles and fans:** either buy a proprietary circle kit (tapered units in radial courses) or accept heavy sawing. A hand-cut circle in rectangular blocks is a two-day job for a competent cutter on a 3 m circle. Set out from a centre pin with a tape or a trammel.
- **Manholes and penetrations:** these are the classic settlement points because the surrounding backfill is rarely compacted to the same standard as the general layerworks. Backfill all service trenches within the paving area to subgrade level with approved granular material before subbase construction; complete all piped and subsoil drainage under the pavement with the subgrade preparation. Confirm the position of existing services with the relevant authority. Cut blocks to the manhole rather than infilling, set the paving 5–10 mm proud of the cover, and use a full-depth concrete collar only where the geometry defeats cutting.
- **Change of pattern orientation:** the CMA publishes a cut-joint detail for this. Do not let a herringbone field simply run into a stretcher field — the junction needs a deliberate cut joint or a contrasting soldier band.
- **Tie-in to bitumen surfaces:** a detailed junction is required; a block field abutting an asphalt surfacing less than 100 mm thick still needs its own edge restraint.

## Workmanship defects, and how to inspect for each

| Defect | What you see | Cause | Inspection test |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dishing** | Local hollow, often near a trench, manhole or the middle of a driveway | Bedding sand used to level an uneven subbase; uncompacted trench backfill; depression filled with sand instead of subbase material | 3 m straightedge in two directions; flood test for ponding; if it holds water it fails |
| **Tramlining** | Two parallel depressions along the wheel paths | Bedding sand too deep, joints not filled, no lock-up developed, pattern with continuous joints parallel to traffic | Straightedge across the wheel paths; check joint fullness with a screwdriver — it should not enter more than a few mm |
| **Poor compaction** | Blocks rock underfoot; a hollow sound under the plate; loose feel | Fewer than two passes before jointing; compactor too small for the block thickness; joints not filled before trafficking | Walk the field listening and feeling; verify the compactor's plate area and centrifugal force against the SANS 1200 MJ requirement for the block thickness |
| **Sand depth variation** | Level changes with no change in the subbase; blocks sitting proud or low in patches | Surcharge not re-derived when sand moisture or type changed; screeding by eye | Lift three blocks in suspect areas and measure the bed; it must be 25 ± 10 mm |
| **Wrong pattern at edges** | Slivers of cut block along kerbs; herringbone running unresolved into an edge | No soldier course; no plan for where cuts fall | Walk the perimeter; any cut under 25 % of a full block is a defect |
| **Inadequate edge restraint** | Perimeter blocks creeping outward, joints opening, kerb tilting | Restraint cast after laying, no concrete haunch, restraint not founded on the subbase | Push a perimeter block by hand; check the haunch by excavating one 300 mm length |
| **Open or unfilled joints** | Visible gaps, weeds, sand loss | Joint sand washed out, never topped up at 3 months, wrong jointing sand (too coarse) | Screwdriver probe; sieve a sample of the joint sand — it must pass 1,18 mm with 10–50 % passing 0,075 mm |
| **Joints too narrow** | Edge and corner spalling under load; chipped arrises | Blocks butted, no 3 mm allowance | Measure 20 consecutive joints; average must be ~3 mm |
| **Joints too wide** | Loose feel, no load transfer, sand loss | Careless placing, worn moulds, no string line reference | As above; > 6 mm is out of tolerance |
| **Shoving / creep** | Blocks displaced at gate approaches, on gradients, at turning points | No anchor beam on slope; laid downhill; braking and turning forces with weak horizontal interlock | Straightedge along the slope; look for a bulge above the bottom restraint |
| **Cement in the joints** | Hard, cracked joint lines; grey staining on the blocks | Someone "grouted" the paving | Scrape a joint; the material should be loose sand |
| **Efflorescence** | White haze, stronger on dark blocks, vanishes when wet | Natural; lime migration (see `05_paving-manufacture.md`) | Wet the surface — if the haze disappears it is efflorescence, not colour failure. Not a defect |
| **Colour banding** | Visible bands or blocks of a different shade | Deliveries from different production runs mixed unsystematically; mould wear changing plan size | Ask for delivery notes; the fix is to blend from three packs at once during laying, not to sort afterwards |
| **Ponding** | Standing water after rain | Fall below 2 %, or fall within the tolerance band so the tolerance eats the fall | Flood the surface. SANS 1200 MJ allows the engineer to order this; the contractor pays if ponding occurs, the employer pays if not |

## How paving contractors quote

Typical structure of a residential block-paving quotation in southern Africa:

1. **Preliminaries** — site establishment, setting out, protection, spoil removal. Often absorbed into the rate on small jobs, which is where disputes start.
2. **Excavation and cart away** — per m³ or per m². Depth to formation, and whether spoil is spread on site or removed, are the two big variables.
3. **Layerworks** — per m² per layer, or per m³ compacted, stating material class (G5, G6, C4) and compacted thickness. If the quotation does not name a material class and a thickness, it is not a quotation.
4. **Bedding sand** — per m², stating 25 mm compacted.
5. **Blocks — supply** — per m², by product name, thickness, colour and pattern, with a stated waste allowance.
6. **Blocks — lay** — per m², usually the labour-only rate; higher for herringbone, circles, cobble formats and small areas.
7. **Edge restraints** — per linear metre by type, including the concrete haunch.
8. **Cutting** — sometimes a separate item on jobs with many curves or manholes; more often absorbed.
9. **Jointing and compaction** — usually absorbed into the laying rate.
10. **Provisional items** — additional excavation in hard material, dealing with unsuitable subgrade, service diversions.

**What a fair rate looks like** is a market question that changes with cement, fuel and exchange rates, so no rand figure is given here (see `10_specifying-and-costing-paving.md` for how to build one up). The structural test of a quotation is not the number — it is whether the following are all named: block product and thickness; pattern; bedding sand thickness; subbase material class and compacted thickness; edge restraint type and haunch; waste allowance; who does the excavation and spoil removal; the compaction procedure; the joint top-up visit; and the defects period. A quotation missing any of these is a quotation for a different, cheaper job than the one you think you are buying.

**Red flags in a paving quotation:** a single all-in rate per m² with no breakdown; no mention of the subbase; "sand bed" with no thickness; no edge restraint item; a price that is 30 % below the others (the saving is always in the layerworks, where you cannot see it); an offer to grout the joints with cement; and no return visit for joint top-up.

## Warranty and the defects period

Manufacturers guarantee the *block*, not the *pavement*: Bosun, for example, offers a five-year product guarantee. The installation warranty is a separate contractual matter with the paviour. A reasonable residential arrangement is a 12-month defects liability period covering settlement, joint loss and edge movement, with an explicit exclusion for damage from vehicles heavier than the design case and from subsequent trenching by others — and an explicit inclusion of the three-month joint top-up visit.
