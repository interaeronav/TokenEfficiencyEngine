# Namibia, the Cuvelai system and the Ohangwena aquifer

**Summary.** Namibia is the driest country in sub-Saharan Africa and every one of its perennial rivers is on an international border. Northern Namibia — where roughly 40% of the population lives on about 5% of the land area — depends on three things: imported Kunene water through the Calueque–Oshakati canal, a shallow perched aquifer of variable quality, and, since 2012, a newly-confirmed deep freshwater aquifer (Ohangwena II / KOH-2) at 220–300 m depth. This file sets out the regional water resources, the Cuvelai drainage and its *efundja* floods, the Ohangwena aquifer system in detail, and the practical water options for a homestead near Okongo.

## 1. Namibia's water resources in outline

Namibia's water problem is structural, not incidental:

- **All perennial rivers are border rivers.** The **Kunene** (north-west, Angola border), the **Okavango** (north-east, Angola border), the **Zambezi** and its Kwando–Linyanti–Chobe system (far north-east), and the **Orange** (south, South Africa border). Not one perennial river flows through the interior.
- **Interior surface water is ephemeral.** Every other river flows for days to weeks per year (see `03`).
- **Groundwater carries the country.** Most towns, farms and communal water points depend on boreholes, and Windhoek runs one of the world's longest-established potable managed-aquifer-recharge and direct-reclamation systems.
- **Rainfall gradient** runs from under 50 mm/yr on the coast to ~550–700 mm/yr in the Zambezi Region and the north-east; Ohangwena sits near the wet end of the national range, and is still semi-arid because of evaporative demand.

**Transboundary institutions.** Namibia is party to a set of basin commissions:
- **Kunene** — the Permanent Joint Technical Commission (PJTC) with Angola, which governs the Ruacana and Calueque schemes.
- **Okavango** — **OKACOM**, the Permanent Okavango River Basin Water Commission, established by a 1994 tripartite agreement between Angola, Namibia and Botswana. <https://www.okacom.org/>
- **Zambezi** — **ZAMCOM**, the Zambezi Watercourse Commission (eight riparian states).
- **Orange–Senqu** — **ORASECOM**, with Lesotho, South Africa and Botswana.
- **Cuvelai** — a bilateral Angola–Namibia Cuvelai watercourse commission (CUVECOM).

`needs-verification`: only the OKACOM 1994 agreement was independently confirmed in this pass. The PJTC, ZAMCOM, ORASECOM and CUVECOM founding dates and mandates should be checked against the treaty texts before they are quoted in any formal document.

**[NA] Domestic law.** The **Water Resources Management Act 11 of 2013** (GG 5367), signed 2 December 2013, was brought into force on **29 August 2023** by GN 268/2023 (GG 8187). Key provisions for a private landowner:

- **s. 39** — an owner or occupier of land outside a local authority area and outside a water protection area may, **without a licence**, abstract water from a well on the land for own domestic use, and may dig or sink such a well for that purpose.
- **s. 40** — an owner or occupier, **including on communal land**, may collect meteoric (rain) water falling onto the land for own domestic use without a licence, provided this does not harm the environment or downstream users. This is the statutory basis for rainwater harvesting.
- **s. 56** — outside the s. 39 exemption, a **borehole licence** from the Minister is required to drill, deepen, enlarge, alter, clean or rehabilitate a borehole, or to run a drilling programme. The Minister may publish gazetted exemptions.
- **s. 67** — borehole **drillers and well constructors must be licensed**.
- **s. 218** — a duty to keep and provide records of drilling. In practice this means your driller's log should be lodged; it is also how the national groundwater database gets built, so insist on a copy for yourself.

> ⚠️ **[NA]** The s. 39/s. 40 exemptions are for **own domestic use** only. Abstraction for irrigation, commercial use or supply to others falls under the licensing regime in Part 9 of the Act. If your homestead is inside a proclaimed village or settlement area (Okongo is a proclaimed village), the "outside a local authority area" condition in s. 39 may not be met — check the boundary.

## 2. The Cuvelai drainage system and the iishana

The **Cuvelai-Etosha Basin** covers roughly 160,000 km² across southern Angola and north-central Namibia, and drains to the **Etosha Pan**, the basin's lowest point and a terminal salt sink. It is an endorheic, intracontinental sedimentary basin; the underlying tectonic depression is the **Owambo Basin**, which extends further east and holds 400–600 m of Cretaceous-to-Recent sediment in its centre. It is home to about 1.2 million people, roughly 70% of them in Namibia, and to about 40% of Namibia's population on ~5% of its land area.

Its distinguishing feature is the **iishana** (singular *oshana*): hundreds of shallow, broad, grass-floored, interconnected channels rather than a single incised river. They are typically a few hundred metres wide, less than 1–2 m deep, and separated by almost imperceptible sandy rises. Because the basin gradient is on the order of 1:5,000 to 1:10,000 (roughly 100 m of fall over the whole Namibian portion), water moves slowly, spreads laterally and ponds — which is why the same landscape is both a flood hazard and the region's most important recharge mechanism.

### The efundja

**Efundja** is the seasonal flooding of the iishana. Its mechanics:

- **Two sources.** Local rainfall on the Namibian iishana plain, and — more importantly for large events — runoff generated in the Angolan highlands that arrives down the Cuvelai channels from the north.
- **Timing.** Rain begins in a minor wet season around November–December, with the core season January–March. Flood arrival in the Namibian iishana typically peaks between about **February and April**, lagging the rain. Because Angolan runoff has to travel, a big efundja can arrive during a locally dry spell — an important warning-system point.
- **Duration.** Weeks to a few months; the iishana then drain, evaporate and dry back, with residual pools persisting into the dry season in the deeper depressions.
- **Extent.** Highly variable. In a strong year the inundated area is a connected sheet across much of the central-north; in a weak year the iishana never connect at all.
- **Variability.** Substantial. The 2008, 2009 and 2011 seasons produced severe, damaging regional floods; 1992–93 and 2019 were extreme droughts. In the modelled Okongo rainfall series the wettest year (2007, ~1,330 mm) is more than six times the driest (2019, ~212 mm).
- **Slow-onset, therefore forecastable.** Unlike flash floods in steep terrain, the efundja develops over days to weeks. Upstream Angolan gauge readings, satellite inundation mapping and Google Flood Hub-type forecasts all have useful lead time here — which is precisely why the absence of an operational early-warning system has been repeatedly identified in the flood-impact literature as a vulnerability driver.
- **Consequences.** Loss of *mahangu* crops, destruction of traditional homesteads and roads, cut-off of clinics and schools, contamination of hand-dug wells, and post-flood water-borne disease. Wanke et al. found EC and turbidity in surface water lowest during flooding (average ~33 µS/cm, ~120 FAU), which sounds benign but reflects dilution rather than safety — the bacteriological risk is highest then.

**Okongo's position matters.** Okongo lies in the far north-east of Ohangwena, on the Eastern Sand Zone, not in the heart of the iishana plain. That has three practical consequences: (i) deep sandy soils with high infiltration and little surface runoff, (ii) less exposure to efundja inundation than Oshakati or Ongwediva, and (iii) better shallow groundwater quality than the western parts of the basin. It is also further from the Calueque canal, so piped bulk water is thinner on the ground.

## 3. The Ohangwena aquifer system

The Namibian portion of the basin is underlain by the **Cubango Megafan**, a very large alluvial fan sourced from the Angolan highlands, whose sediment architecture — stacked sand bodies separated by fine-grained, clay-rich layers — creates the stacked "storeys" of groundwater.

### KOH-0 — the perched aquifer (0–30 m)
Discontinuous perched lenses of shallow groundwater, tapped by thousands of hand-dug wells across the region. Quality in Ohangwena is generally good: EC 52–296 µS/cm, pH 7.1–7.6, average temperature ~25 °C, hydrochemically Na⁺-dominated with HCO₃⁻ as the main anion. Problems: high turbidity (up to 255 NTU in shallow wells), potassium above WHO limits in about half of shallow-well samples, and bacteriological contamination from unprotected wellheads and livestock. These are seasonally recharged and can dry out in a bad year.

### KOH-1 — the first continuous aquifer (30–50 m)
The regionally continuous upper aquifer. In eastern Ohangwena it is marginal for drinking (EC 500–1,200 µS/cm) and its salinity increases markedly westwards; in Omusati and the west the equivalent shallow groundwater is unusable, reaching 11,450 µS/cm with ~70% of hand-dug wells unfit on sulphate alone. This west-to-east freshening is the single most important spatial pattern in Cuvelai hydrogeology and it is why the water supply of the central north was historically built on imported Kunene water rather than local boreholes.

### KOH-2 — the Ohangwena II deep aquifer (~220–300 m)
The significant discovery. The history is worth knowing because it explains why nobody found it earlier: government drilling programmes in the 1970s and 1980s rarely went below 100 m. During a drought-relief programme an exploration well near **Eenyama** was pushed past the conventional 150 m limit to a total depth of **259 m**; below a sequence of silts and claystones it struck good-quality freshwater at about **240 m**, without reaching the base of the aquifer. The confined aquifer's pressure head is *higher* than that of the brackish aquifer above it — it is sub-artesian — which is itself an important clue that the two systems are hydraulically separate.

Subsequent work (about a dozen deep monitoring and pumping wells, two of them cored) established:
- **The seal.** The aquitard between KOH-1 and KOH-2 is fine sandy and silty with high clay content. Clay-mineralogical work showed swelling clays in the pore spaces; hydraulic tests on water-saturated, triaxially pressurised samples gave conductivities on the order of **10⁻⁹ m s⁻¹**. Dry core looked porous; under confining pressure and saturation it is essentially impermeable.
- **The aquifer.** Pumping tests gave permeability coefficients of **10⁻⁶ to 10⁻⁵ m s⁻¹**, consistent with grain-size analysis. Combined with substantial thickness, high porosity and relative homogeneity, KOH-2 is considered highly productive.
- **It is semi-fossil, not fossil.** Numerical flow modelling constrained by heads, flow direction from contour maps, and ¹⁴C groundwater ages, using a heuristic ensemble of 143 physically realistic boundary conditions, showed recharge comes predominantly from the **foothills of the Angolan highlands** in the north. Infiltration from the Kunene highlands is negligible and from the Cubango River small. Best model realisations achieved an RMSE of 0.82 m on groundwater levels. TerraSAR-X interferometry independently showed a seasonal uplift signal over the inferred Angolan recharge outcrop.
- **The number that governs use.** Recharge is **less than 1% of mean annual precipitation**, giving recharge volumes of only **a few million cubic metres per year** — and that, not the stored volume, is the maximum sustainable yield. The authors are explicit: this can sustainably support local drinking-water supply if managed carefully, but large-scale irrigation or a long-distance transfer scheme (e.g. to Windhoek) "should be considered with a great deal of scepticism". Residence time in the slow eastern part of the system may be 100,000 years or more.

> ⚠️ **Drilling into KOH-2 is a specialist job, not a routine borehole.** The swelling-clay aquitard is difficult to drill, must be penetrated carefully, cased continuously with a strong casing string, and the annulus grouted to prevent brackish KOH-1 water leaking down into the fresh aquifer. Himmelsbach et al. state it is "of utmost importance that the pressure level of the deep aquifer must not fall below the bottom of the aquitard during pumping" — over-pumping does not just lower yield, it risks permanently compromising the seal and the resource. Do not commission a deep Ohangwena borehole from a contractor who has not done one before.

## 4. Calueque–Oshakati canal and the piped system

Because local shallow groundwater in the central north is too saline, water supply there has for decades depended on the Kunene: water is taken from the **Calueque Dam in Angola** and conveyed south into Namibia by an **open canal** to Oshakati, from which a **dendritic system of small-diameter pipelines** distributes to towns and rural water points across Ohangwena, Omusati, Oshana and Oshikoto. The system's weaknesses are named in the literature: **seepage and evaporation losses from the open canal**, growing demand, and dependence on infrastructure and a reservoir in another country. Isotope and hydrochemistry studies have been used specifically to quantify the evaporation loss along the canal.

## 5. Rural water supply in Ohangwena

**NamWater** is the national bulk water supplier (a commercial state-owned enterprise); the Ministry responsible for agriculture, water and land reform handles rural water supply policy; **regional councils** and community-level **water point committees** manage local schemes. In practice a rural Ohangwena household draws from some combination of:
1. a **NamWater/DRWS community water point** (a standpipe or tank fed from the canal-and-pipeline network),
2. a **hand-dug well** into KOH-0 (shallow, cheap, seasonal, turbid, bacteriologically risky),
3. a **drilled borehole** into KOH-0 or KOH-1 with a hand pump, windmill or solar pump,
4. **rainwater harvesting** from roofs into tanks,
5. **surface water** from iishana and pans during and after the efundja.

Wanke et al. concluded that adding responsibility to water point committees, fencing off wells, using perched aquifers deliberately, and rainwater harvesting are the local-scale actions with the biggest return — and that in the Cuvelai "sufficient water exists, but its quality and quantity are highly variable".

## 6. Drought and flood history

| Period | Character |
|---|---|
| 1992–93 | Extreme aridity; harvest failure; livestock losses up to one third |
| 2007–2011 | Repeated wet seasons and severe regional efundja flooding (2008, 2009, 2011 the worst) |
| 2013 | National drought emergency |
| 2015–2016 | El Niño drought; 2015 among the driest modelled years at Okongo |
| 2019 | Severe drought, national state of emergency; the driest year in the 1991–2024 modelled series (~212 mm) |
| 2020–2024 | Mixed; several below-mean years in the modelled series (2023 ~358 mm, 2024 ~281 mm) |

The alternation of dry and wet phases on roughly a 10–15 year rhythm is the documented long-term pattern. Any water plan for a homestead must survive two consecutive bad years, not one.

## 7. Practical water options for a homestead near Okongo

**Option A — hand-dug well or shallow borehole into KOH-0 (0–30 m).**
Cheapest. Water quality in eastern Ohangwena is usually acceptable (EC 52–296 µS/cm) but turbidity and bacteriological contamination are the norm rather than the exception, and yield can fail in a drought year. Non-negotiables: a sealed and raised wellhead, a fenced perimeter to keep livestock out, a properly grouted annulus, and treatment before drinking (see `05`). Legally covered by s. 39 of the Act if outside the local authority area.

**Option B — drilled borehole into KOH-0/KOH-1 (30–60 m).**
More reliable yield, more likely to be brackish (EC 500–1,200 µS/cm) at the KOH-1 level. Test EC and fluoride/nitrate before committing to a pump. Requires a borehole licence unless the s. 39 domestic exemption applies, and a licensed driller under s. 67.

**Option C — deep borehole into KOH-2 (220–300 m).**
Excellent water; very expensive; specialist drilling and casing; and it must be justified against the basin-wide sustainable yield. This is a scheme-scale, not homestead-scale, option.

**Option D — NamWater / community water point connection.**
Where a pipeline reaches, this is usually the lowest-effort supply, but continuity is not guaranteed and Okongo is at the thin end of the network. Treat it as one source among several, not the only one.

**Option E — rainwater harvesting.** Legally permitted without a licence (s. 40) for own domestic use. Scale, worked in full in `05`. In summary, using a mean of ~550 mm/yr with a runoff coefficient of 0.85:

```
Annual yield  ≈ 550 mm × 0.85 × A(m²) = 468 L per m² of roof per year
```
A 150 m² roof therefore yields about **70 m³ per year in an average year** — but only about **27 m³ in a 2019-type year**. Design against the dry year, not the mean.

**Storage sizing, first cut.** With rain confined to December–March, storage has to bridge roughly eight dry months. For a household using 60 L/person/day, four people:
```
Dry-season demand = 4 × 60 L/d × 240 d = 57.6 m³
```
which exceeds an average year's harvest from a 150 m² roof. The honest conclusion for Okongo: **rainwater harvesting alone cannot supply a household year-round from a normal roof.** It is a genuine and worthwhile supplement — enough to cover drinking and cooking, to buffer supply interruptions, and to irrigate a garden through the early dry season — and it should be combined with a borehole or a NamWater connection. A 10–20 m³ tank sized on the *first-flush-corrected wet-season surplus* is a rational target; sizing method and a full month-by-month worked example are in `05`.

**Recommended combination for a homestead near Okongo:**
1. Borehole into KOH-0/KOH-1 as the primary volume supply, with EC, fluoride, nitrate and bacteriological testing before commissioning.
2. Solar-pumped delivery to an elevated storage tank (see `05`).
3. Roof rainwater harvesting into a separate 10–20 m³ tank as the potable-quality source, with first-flush diversion and point-of-use treatment.
4. NamWater connection where available as a backup.
5. Build the house above the local flood level and manage stormwater on the plot (see `06`).
