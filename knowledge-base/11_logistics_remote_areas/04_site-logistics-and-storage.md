# Site logistics, storage and material protection on remote sites

**Summary.** On a remote site, material that arrives damaged, degraded or stolen cannot be replaced quickly — the replacement takes a whole freight cycle. That inverts the usual economics: it is worth spending unusual amounts on storage, protection and security, because the alternative is not a top-up delivery, it is a stopped programme. This file covers laydown planning, protection against heat and dust, cement storage in a humid northern Namibian summer, timber stacking and stickering, theft control, fuel storage, water supply, and a site logistics plan template.

> ⚠️ Cement bags stored on the ground absorb moisture from below within days. Dunnage — pallets, poles or a raised timber platform — is not optional, it is the difference between usable and wasted cement.

## 1. Laydown planning

A laydown area is not "wherever the truck stopped". Plan it on the site layout drawing before mobilisation.

**Principles.**
- **Locate by flow, not by convenience.** Material should move in one direction: delivery point → store/stack → work face. Every reverse movement is double handling, and double handling is where damage happens.
- **Keep it out of the permanent works footprint.** Material stacked on the future building platform will be moved at least twice. Use the future parking or apron area.
- **Level and drain it.** Falls away from stacks. Water ponding under a brick stack in February will destroy the bottom courses and undermine the stack.
- **Segregate by trade and by sequence.** A single mixed heap is unmanageable and invites pilferage.
- **Leave working aisles.** At least **3.5 m** between stacks for a tractor or telehandler, **1.2 m** for a wheelbarrow route.
- **Plan the vertical.** Stack heights are limited by stability and by what your lifting equipment can reach and place.

**Typical zone allocation for a small village building:**

| Zone | Contents | Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Secure store (container) | Ironmongery, taps, cable, tools, power tools, small plant | Lockable, alarmed if possible, inventory-controlled |
| Dry store (container or shed) | Cement, plaster, adhesives, paint, sealants | Raised floor, ventilated, shaded, stock-rotated |
| Covered stack | Timber, boards, doors, joinery | Roofed, ventilated, off ground, stickered |
| Open stack | Brick, block, aggregate, sand, rebar, scaffold | Drained, dunnaged, banded |
| Bulk bays | Sand, stone, spoil | Three-sided bays if possible; keeps materials from mixing |
| Fuel point | Diesel, petrol, LPG | Bunded, signed, away from everything else |
| Waste and salvage | Offcuts, packaging, scrap steel | Segregated; scrap steel has resale value and legs |
| Welfare | Shade, water, toilets, first aid | Adjacent to work face |

## 2. Protection against heat and dust

**Heat.** Sustained 35–40 °C affects material as well as people.
- **Direct sun degrades polymers.** Membranes, damp-proof courses, geotextiles, PVC pipe, cable and plastic sheeting embrittle and discolour with UV exposure. Store them under cover, not under a black tarpaulin (which cooks them), and keep them in their original wrapping until use.
- **Sealants, adhesives and paint have temperature-limited shelf lives.** A sealed shipping container in the Namibian sun can exceed 60 °C internally. Shade the container, ventilate it (roof vents and a door screen), or better, build a simple shade roof over it — a pole frame with IBR sheeting and a 300 mm ventilated air gap can drop internal temperature by 10–15 °C.
- **Timber dries and moves.** Timber delivered at 18 % moisture content will fall toward equilibrium quickly in this climate; unrestrained, it cups, twists and splits. See §4.
- **Concrete work.** High temperature accelerates set and increases plastic shrinkage cracking risk. Store aggregate and mixing water in shade; work early morning; keep cement cool.

**Dust.** Fine Kalahari dust penetrates everything.
- **Machinery.** Air filters clog fast; service intervals should be halved. Carry spare filters — a compressor or generator down for a filter is a stopped site.
- **Finishes.** Dust on a substrate destroys adhesion for paint, tile adhesive and sealants. Damp down before finishing work; keep finishing areas closed once glazed.
- **Electricals and electronics.** Seal distribution boards; keep laptops, survey instruments and inverters in sealed boxes with desiccant.
- **Stored goods.** Cover stacks. A dusty pallet of sheeting is not damaged, but a dusty pallet of white sanitaryware arrives at handover looking second-hand.
- **Suppression.** Damping the haul route and laydown with water reduces dust, tyre wear and complaints from neighbours. Budget the water.

## 3. Cement storage in humidity

Bagged cement is the material most likely to be wasted on a remote site, and the loss is invisible until the concrete underperforms.

**Failure mechanism.** Cement hydrates on contact with atmospheric moisture. Early stage: loose lumps that break under thumb pressure — strength already reduced. Later: hard lumps that will not break — the bag is scrap. In a humid February in Ohangwena, poorly stored bags can be compromised in weeks.

**Storage rules.**

1. **Off the ground, always.** Minimum **150 mm** clear, on pallets, poles or a timber platform. Never directly on a concrete slab either — concrete wicks moisture.
2. **Off the walls.** Leave a **300–500 mm** gap between stack and any wall or container skin, so condensation running down the wall does not reach the bags and so air can circulate.
3. **Under a roof, not under plastic.** A closed plastic sheet traps condensation against the bags. Use a ventilated roof; if plastic must be used, tent it with an air gap and open the ends.
4. **Stack height.** No more than **8 bags** high for extended storage (up to 12 for short periods) to avoid "warehouse pack" — compaction that makes lower bags set from pressure alone. `needs-verification`
5. **Rotate strictly: first in, first out.** Mark every pallet with the **delivery date and the bag's date of manufacture** on arrival. This single habit prevents most cement loss.
6. **Keep the stack tight and covered.** Small stacks with big surfaces lose most.
7. **Buy fresh.** Reject bags already old at the merchant. Ask for the manufacturing date at order stage, and write it into the purchase order as a condition.
8. **Do not over-buy against shelf life.** Where the safety-stock calculation in `01` demands more cement than can be used within the storage window, fix the lead-time variance instead.

**Silo option.** For any project consuming more than roughly 100 tonnes of cement, a **bulk cement silo** should be priced. Bulk cement is cheaper per tonne than bagged, it does not degrade in a sealed silo, it eliminates bag waste and hand offloading entirely, and it removes the single largest manual-handling injury source on site. The costs are the silo hire, a level compacted base, a pneumatic delivery tanker that must reach the silo, and a power supply.

**Field test before use.** Squeeze a handful. Free-flowing powder: good. Soft lumps that crumble: use for non-structural work only, at increased content. Hard lumps: discard. Record the decision — a rejected pallet is a claim against the supplier if it was old on delivery.

## 4. Timber stacking and stickering

Timber is the material most damaged by bad site storage in a hot dry climate.

**Rules.**
- **Off the ground on level bearers.** Bearers at maximum **1.2 m** centres for structural timber, **600–900 mm** for boards and thin sections. Bearers must be level and in line — a twisted set of bearers produces a twisted stack.
- **Sticker between layers.** Stickers (uniform battens, typically 19–25 mm) placed **directly above the bearers**, at the same spacing, in every layer. Misaligned stickers put point loads into the middle of boards and cause permanent bow.
- **Uniform sticker thickness.** Mixed sticker thickness = stepped stack = bent timber.
- **Roof it, ventilate it.** A shade roof with airflow. Direct sun causes surface checking and end splits; sealed plastic causes mould and staining.
- **Seal the ends** of high-value or wide sections with end-grain sealer or paint to slow differential drying and end-splitting.
- **Weight the top** of the stack, or restrain it with banding over a top bearer, to hold the upper boards flat.
- **Keep sheet material flat.** Plywood, OSB, gypsum board and fibre cement stack **flat, never on edge**, on a full-support platform. Board stored on edge for a week bows permanently.
- **Gypsum plasterboard needs a dry store.** It is the material most likely to be ruined by one rainstorm. If it cannot be stored dry, do not deliver it yet.
- **Joinery — doors, frames, cabinets — goes in a container, wrapped, upright and separated,** not in the open stack. Finished joinery arriving 700 km from the maker and then warping on site is a total loss.

**Acclimatisation.** Bring internal joinery and flooring to site and let it stand in the ventilated store, in the conditions it will live in, before fixing. This is often skipped on remote projects because the material arrives late; the resulting shrinkage gaps are then blamed on the joiner.

## 5. Theft and pilferage control

Remote sites are exposed: high-value material, few people, long response times, and a local market for anything portable.

**What goes.** In rough order: fuel; copper cable and pipe; tools and power tools; taps, mixers and other brass; small plant (grinders, drills, generators); cement (readily resold in a village); roof sheeting; timber; scrap steel; batteries and solar panels.

**A layered response.**

1. **Perimeter.** Fence the compound. Even a simple droppered fence with a single gate changes behaviour, because it converts casual removal into deliberate trespass.
2. **One gate, one route, one register.** Everything in and out is recorded. Publish that this is the rule on day one, before anything goes missing, so it is a system rather than an accusation.
3. **Container stores with proper locks.** Container-door lock boxes that shroud the padlock; a closed-shackle padlock. A hasp on a container door is defeated in seconds without a lock box.
4. **Issue against requisition.** Consumables and small items are issued in daily quantities, signed for by the foreman. Bulk pilferage on remote sites is nearly always continuous small-scale, not one dramatic break-in.
5. **Local, resident security.** A watchman who lives locally, is paid properly and on time, and is known in the village. Underpaid or externally imposed security is the least effective option and often the source of the leak.
6. **Community stake.** The strongest anti-theft measure on a village project is that the community regards the project as theirs. See `07_local-sourcing-and-community.md`. A clinic being built for the village is protected by the village; a contractor's compound is not.
7. **Design out the target.** Deliver copper, taps and sanitaryware in the last waves. Fit and commission promptly. Do not store six months of solar panels on an open site.
8. **Mark and record.** Photograph and serial-number all plant; paint-mark tools; record everything of value with serial numbers on arrival. Marked goods are harder to sell and easier to recover.
9. **Fuel is the highest-risk item.** Meter it, lock it, and reconcile daily (see §6).
10. **Investigate quietly and early.** A small, promptly noticed discrepancy handled discreetly stops the pattern. A large loss discovered at handover cannot be resolved at all.

## 6. Fuel storage

Remote sites store diesel for generators, plant, pumps and vehicles. It is the highest-risk stored substance on site — fire, environmental and theft risk together.

**Practice, and note that specific Namibian statutory thresholds are `needs-verification`:**

- **Establish the licensing threshold first.** Bulk fuel storage above a quantity threshold requires authorisation from the relevant Namibian authority (municipal/regional fire authority and the petroleum regulator) and may require an environmental clearance certificate. Confirm before ordering a bowser. `needs-verification`
- **Bunding.** Any bulk tank must sit in a bund with capacity of at least **110 % of the largest tank**, impermeable, with no drain to ground. A self-bunded (double-skinned) tank satisfies this and is the practical answer.
- **Siting.** Away from the site boundary, from accommodation, from stored combustibles and from any watercourse or borehole. Signed, with no smoking and no ignition sources, on firm level ground with vehicle access that does not require reversing past stacks.
- **Firefighting.** At least two dry-powder extinguishers (9 kg) at the fuel point, plus a bucket or bin of sand. Train the site on their use.
- **Spill kit.** Absorbent, drip trays under every dispensing point, and a plan for contaminated material.
- **Dispensing.** A hand or electric pump with a **meter**. Never siphon or decant by gravity into open containers.
- **Reconciliation.** Log every issue: date, plant/vehicle, hours or km, litres, signature. Reconcile tank dip against issues **daily**. Diesel disappears in the gaps between weekly checks.
- **Fuel quality.** Water and dust contamination will kill injectors. Keep the tank full to limit condensation, fit a water separator, and filter on transfer.
- **LPG cylinders** stored upright, outdoors, in a ventilated cage, away from the diesel point.

## 7. Water supply on site

Ohangwena has **84.3 %** household access to safe water, largely from the piped network fed from the Calueque–Oshakati scheme and from boreholes — but a construction site is not automatically connected, and a village supply may not tolerate construction demand.

**Demand.** A small building project needs water for concrete and mortar mixing, block curing (substantial — curing blocks consumes more water than making them), plaster, dust suppression, wheel and plant washing, and welfare. Curing is the item people underestimate.

**Options.**

| Source | Notes |
|---|---|
| Municipal / village connection | Best where available. Agree the metered rate, connection point and permitted draw with the village council **before** relying on it. Do not compete with domestic supply. |
| Existing borehole | Test yield and quality first. Groundwater in parts of northern Namibia is saline; saline mixing water damages concrete and corrodes reinforcement. |
| New borehole | Capital cost and permitting; only for larger or long-duration projects. Can be left as a legacy asset for the community. |
| Water bowser deliveries | Reliable but expensive and freight-bound; needs on-site storage. |
| Rainwater harvesting from the new roof | Seasonal, but once the roof is on it can supply curing water in Feb–Apr at near-zero cost, and the tanks stay as a community asset. |

**Storage.** Provide on-site storage sized for at least **3 days** of peak demand, so a pump failure or delivery delay does not stop concreting. Tanks on a raised, properly founded stand give gravity pressure without a pump. Cover tanks against dust, algae and mosquitoes.

**Quality.** Mixing water must be free of organics, oils, salts and excessive sulphates. The pragmatic field rule is that water fit to drink is fit to mix — but saline borehole water may be drinkable by local standards and still unsuitable for reinforced concrete. **Test it.** A chloride and sulphate test on the source is cheap relative to a corroded structure.

## 8. Site logistics plan template

A one-to-three page document, produced before mobilisation, updated monthly.

```
SITE LOGISTICS PLAN — [Project] — Rev [n] — [date]

1. SITE AND ACCESS
   1.1 Site address, GPS coordinates, what3words
   1.2 Route from the nearest tarred road: description, distance, surface
   1.3 Access restrictions: max vehicle, max mass, overhead clearance, seasonal
   1.4 Turning head: location and size
   1.5 Site contact: name, mobile, hours; alternate contact

2. DELIVERY MANAGEMENT
   2.1 Delivery calendar (wave dates, contents, cut-off dates)
   2.2 Booking procedure and notice period
   2.3 Permitted delivery hours; no unloading after dark
   2.4 Offloading method by material type; plant provided by whom
   2.5 Free offloading time and demurrage rate per contract
   2.6 Goods-inward inspection and rejection procedure

3. LAYDOWN AND STORAGE
   3.1 Site layout drawing reference showing all zones
   3.2 Zone schedule: contents, area, ground treatment, cover, security
   3.3 Stacking rules by material (heights, dunnage, stickering)
   3.4 Stock rotation rule and date-marking procedure
   3.5 Double-handling avoidance: material flow direction

4. SECURITY
   4.1 Perimeter, gate, gate register
   4.2 Secure store contents and key holders
   4.3 Issue-against-requisition procedure
   4.4 Security personnel: hours, reporting line, escalation
   4.5 High-risk item register (serial numbers, photographs)
   4.6 Loss reporting: to whom, within what time

5. UTILITIES AND CONSUMABLES
   5.1 Water: source, storage volume, daily demand estimate, quality test result
   5.2 Power: source (grid/generator/solar), capacity, fuel demand
   5.3 Fuel: tank type and volume, bund, dispensing, reconciliation, authorisation
   5.4 Communications: network coverage, satellite fallback, radio

6. WASTE AND HOUSEKEEPING
   6.1 Waste streams and segregation
   6.2 Removal route and frequency; backload opportunities
   6.3 Salvage and reuse policy; what is offered to the community

7. WELFARE AND ACCOMMODATION
   7.1 Accommodation location; catering; potable water
   7.2 Sanitation provision and servicing
   7.3 First aid; nearest medical facility and travel time; evacuation plan

8. EMERGENCY AND CONTINGENCY
   8.1 Vehicle recovery: who, what equipment, contact, cost
   8.2 Plant breakdown: critical spares held on site
   8.3 Rainy season plan: last heavy delivery date, wet-season stock, work plan
   8.4 Medical evacuation route and airstrip use

9. RESPONSIBILITIES
   9.1 Logistics owner (name); authority to refuse off-calendar deliveries
   9.2 Storekeeper (name); duties
   9.3 Review frequency and distribution list
```
