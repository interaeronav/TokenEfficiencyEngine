# The last mile and site access on remote sites

**Summary.** The last mile is where remote logistics actually fails. A 34-tonne interlink can reach Eenhana or Okongo on tar without difficulty; it cannot necessarily reach a homestead 12 km down a sand track, cannot turn round in a village lane, and cannot offload itself. This file covers unsealed road behaviour in Cuvelai-basin sand, seasonal impassability, vehicle selection for the final leg, load restrictions, offloading without a crane, the access preparation that should be done in week one, and how to plan deliveries onto a site with no hard standing.

## Key facts

| Item | Value | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Main rainy season | **February–April** | Smaller season Sep–Nov |
| North-eastern Namibia annual rainfall | **~600 mm** | Highest in the country |
| Ohangwena rural share of population | **85.5 %** | Dispersed homesteads, informal tracks |
| Eenhana → Okongo | **~120 km, tarred** | Continues toward Nkurenkuru |
| Interlink turning circle | **~22–25 m** kerb-to-kerb | Needs a formed turning head |
| Rigid 8 t truck turning circle | **~14–16 m** | Realistic in a village |
| Nearest weighbridges | **Oshakati**, Grootfontein | Roads Authority |
| Okongo airstrip | present, **small aircraft only** | Emergency personnel/parts, not cargo |

> ⚠️ Never send a full interlink to an unproven access without a physical reconnaissance. The recovery cost of a bogged 34-tonne combination in wet Cuvelai sand exceeds the cost of splitting the load into three smaller vehicles many times over.

## 1. The two surfaces you will meet

**Gravel (graded, engineered).** District roads under the Roads Authority: a formed carriageway, some camber, side drains, and periodic blading. Behaves well when dry and recently graded. Problems: corrugation (which shakes fixings loose, cracks sanitaryware and fatigues sheeting bundles), loose material on curves, dust, and washboarding on gradients. A loaded interlink can normally use a maintained gravel road but will travel at 40–60 km/h, not 80.

**Sand (unformed track).** The access from the district road to a village site is often an unengineered two-track through Kalahari sand. This is a different problem entirely:
- **Dry, loose sand** is worst for heavy vehicles: traction fails, and a laden rigid can bog on a flat surface with no rain involved.
- **Wet sand immediately after rain can actually be firmer** than dry sand — but the surrounding *oshana* depressions fill and the track can become an impassable pan.
- **Ruts self-deepen.** The third heavy vehicle over a soft track does far more damage than the first.

**Cuvelai specifics.** The *oshanas* — shallow, seasonally inundated depressions — define drainage across north-central Namibia. Their key logistics property is that they are **flat**: water does not run away, it sits. Communications are routinely hindered during the rainy season. A route that is a mild dip in September is a 300 m water crossing in March.

## 2. Seasonal planning

Build the delivery calendar around the season, not the other way round.

| Period | Condition | Delivery policy |
|---|---|---|
| **May – August** (dry, cool) | Best. Firm ground, low temperatures, long working days | **Bring everything heavy.** Bulk cement, aggregate, brick, steel, plant |
| **September – November** (hot, small rains) | Hot (up to ~40 °C), occasional storms, dust | Continue heavy deliveries; manage heat effects on labour and concrete |
| **December – January** (hot, building rains) | Deteriorating | Last window for heavy loads. Complete roof cover |
| **February – April** (main rains) | Worst. Tracks soft or flooded, oshanas full | **Plan for zero heavy deliveries.** Light vehicles only. Work off stock |
| Transition weeks | Unpredictable | Decide day by day; keep a light-vehicle fallback |

The corresponding programme logic: **get the building weather-tight and the site stocked before February.** A remote project that reaches the rains with the roof on and 8 weeks of finishes material on site will keep working through them. One that reaches the rains still needing block deliveries will stop.

## 3. Vehicle selection for the last leg

| Access condition | Recommended | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Tar to site boundary | Interlink 30–34 t | — |
| Maintained gravel, dry | Interlink 30–34 t, reduced speed | — |
| Maintained gravel, wet | Tri-axle rigid ~10–15 t or split loads | Full interlink |
| Formed sand track, dry | 6×4 or 6×6 rigid, 8–10 t, high flotation tyres | Interlink; low-slung trailers |
| Unformed sand, dry | 4×4 rigid, ≤6 t, tyres deflated to ~1.5–1.8 bar | Anything two-wheel-drive |
| Wet sand / oshana crossing | **Do not deliver.** 4×4 LDV only, essentials | Everything else |
| Very long or heavy single item | Low-bed with route survey and prepared access | Improvisation |

**Practical notes.**
- **Tyre pressure is the cheapest traction tool there is.** A driver who will not deflate on sand and re-inflate on tar should not be on the job. Carry a compressor.
- **Two-wheel-drive is the usual failure.** Most commercial rigids in the region are 4×2 or 6×4 without cross-axle diff locks; they will bog where a 6×6 walks through.
- **Never send the only vehicle.** For the first delivery down a new access, send a recovery-capable vehicle (a tractor, a grader, or a 4×4 with a snatch strap and a rated recovery point) in company.
- **Split at the tar.** Transhipping 30 t into three 10 t loads at a hard standing on the tarred road is a known, priced operation. Recovering a bogged interlink is not.

## 4. Load restrictions

Three separate restriction regimes apply and they are often confused.

1. **Statutory axle and mass limits** — Namibian permissible GVM 56 000 kg; tandem axle unit 24 000 kg; single non-steering wheel 4 000 kg; bridge formula `P = 2 100 L + 18 000`. Enforced at weighbridges including **Oshakati** and **Grootfontein** in the north. These apply on every public road regardless of surface.
2. **Posted or administrative restrictions** — some district roads, low-level crossings and small bridges carry lower posted limits, and some are closed seasonally by the roads authority or the regional council. Check before dispatch; a posted limit is not negotiable and a driver who ignores it invalidates insurance.
3. **Practical bearing capacity** — the real limit on an unformed track, which no sign states. Assessed by reconnaissance: probe the surface, look at what local vehicles use, ask the community, and look at rut depth from previous heavy traffic.

**Culverts and low-level crossings** are the specific hazard. A concrete pipe culvert built for light traffic will collapse under a 24 t tandem. Where a route crosses one, either verify its design capacity or bridge it temporarily with a spread of railway sleepers, heavy timber or steel plate to distribute the load.

## 5. Offloading without a crane

Most remote sites have no crane, no forklift, and no hard standing. Plan the offload before the load leaves.

**Options, best to worst:**

1. **Vehicle-mounted crane (HIAB / brick crane).** The single best answer for palletised material — bricks, blocks, bagged cement, sheeting bundles. Reach is typically 6–10 m at reduced capacity; the truck must stand on firm ground within reach of the stack. **Specify it in the freight order** and pay for it; do not hope the transporter provides one.
2. **Truck-mounted forklift (Moffett-type, piggyback).** Carried on the rear of the trailer, dismounts, and can travel short distances on firm ground. Excellent for pallets; poor on soft sand.
3. **Rough-terrain telehandler hired to site.** Justified when the project has weeks of lifting to do. Hire cost is high but it also serves masonry, roof and plant work. On a village project, a telehandler for the roof phase often pays for itself.
4. **Tractor with front-end loader or pallet forks.** Frequently the most realistic local option: agricultural tractors exist in the region, operators exist, and a tractor tows a bogged truck as a bonus. Capacity is modest (typically under 1.5 t on forks) and stability on slopes is poor.
5. **Tipping.** Sand, aggregate and spoil should simply be tipped. Ensure the tipper has room and firm ground; a tipper raising its body on soft or cambered ground can roll.
6. **Ramp and roll / skid.** Heavy single items (tanks, machinery) can be skidded down heavy timber ramps with a winch. Slow, needs planning, needs competent supervision.
7. **Hand offloading.** The default fallback and often the only one. It works for bags, loose brick, small sections and boxes. Its costs are real: time (a 600-bag cement load is a 3–5 hour hand offload for a small gang), breakage, and injury risk.

> ⚠️ Manual handling: repeated lifting of 50 kg cement bags causes back injury. Use a two-person lift or a chute/roller, rotate the gang, work in the cool of the day, and never hand-offload onto uneven ground.

**Demurrage.** Freight contracts allow limited free offloading time (often 2–4 hours) and charge thereafter. A hand offload of a full load can exceed the free time. Either negotiate a longer free period for remote deliveries, or have the gang, the plant and the stack area ready before the vehicle arrives.

## 6. Site access preparation — do it in week one

Access preparation is the highest-return spend on a remote project. It is cheap, it happens once, and every subsequent delivery benefits.

**The week-one access package:**

1. **Reconnaissance and route survey.** Drive the full route in a light vehicle. Record: surface type by section, width, overhead clearances (trees, power lines), culverts and crossings with an assessment, soft spots, turning opportunities, and any point where a long vehicle cannot pass.
2. **Widening and clearing.** Cut back vegetation to give at least **4.0 m clear width** and **4.5 m clear height** for the full route. Trees that scratch paint also tear tautliner curtains and break sheeting bundles.
3. **Soft-spot treatment.** Import gravel or laterite to the worst 50–200 m. In Cuvelai sand, a **150–200 mm compacted gravel layer** transforms a track. Where gravel is unavailable, geotextile plus imported material, or a corduroy of poles, will get one season's use.
4. **A turning head.** A formed circle or hammerhead large enough for the largest planned vehicle. **This is non-negotiable** — reversing an articulated vehicle 800 m down a sand track in the dark is how deliveries get abandoned.
5. **A hard standing for offloading.** See below.
6. **Drainage.** A single mitre drain and a shallow side drain can keep a section usable through the rains. Never let water pond on the access.
7. **Signage and a gate.** A sign at the turn-off from the district road with the project name and a phone number, plus a controlled gate. Drivers who cannot find the turn-off phone at 22:00 or give up.
8. **A written access instruction** issued with every delivery order: GPS coordinates, the turn-off description, vehicle restrictions, the site contact and hours, and the offloading arrangement.

**Indicative cost.** A basic access package — 400 m of clearing, 150 m of imported gravel, a turning head and a 15 × 20 m hard standing — is typically a few days of a grader, a tipper and a roller plus material. Against the cost of one abandoned 30 t delivery plus recovery plus re-delivery, it pays back on the first or second load.

## 7. Delivering onto a site with no hard standing

If the offloading area is soft sand, everything above fails. Build a hard standing before the first heavy delivery.

**Minimum specification for a delivery and laydown hard standing:**

- **Size:** at least **15 m × 20 m**, sited so a rigid can enter, offload and leave without reversing far. Larger if a telehandler will work there.
- **Construction:** strip topsoil and organics; place and compact **150–250 mm** of graded gravel/laterite or crushed material in two layers; compact each layer with a roller or plate. Where the subgrade is very poor, lay a geotextile separator first — it prevents the imported layer from punching into the sand and roughly doubles its life.
- **Falls:** 1:50 to 1:100 away from the stack area so water sheds.
- **Edge restraint:** a shallow bund or a line of blocks so the layer does not spread.
- **Alternatives where gravel is scarce:** compacted crusher run; stabilised sand (sand plus 4–6 % cement, mixed and compacted — often viable because sand is the one material Ohangwena has in abundance); steel road plates or heavy timber mats for a short-term point load; interlocking plastic ground-protection mats hired in.

**Sequencing tip.** The hard standing is usually in the same place as the eventual apron, parking or access road. Build it once as a subbase, use it for the whole job, and surface it at the end. That converts a temporary cost into a permanent asset.

## 8. Delivery-day checklist

Issue this with every heavy delivery.

- [ ] Route confirmed passable within the last 48 h (phone the local contact after rain)
- [ ] Vehicle type and payload confirmed against the access assessment
- [ ] Offloading method confirmed: crane on truck / Moffett / telehandler / tractor / hand
- [ ] Offloading gang booked and on site, with water and PPE
- [ ] Stack area prepared: level, drained, dunnage laid, covers ready
- [ ] Turning head clear of parked vehicles and material
- [ ] Delivery note, bar schedule or packing list in hand for checking **before** the vehicle leaves
- [ ] Camera ready to photograph damage on arrival
- [ ] Site contact reachable; number given to the driver and the transport office
- [ ] Free offloading time and demurrage rate known
- [ ] Recovery plan: who to call, what equipment is on site, what it costs
- [ ] Arrival window in daylight; no unloading of heavy material after dark
