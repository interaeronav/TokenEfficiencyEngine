# Construction logistics in remote areas — overview

**Summary.** On a remote site, logistics stops being an administrative function and becomes the dominant constraint on cost, programme and quality. The same brick that costs N$4 in a Windhoek yard can land at N$7 in Okongo; the same 3-day lead time becomes 3 weeks; and a single forgotten box of fixings can idle a crew for a fortnight. This domain uses **Okongo, Ohangwena Region, northern Namibia** as its working case — a village of about 3 564 people, roughly 120 km east of Eenhana on a tarred road toward Nkurenkuru, roughly 1 000 km by road from Walvis Bay and about 660 km from Windhoek. The core discipline is: convert *distance* into *money and time* explicitly, buy in fewer and larger consignments, buffer against variance rather than against average demand, and make the last 30 km of unsealed road a design input rather than a surprise.

> ⚠️ Overloading is enforced. Under the Roads Authority regime an excess under 2 tonnes attracts an on-the-spot fine and detention until the load is redistributed; over 2 tonnes goes to court and the vehicle is impounded. A "just squeeze it on" instruction to a driver can strand a full load 400 km from site.

## 1. Why cost behaves differently

Three effects compound.

**Freight is a step function, not a linear one.** A 34-tonne payload interlink costs roughly the same to send to Okongo whether it carries 34 tonnes or 9. So the cost *per unit delivered* falls steeply with consignment size and then flattens. On a metropolitan site this barely matters because a merchant absorbs it into a delivery charge. At 1 000 km it dominates: the difference between four part-loads and one full load can be 30–40 % of the delivered price of heavy, low-value goods.

**Value density decides everything.** Divide the ex-works value of a material by its mass. Cement at, say, N$2 000/tonne has low value density; a consignment of ironmongery at N$400 000/tonne has very high value density. Freight adds a large *percentage* to the first and a negligible one to the second. The practical rule: **make or buy the low-value-density items as close to site as possible; import the high-value-density items freely.** This is why block-making on site is often defensible in Ohangwena while making your own door furniture never is.

**Wastage and damage are freight costs in disguise.** A pallet of face brick that arrives with 8 % breakage has effectively raised its delivered price by 8 %, and — worse — may trigger a re-order whose freight cost is a whole extra trip. Remote sites should carry higher wastage allowances than urban ones and should buy in a way that makes the marginal top-up unnecessary.

## 2. Why time behaves differently

Urban construction runs on *replenishment*: the merchant is 20 minutes away, so lead time is short and its variance is small. Remote construction runs on *campaigns*: material arrives in waves, and between waves you have what you have.

The consequence is that **lead-time variance, not lead-time length, is the real enemy**. A supplier who reliably takes 21 days is easy to plan around. A supplier who takes "7 to 28 days" forces you to hold three weeks of buffer stock or accept a three-week programme risk. Most remote-site delay is caused not by long lead times but by unquantified ones.

Second, **the rainy season is a hard gate**. In the Cuvelai system of north-central Namibia the seasonal *oshanas* fill and the unsealed network degrades; Namibia's main rainy season runs February to April with a smaller one September to November. Any heavy delivery — cement, bulk aggregate, roof trusses, plant — should be planned to land before February wherever the programme allows, and the site should be weather-tight before it.

Third, **cross-border and corridor time is lumpy**. Corridor transit times are published as ranges (Walvis Bay–Johannesburg 2 days, Walvis Bay–Lubumbashi 6–7 days) and those ranges assume the border behaves. Customs, weighbridge queues and a single mechanical failure each add a day or more with no warning.

## 3. Why risk behaves differently

On a remote site the ordinary risks of construction acquire a multiplier because **recovery is slow**. The same event — a rejected batch of blocks, a burnt-out compressor, a theft of copper — costs days in a city and weeks in a village. That changes optimal behaviour in specific ways:

- **Carry spares for anything that stops work.** A second water pump costs less than four idle days.
- **Prefer repairable technology.** A vibrating poker that can be fixed in Ondangwa beats a sealed unit that must go to Windhoek.
- **Inspect at dispatch, not at delivery.** A rejection discovered on site is a whole freight cycle lost; a rejection discovered in the supplier's yard is a phone call.
- **Assume the cheapest supplier is the least reliable.** On a remote site, reliability is worth paying for, because the cost of failure is asymmetric.

## 5. The five decisions that determine remote-site logistics cost

1. **Consolidation policy** — how many deliveries the whole project will receive. Usually the single largest lever (see `01`).
2. **Make-or-import boundary** — which materials are produced within 30 km of site and which are freighted (see `06`, `07`).
3. **Staging strategy** — whether a depot is held in Oshakati/Ondangwa as a buffer between the long haul and the last mile (see `01`, `03`).
4. **Access preparation** — what is spent up front on the approach road, hard standing and offloading provision (see `03`).
5. **Buffer sizing** — how much safety stock and float is deliberately purchased against lead-time variance (see `01`, `05`).

Everything else — negotiating rates, chasing orders, expediting — operates within the envelope those five decisions set.

## 6. The northern Namibian supply picture in one paragraph

Northern Namibia is better supplied than its remoteness suggests. There are builders' merchants across the Cuvelai settlements — Oshakati, Ongwediva, Ondangwa, Eenhana, Outapi, Oshikango, Omuthiya — and a Build it branch **in Okongo itself**. Cement is produced domestically at Otavi (Ohorongo, ~0.7 Mt/yr, commissioned December 2010) and by Cheetah Cement, so it does not have to be imported. Steel merchant capability, including cut-and-bend, exists as close as Ondangwa. What the region does **not** have is specialist stock, fabrication, joinery of controlled quality, glazing, plant hire depth, or design services; those come from Windhoek, Walvis Bay or South Africa. The practical shape of a project in Okongo is therefore: **bulk and commodity locally or regionally, specialist and value-dense from the south in consolidated loads, and nothing at all improvised in the rainy season.**
