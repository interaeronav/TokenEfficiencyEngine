# Lessons from humanitarian, military, mining and expedition logistics

**Summary.** Four disciplines routinely solve harder versions of the remote construction logistics problem: **humanitarian relief** (UN/WFP, the Logistics Cluster, UNHRD, the Red Cross movement), **military engineering logistics**, **mining camp resupply**, and **polar and expedition resupply**. Each has evolved practices under conditions where failure is not merely expensive, and most of those practices transfer directly to a village construction site. This file extracts the transferable ones: capacity assessment before deployment, classification of supply, kitting, pre-positioning, the pipeline concept, common services, discipline about the last mile, and the resupply-window mentality.

## Key facts

| Source discipline | Anchor facts |
|---|---|
| **Logistics Cluster** | Established by the Inter-Agency Standing Committee; **WFP is lead agency**; coordinates from **Rome**; produces **Logistics Capacity Assessments** of infrastructure in vulnerable countries; provides coordination, information management, training and common services |
| **UNHRD** | A network of **five global hubs**; offers **free storage** and rapid access to emergency supplies, technical assistance, training, temperature-sensitive logistics and supply-chain advisory |
| **Humanitarian logistics** | Three flows — **material, information, financial**; four actors — donors, aid organisations, local governments, beneficiaries; four warehouse types — general delivery, slow rotation, quick rotation, temporary collection; transport contracting by **tonne/km, exclusive vehicle use, or daily rate** |
| **Military supply classes** | **I** rations and water; **II** clothing, tentage, tools; **III** POL (fuel, oil, lubricants); **IV** **construction materials, fortification and barrier materials**; **V** ammunition; **VI** personal demand; **VII** major end items; **VIII** medical; **IX** repair parts; **X** non-military/development materiel |
| **Operation Deep Freeze** | Antarctic resupply runs **late September to early March**; ski-equipped **LC-130** aircraft flown by the 109th Airlift Wing, staging from **Christchurch**; icebreaker support including **USCGC Polar Star**; 2021–22 delivered ~100 personnel and nearly 50 000 lb of food by air to McMurdo |
| **Sphere Handbook** | Minimum standards in humanitarian response across water and sanitation, shelter, food, nutrition and health; first published 2000, fourth edition 2018 |

## 1. Humanitarian logistics — UN/WFP, the Logistics Cluster and the Red Cross

### 1.1 Assess the capacity before you commit to a plan

The Logistics Cluster's **Logistics Capacity Assessment** is a standing survey of a country's ports, airports, roads, rail, warehousing, fuel and customs — compiled *before* an emergency so that responders are not discovering the road network under time pressure.

**Transferable practice.** Produce a one-off **project logistics capacity assessment** at feasibility stage, before design is fixed:
- Ports and points of entry, and their realistic throughput.
- Road network to site by class and surface, with seasonal restrictions.
- Bridges, culverts and their capacities on the final approach.
- Available vehicle types and operators, with real payloads.
- Warehousing available for hire between the source and the site.
- Fuel availability and quality along the route.
- Customs, permitting and weighbridge points.
- Local supply capacity: what can genuinely be bought within 30, 100 and 300 km.
- Labour and plant availability.

This document costs a few days and reshapes design decisions. Most remote construction failures are traceable to it never having been written.

### 1.2 Pre-position

UNHRD holds stock in **five hubs** so that response begins from a depot rather than from a purchase order. Humanitarian pre-positioning explicitly estimates required quantities using **safety stock levels** and then chooses **optimal warehouse locations**.

**Transferable practice.** The staging depot argument in `01_supply-chain-design.md` is the construction version of this. The additional humanitarian insight is that pre-positioning is a **portfolio decision across projects**, not a per-project one: a contractor running three projects in northern Namibia should hold a shared depot in Ondangwa, where none of the three could justify one alone.

### 1.3 Kitting and standard modules

Relief operations ship **standard kits** — a family shelter kit, a hygiene kit, a water treatment kit — rather than lists of individual items. The kit is packed once, verified once, and can be issued without a picking exercise in the field.

**Transferable practice.** Kit construction material by work package, not by trade or by supplier:
- A **"door kit"** — door, frame, hinges, lock, handle, screws, sealant, architrave — banded, labelled and numbered per opening.
- A **"window kit"** per opening.
- A **"WC kit"** — pan, cistern, seat, connector, isolating valve, fixings, sealant.
- A **"roof bay kit"** — sheets, purlin cleats, screws, washers, closures, flashing pieces.

Kitting eliminates the single most destructive remote-site failure mode: **the missing small item**. A N$40 packet of screws stopping a N$400 000 roof for three weeks is entirely normal on remote sites and entirely avoidable by kitting.

### 1.4 The three flows

Humanitarian logistics models **material flow, information flow and financial flow** as equally important. Remote construction habitually manages the first, neglects the second and is surprised by the third.

**Transferable practice.**
- **Information flow:** a single delivery tracker visible to site, buying office and client, updated at dispatch and at arrival. Consignment numbering (wave/consignment/package) applied physically to every package.
- **Financial flow:** cash-flow modelling against the delivery calendar (see `08`), and supplier credit limits raised *before* the wave that will breach them.

### 1.5 Common services

The Logistics Cluster's core idea is **common services**: rather than every agency contracting its own trucks and warehouses, one actor provides shared transport, storage and coordination.

**Transferable practice.** Where several projects, contractors or subcontractors operate in the same region, share the logistics: joint transport bookings, a shared depot, a shared plant pool, a shared fuel bowser, joint recovery arrangements. On donor-funded rural programmes this is often the single biggest available saving and is almost never done, because each project is contracted separately. The fix is at procurement strategy level — make shared logistics a contractual feature of a multi-site programme.

### 1.6 Minimum standards for the people, not only the materiel

The **Sphere Handbook** sets minimum standards for water, sanitation, shelter, food and health in humanitarian response. Construction camps in remote areas frequently fall below what humanitarian practice would consider a minimum for a displaced population.

**Transferable practice.** Set explicit, written minimum standards for the site camp — potable water per person per day, sanitation provision and servicing frequency, sleeping area per person, shade and rest provision, food, first aid, and a medical evacuation plan — and audit them. Labour reliability, which is a logistics risk (see `08`), is directly downstream of this.

### 1.7 Tracking

Humanitarian supply chains use barcodes, RFID and card systems for **end-to-end visibility**, on the reasoning that transparency is what lets everyone downstream plan.

**Transferable practice.** Number every consignment and every package; photograph loads at dispatch and at arrival; keep one tracker. Low-tech versions work: a printed label, a WhatsApp photograph of the loaded truck, and a shared spreadsheet deliver most of the benefit.

## 2. Military engineering logistics

### 2.1 Classify supply

Military logistics classifies everything into **Classes I–X**: rations and water (I), clothing and tools (II), fuel and lubricants (III), **construction materials (IV)**, ammunition (V), personal demand items (VI), major end items (VII), medical (VIII), repair parts (IX) and non-military programme materiel (X).

**Transferable practice.** The value is not the specific classes but the **habit of classifying by resupply behaviour**, because each class needs a different replenishment rule:

| Construction analogue | Behaviour | Replenishment rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Consumed continuously** (cement, fuel, water, food) | Predictable rate | Reorder point + safety stock |
| **Consumed in a burst** (roof sheeting, glazing) | One event | Delivered to a wave, just before the event |
| **Durable, must not fail** (plant, tools) | Fails randomly | Held with spares and redundancy |
| **Repair parts** | Fails randomly, low value, long lead | **Held on site, always** |
| **Personal / welfare** | Continuous, morale-critical | Never allowed to run out |

The military insight worth stealing outright is **Class IX**: repair parts get their own class because they are cheap, low-volume and completely determine whether everything else works. Remote construction sites systematically under-stock them.

### 2.2 Push and pull

Military logistics distinguishes **push** (higher command sends a standard package on a schedule) from **pull** (the unit requisitions what it needs). Push is used when demand is predictable or communications are poor; pull is used when demand is specific and the channel is reliable.

**Transferable practice.** Run a **hybrid**: push a standard package on every wave — consumables, fixings, PPE, fuel, filters, spares, welfare items — regardless of whether site asked for it, and let site pull the specific items. The standard push package is cheap, prevents the small-item stoppage, and removes the requirement for site to remember everything before a cut-off.

### 2.3 Basic loads and days of supply

Units carry a **basic load**: a defined quantity of each supply class, expressed in **days of supply**, held forward and replenished to that level.

**Transferable practice.** Express remote site stock in **days of cover**, not in value or quantity, and set a target basic load per material: "cement 14 days, fuel 10 days, fixings 30 days, filters 60 days". This is instantly legible to everyone and makes the weekly logistics report a single line per item.

### 2.4 Reconnaissance and route classification

Military engineers formally classify routes — width, surface, bridge classification, overhead clearance, choke points — and no significant move is made over an unclassified route.

**Transferable practice.** The route survey in `03_last-mile-and-site-access.md` is this practice. Formalise it: a written route card carried by every driver, with the turn-off, the restrictions, the choke points, the site contact and the recovery plan.

### 2.5 Redundancy in the critical few

Military planning identifies single points of failure and duplicates them. **Transferable practice:** two water pumps, two generators, two vehicles capable of reaching site, two people trained on every critical task, two suppliers on account for every commodity.

## 3. Mining camp resupply

Remote mining and exploration camps run continuous, high-volume resupply into sites with worse access than most construction projects. The transferable practices are consistent across the industry, though specific figures below are general practice rather than sourced. `needs-verification`

- **The camp is a project in its own right.** Accommodation, catering, water, power, waste and medical are planned, resourced and managed as a distinct workstream with its own budget and manager — not as an afterthought of the construction contract. On remote construction projects the camp is usually under-resourced, and labour turnover follows.
- **Fixed resupply cycles.** A camp runs a weekly or fortnightly resupply convoy on a published schedule. Everything is ordered against it. This is exactly the delivery-calendar discipline of `01`, applied to consumables as well as materials.
- **Standardise the fleet.** One make and model of light vehicle, one of generator, one of pump, means one set of spares and one skill set. Remote sites that accumulate mixed plant accumulate mixed spares and end up with none of the right ones.
- **Backload discipline.** No vehicle returns empty: it takes out waste, scrap, damaged goods, returns and empty containers. This halves the effective cost of waste removal and is very frequently ignored on construction projects, which pay for one-way freight in both directions.
- **Fuel is managed as a system.** Metered, reconciled, quality-controlled, with defined minimum stock. It is the highest-consequence consumable on any remote site.
- **Rosters and travel are logistics.** People movement is scheduled with the same rigour as materials. A crew stranded because a flight or a vehicle was not booked is the same failure as a missing delivery.
- **Medical and evacuation planned in advance.** A named facility, a route, a travel time, a communications plan, and a trained first-aider. (Okongo is unusual and fortunate here: it has a 62-bed district hospital in the village, plus an airstrip for small aircraft.)

## 4. Antarctic and expedition resupply

Polar resupply is the extreme case: a **single annual window**, no possibility of a top-up, and total failure if something is forgotten. Operation Deep Freeze runs the austral summer, **late September to early March**, using ski-equipped **LC-130** aircraft and icebreaker-escorted sealift.

**Transferable practices:**

- **The window mentality.** When resupply is impossible outside a window, planning quality rises dramatically. Northern Namibia has a soft version of the same constraint — the February–April rains. Treat the pre-rain period as a **hard resupply window** and plan with polar seriousness: what must be on site before it closes, and what happens if something is missing.
- **Complete-to-the-last-screw manifesting.** Polar programmes manifest every item, because there is no shop. The construction equivalent is a **complete bill of materials including consumables, fixings, spares and tools** — the categories habitually left off a bill of quantities and habitually the cause of stoppages.
- **Redundancy on anything life- or mission-critical.** Two of everything that cannot fail.
- **Cargo staged and packed months ahead**, at a marshalling point (Christchurch for McMurdo), inspected and consolidated before the window opens. The construction analogue is the consolidation yard, loaded and checked before the wave, not on the morning of dispatch.
- **Winter-over planning.** Polar stations plan explicitly for the period when nothing can arrive: what work is done, what stock is held, what fails gracefully. **Write a wet-season plan for the site with the same explicitness** — a declared list of work that can proceed, stock held to support it, and an accepted reduction in output rather than a pretence of normal working.
- **Debrief and improve.** Each season's manifest is reviewed against what was actually consumed, and the next manifest is corrected. Construction almost never does this. **Keep a "what we ran out of" log** and use it to write the next project's push package.
