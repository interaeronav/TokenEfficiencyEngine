# Local sourcing and community engagement in rural Namibia

**Summary.** In a rural Ohangwena setting, some materials and nearly all unskilled and semi-skilled labour can genuinely be sourced within 30 km of site — sand, water, labour, block-making, some timber and poles, and increasingly aggregate through small local crushing operations. What comes with local sourcing is an informal supply sector with no test certificates, no consistent grading and no contractual recourse, so quality control moves from *checking paperwork* to *testing material and supervising process*. Alongside that sits an equally practical requirement: a village project succeeds or fails partly on its relationship with the traditional authority, the village council and the community, and that relationship is a logistics asset — it determines site security, labour supply, land access and whether people help or obstruct.

## 1. What can genuinely be sourced locally

### Sand

**Available and abundant.** The Cuvelai system is sand. What is *available* and what is *suitable* are different questions.

- **Aeolian (wind-blown) Kalahari sand** — extremely uniform, fine, rounded grains, very poorly graded. It is the sand you will be offered. Used alone it makes weak, harsh, high-shrinkage mortar and poor concrete, because there is no coarse fraction to interlock and no fines variety to fill voids.
- **Oshana / pan sands** may carry silt, clay and organic matter.
- **River or borrow-pit sand** with a broader grading is the material you want, and it is worth travelling 20–40 km for.

**Controls:** a field settling test (sand in a jar of water, shaken, left 1 hour — a silt layer above 6–8 % of the sand depth is a warning), a sieve analysis on the first load from each source, an organic-impurities check, and a chloride test where the source is near saline groundwater. Blending a fine local sand with a coarser imported fraction, or with crusher dust, is usually cheaper than importing all the sand.

### Aggregate

**Partially available.** There is no large commercial quarry in the immediate area. Options are:
- **Imported crushed stone** from an established quarry — high freight cost, known grading, test certificates.
- **Local small-scale crushing** — sometimes available; grading and cleanliness are variable.
- **Calcrete/laterite** — widely used for sub-base and fill and often locally winnable; unsuitable as structural concrete aggregate.

For structural concrete, the honest answer is usually **import the coarse aggregate and win the sand locally**, blending to a designed grading. For fill, sub-base and hardstanding, local material with compaction control is entirely appropriate.

### Water

Ohangwena has **84.3 %** household access to safe water, largely from the piped network. For a construction site, the practical sources are a metered connection agreed with the village council, an existing borehole (test for salinity and sulphate), bowser deliveries, or rainwater harvested off the new roof. Do not simply draw from a community standpipe without an agreement — competing with domestic supply is the fastest way to lose community goodwill. See `04_site-logistics-and-storage.md`.

### Labour

**The strongest local resource.** With 85.5 % of the region rural and only about a quarter of households on wages, there is a large, willing pool for general labour, block-making, excavation, and — with supervision — bricklaying, carpentry and finishing. Regionally there are also trained artisans working out of Oshakati, Ondangwa and Eenhana.

What is usually **not** available locally: qualified electricians and plumbers with registration, welders with coded qualifications, surveyors, formwork specialists, and experienced site supervisors. Plan to import supervision and specialist trades, and to train local labour under them.

### Local block yards

Small block-making operations exist across the north and are the default masonry source. They vary enormously. Diagnostic questions and checks:

- **Cement content.** Ask bags per batch and blocks per bag. More than ~35 blocks from a 50 kg bag of cement is a warning sign for structural work.
- **Curing.** Ask to see the curing area. Blocks cured in the sun without water for two days are weak whatever the mix. Look for shade, covers and evidence of watering.
- **Age at sale.** Blocks sold at 3–5 days old have not developed strength. Insist on a minimum age.
- **Dimensional consistency.** Measure ten blocks. Variation over ±3 mm makes coursing and plaster thickness a nightmare.
- **Test.** Buy a sample batch and have blocks crushed at a laboratory (Oshakati/Windhoek) before committing.
- **Capacity.** A yard producing 300 blocks/day cannot supply a project needing 800.

### Timber

**Limited but real.** Local sources are poles and rough sawn material from the woodland belt in eastern Ohangwena and the Kavango, plus timber merchants in Eenhana, Ondangwa and Oshakati. Local poles are appropriate for fencing, scaffolding (with care), shuttering supports, and traditional construction. Local rough timber is **not** appropriate for structural roof members: it is ungraded, untreated, of unknown moisture content and unknown species. Structural timber should be graded and preservative-treated to the correct hazard class, which in practice means buying from a merchant with certification.

**Also worth checking:** eucalyptus and pine plantation off-cuts, and locally made rough furniture and joinery for non-critical items.

### Other genuinely local items

Gravel and calcrete for access roads and hard standing; labour-based road maintenance; local transport (a tractor and trailer, an LDV); catering and accommodation; security; and — for finishes — locally made items such as burglar bars, gates and simple steel windows from a village welder, where the specification tolerates them.

## 2. The quality-control problem with informal supply

Formal supply chains manage quality with **documents**: SANS marks, NRCS Letters of Authority, test certificates, batch numbers, warranties. Informal supply has none of these, and demanding them simply means you buy nothing locally. The response is to move the control from documentation to **testing and supervision**.

**A workable regime for informal supply:**

| Control | How |
|---|---|
| **Qualify the source once** | Visit the yard or pit. Photograph it. Take samples. Test before the first order, not after the first failure. |
| **Test on delivery, by batch** | Sieve analysis for each new sand source; settling test on every load; block crushing on a sample from each 2 000 blocks. |
| **Fix the specification physically, not verbally** | Provide the mould, the mix ratio by *bags and barrows* not by percentage, and a sample block kept on site as the reference. |
| **Pay for quality, not just quantity** | Price the order against a tested standard, with a stated rejection right and a retention. |
| **Supervise the process** | For anything made on site or nearby, watch the mixing. Under-cementing is the universal failure and it is invisible in the finished product. |
| **Reject visibly and early** | A rejected first load, handled respectfully but firmly, sets the standard for the whole project. A rejection in month four is a dispute. |
| **Keep records** | Source, date, quantity, test result, decision. This is also your defence if the structure is later questioned. |
| **Design for the material you can get** | Where local block strength is uncertain, design the wall to a conservative assumption rather than specifying a strength you cannot verify. |

**The design-side corollary.** Specify in a way informal supply can meet: nominal dimensions with realistic tolerances, mortar mixes by volume, generous cover to reinforcement, and details that tolerate dimensional variation (rendered rather than fair-face, for example). A specification lifted from an urban project guarantees either non-compliance or 660 km of freight.

## 3. Community engagement and traditional authority

### Why this is a logistics topic

On a village project, the community determines:
- whether material left on site overnight is still there in the morning;
- whether you can get 20 labourers on Monday;
- whether the access track can be widened across someone's field;
- whether water can be drawn from the village supply;
- whether the project is spoken about with pride or resentment for the next twenty years.

None of that is achievable by contract. All of it is achievable by relationship.

### The structures to engage

Rural northern Namibia has **two parallel authorities**, and both matter.

1. **Traditional authority.** Communal land belongs to the State and is parcelled and distributed through traditional leaders under the **Communal Land Reform Act No. 5 of 2002**, with communal land boards providing statutory ratification. In the Oukwanyama area the traditional authority has been led since 2005 by **Queen Martha Mwadinomho ya Kristian ya Nelumbu**, seated at Omhedi. Below the senior traditional authority sit senior headmen and village headmen — and it is usually the **village headman** who matters day to day.
2. **Formal government.** The **regional council** (Ohangwena, capital Eenhana, twelve constituencies), the constituency councillor, and — in Okongo's case — the **village council** (five seats, since Okongo became a village in 2015). Building approvals, connections, and site permissions run through these.

**Also engage:** the school principal or hospital superintendent if the project serves them, the church, and any local NGO or development committee already working in the village.

### How to engage — the sequence

1. **Before anything else, find out who to talk to.** Ask the constituency office and the village council. Do not assume. Confirm the correct traditional authority for the exact site. `needs-verification`
2. **Make the first approach in person**, through the correct channel, with a Oshiwambo speaker present. Bring the project, the drawing, the timeline and the employment intent.
3. **Explain what the project is *for*.** A clinic, a classroom, a water point — say what it will do for the village. Abstract descriptions of a "development project" create suspicion.
4. **Be explicit about what you need**: land access, an access route, water, security, labour, and a place to put a compound.
5. **Be explicit about what you will *not* do**: how many jobs there will actually be, that not everyone can be employed, what will and will not be given away at the end.
6. **Agree a communication rhythm.** A monthly meeting with the headman, the councillor and any project committee, at a fixed time, is worth more than crisis meetings.
7. **Set up a grievance route.** One named person, a stated response time, and a written log. Most grievances on rural sites are dust, noise, damage to fields, livestock, water, and employment fairness — all foreseeable and all cheap to fix early.
8. **Handle disturbance and damage promptly.** A broken fence, a crop crossed by a truck, a damaged water pipe: fix or compensate immediately and visibly. The cost is trivial; the reputational effect is not.

### Local employment expectations

Expectations will be high and will exceed what the project can absorb. Manage this deliberately.

- **Publish the number of jobs, the skills needed and the duration** before recruitment, through the headman and the village council.
- **Use a transparent recruitment process.** Hiring through one family or one faction is the fastest route to sabotage and theft.
- **Set a rule for rotation** if demand exceeds supply — for example, fixed-period contracts so more households benefit.
- **Include women deliberately.** Rural Ohangwena households are frequently female-headed, and exclusion from a project's wage income is a real harm.
- **Pay correctly, on time, in a way people can verify.** Late wages are the single most common cause of a village project turning hostile.
- **Train, and certify.** A block-making, bricklaying or scaffolding skill left behind is worth more to the community than the wage, and costs the project almost nothing.
- **Buy locally where you can** — food, accommodation, transport, security. Local spend is the least visible and most durable form of community benefit.

### Reputational economics

A village project is a small, highly visible, long-remembered event. The economics of that visibility are asymmetric:

- **Goodwill is cheap to buy and expensive to replace.** A grader passing over the village access road on the way out, a water point left connected, a stack of surplus material handed to the school — each costs little and is remembered for years.
- **Hostility is expensive and compounding.** Theft rises, labour becomes unreliable, disputes escalate to the councillor, and the next project by the same client or contractor starts from a deficit.
- **The client's reputation is at stake, not only the contractor's.** For a donor-funded or government project, the village's account of how it was built is the project's public record.
- **Handover is part of the logistics plan.** Decide early what happens to the site compound, fencing, water tanks, surplus material and the access road. Publicised in advance and delivered as promised, this converts the end of the project into a second round of goodwill. Announced late or reneged on, it produces exactly the opposite.

## 4. A local sourcing checklist

- [ ] Traditional authority and village headman identified and correctly approached
- [ ] Village council and constituency councillor briefed
- [ ] Land access and access route agreed, in writing where possible
- [ ] Water source agreed, metered, and not competing with domestic supply
- [ ] Sand sources identified, sampled, sieve-tested; blend design done if needed
- [ ] Aggregate decision made (import versus local) on tested grading
- [ ] Block yards visited, sampled and crush-tested, or on-site production set up
- [ ] Local timber use limited to non-structural applications; structural timber graded and treated
- [ ] Labour requirement published; transparent recruitment agreed with the community
- [ ] Wage rates, payment dates and payment method fixed and communicated
- [ ] Grievance route named, publicised and logged
- [ ] Testing regime and rejection rights written into local purchase orders
- [ ] Legacy plan (water point, access road, surplus material, training) agreed and announced
