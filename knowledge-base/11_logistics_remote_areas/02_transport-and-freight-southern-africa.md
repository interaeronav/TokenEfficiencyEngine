# Transport and freight in southern Africa — corridors, ports, rail and borders

**Summary.** Almost all construction material moving to a northern Namibian site travels by **road**, entering the region either through the **Port of Walvis Bay** or overland from **South Africa** across a SACU border where no customs duty is payable. This file sets out the four Walvis Bay corridors with their published distances and transit times, the two Namibian commercial ports, the practical state of TransNamib rail, the border posts that matter (Ariamsvlei/Nakop, Noordoewer/Vioolsdrif, Oshikango/Santa Clara, Buitepos/Mamuno), the SACU customs position, Namibian legal vehicle masses, abnormal load permitting, and what actually drives a freight quotation.

## 1. The four Walvis Bay corridors

The Walvis Bay Corridor Group (WBCG), established 2000, promotes four road corridors radiating from the Namibian coast.

### 1.1 Trans-Kalahari Corridor (TKC)

Walvis Bay – Windhoek – Buitepos – Ghanzi – Gaborone – Johannesburg/Pretoria. **~1 900 km**; SADC Regional Trunk Road Network **route 40**; officially opened **1998** at a construction cost of approximately **N$850 million**.

| From Walvis Bay to | Distance | Transit |
|---|---|---|
| Ghanzi (Botswana) | 921 km | 1–2 days |
| Gaborone (Botswana) | 1 484 km | 2–3 days |
| Francistown (Botswana) | 1 695 km | 2–3 days |
| **Johannesburg (South Africa)** | **1 768 km** | **2 days** |
| Bulawayo (Zimbabwe) | 1 849 km | 3–5 days |
| Harare (Zimbabwe) | 2 280 km | 3–7 days |

Segment distances: Walvis Bay–Swakopmund 35 km; Swakopmund–Okahandja 290 km; Okahandja–Windhoek 70 km; Windhoek–Buitepos 315 km; Buitepos–Lobatse 750 km; Lobatse–Pretoria 285 km. **Walvis Bay–Windhoek therefore = 395 km.** WBCG claims the TKC "cuts the distance by 400 kilometres" against the southern Namibian routing.

**Why it matters for construction:** the TKC is the fastest road link between the Gauteng manufacturing belt and Namibia. Steel sections, sheeting, sanitaryware, electrical gear and specified joinery from Johannesburg are routinely on the road for 2 days to Windhoek, plus a further day north.

### 1.2 Trans-Cunene Corridor

Walvis Bay – Swakopmund – Otjiwarongo – Ondangwa – **Oshikango/Santa Clara** – Lubango (Angola). Agreement signed **May 1997**.

| From Walvis Bay to | Distance | Transit |
|---|---|---|
| **Oshikango (Namibia)** | **1 059 km** | **1 day** |
| Lubango (Angola) | 1 129 km | 1–2 days |

This is *the* corridor for northern Namibian construction. Okongo lies east of the corridor's northern end: from Oshikango the practical route runs south to Ondangwa, east to Eenhana, and a further **~120 km east to Okongo on tarred road**.

WBCG notes the corridor is "supported by the northern railway line, which presently extends from the Port of Walvis Bay to Oshikango" — see the rail caveats in §3.

### 1.3 Walvis Bay–Ndola–Lubumbashi Development Corridor (WBNLDC)

Walvis Bay – Grootfontein – Rundu – Katima Mulilo – Zambia – DRC. **Over 2 500 km**; highway opened **1999**; Katima Mulilo bridge completed **2004**.

| From Walvis Bay to | Distance | Transit |
|---|---|---|
| Lusaka (Zambia) | 2 072 km | 3–5 days |
| Ndola (Zambia) | 2 390 km | 3–5 days |
| Kitwe (Zambia) | 2 429 km | 3–5 days |
| Kasumbalesa (DRC) | 2 525 km | 4–7 days |
| Lubumbashi (DRC) | 2 615 km | 6–7 days |

Rail exists Walvis Bay–Grootfontein with transhipment, resuming at Livingstone in Zambia — i.e. **no through rail**. Relevant to construction mainly as the route that carries copper-belt traffic, which competes for the same trucks and the same port slots.

### 1.4 Trans-Oranje Corridor

Links the Namibian ports (particularly Lüderitz) with South Africa's Northern Cape and Upington. Lower volume; matters for projects in southern Namibia rather than the north.

## 2. Ports

### Walvis Bay

Namibia's principal commercial port: roughly **3 000 vessel calls** and **5 million tonnes** annually, with a new container terminal raising capacity from **400 000 to 750 000 TEU** per annum. Published turnaround times are short — **12–15 h** for container vessels, **24–48 h** for bulk, **18–20 h** average for break-bulk — and the port markets itself as congestion-free with deep-water anchorage. A liquid bulk terminal for hydrocarbons has been built about **5 km north** of the existing port, with its first phase operational.

**Construction relevance.** Imported goods that do not originate in SACU — European ironmongery, Chinese sanitaryware and tiles, specialist membranes, plant and machinery — usually enter through Walvis Bay rather than Durban, because the road leg from Walvis Bay to northern Namibia is shorter, entirely domestic, and avoids two border crossings.

### Lüderitz

A smaller southern port with a **500 m quay**, two 60-tonne haulers and a 45-tonne reach stacker, **254 nautical miles** south of Walvis Bay. Its case is time saved on southbound and Northern Cape traffic ("more than 2 days" on some routings). For a northern Namibian construction project it is essentially irrelevant except as an alternative if Walvis Bay is disrupted.

## 3. Rail — TransNamib and its practical limits

TransNamib operates roughly **2 687 km** of **1 067 mm Cape gauge** railway, linking Windhoek to Walvis Bay, Tsumeb, Gobabis, the Northern Extension, Upington (via Ariamsvlei) and Lüderitz (via Seeheim). It offers bulk freight, station-to-station containers, break-bulk and warehousing.

**The constraints that matter:**

- **Axle load.** Only about **48 %** of the network meets the SADC 18.5 t/axle standard; the rest is limited to **16.5–17.5 t/axle**. This restricts train mass and therefore economics.
- **Station-to-station only.** Rail delivers to a siding, not to site. Every rail movement needs road at both ends plus two transhipments — which is exactly where damage to bagged cement, sheeting and joinery occurs.
- **The northern extension is thin.** The line reached Ondangwa by mid-2006 and was intended to continue to Oshikango; service has historically been intermittent, with early operations affected by locomotive failures. Treat any northern rail plan as `needs-verification` at the time of use.
- **Weather.** In early 2011 the Karasburg–Ariamsvlei mainline was damaged by flash floods and services were suspended — a reminder that rail is not immune to the rainy season.
- **Speed and reliability.** Road wins on transit time for almost every construction consignment under a full trainload.

**When rail is worth it:** genuinely bulk, non-urgent, damage-tolerant commodities in trainload quantities — cement in bulk tankers to a depot, aggregate, structural steel billets. For a single village building, rail is not a realistic option.

## 4. Borders and customs

### 4.1 The SACU position — this is the key fact

Namibia, Botswana, Eswatini, Lesotho and South Africa form the **Southern African Customs Union**, the oldest existing customs union in the world in its present form (1910), headquartered in **Windhoek**. SACU maintains a **common external tariff** and the "free interchange of goods between member countries". Customs duties and excise collected go into a common revenue pool and are redistributed by formula.

**Practical consequence for a Namibian builder:** goods bought in South Africa and moved to Namibia attract **no customs duty**. What they do attract is:
- **Import VAT in Namibia** — the standard rate is **15 %**, and the import VAT value base is the greater of FOB plus 10 % or market value. Registered importers can operate a NamRA import VAT account deferring payment to the **20th of the month following importation**.
- **Documentation** — a customs declaration is still required even though the duty is nil. Movement is duty-free, not paperwork-free.
- **Cross-border road transport permits** for the vehicle and operator, obtained from the Namibian Roads Authority (which issues "Domestic Road Transportation Permits, Cross Border Road Transportation Permits and Abnormal Load Permits") and the corresponding South African authority.

Goods originating **outside** SACU (China, EU, India) pay the common external tariff at the first point of entry into the union, plus VAT. That is why the point of entry — Walvis Bay versus Durban — changes the paperwork, and why an "imported" price quoted by a Windhoek merchant already contains duty you cannot avoid by importing yourself unless you can also beat their volume.

Namibian customs operate under the **Customs and Excise Act 20 of 1998**, administered by **NamRA**. Duties and taxes are payable in **NAD or ZAR**.

### 4.2 The border posts that matter

| Border post | Between | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **Ariamsvlei / Nakop** | Namibia ↔ South Africa (Northern Cape, on the N10/Upington road) | Main southern road and rail crossing; the default for freight from Gauteng routed via Upington |
| **Noordoewer / Vioolsdrif** | Namibia ↔ South Africa (Orange River, N7) | Main crossing for Cape Town traffic |
| **Buitepos / Mamuno** | Namibia ↔ Botswana | The Trans-Kalahari crossing; the fast route from Gauteng |
| **Oshikango / Santa Clara** | Namibia ↔ Angola | Northern trade hub; the busiest northern crossing, with an Export Processing Zone of 14 warehouses at Oshikango |

Practical border discipline:
- **Know the operating hours before you dispatch.** A truck arriving after closing loses a night's driving and a driver's rest window. Published hours vary by post and change; confirm each time. `needs-verification`
- **Use a clearing agent** for anything non-routine. Their fee is small against a truck standing at a border.
- **Send documents ahead electronically**, so clearance is being worked while the vehicle is still moving.
- **Weighbridges sit near borders.** Ariamsvlei, Noordoewer and Rosh Pinah all have Roads Authority weighbridges, as do Windhoek (Brakwater, Aris), Gobabis, Walvis Bay (Rooi Kop Road), Oshakati and Grootfontein.

> ⚠️ **Overloading.** Namibian enforcement: excess under 2 tonnes → on-the-spot fine, vehicle detained until the load is redistributed. Excess over 2 tonnes → court proceedings and the vehicle is impounded. Neither outcome is recoverable within a delivery window; both are the transporter's problem contractually and the project's problem in reality.

## 5. Vehicle types and payloads

| Vehicle | Typical payload | Typical deck | Construction use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Superlink / interlink (tri-axle horse + two trailers) | **30–34 t** | ~2 × 12 m | The long-haul workhorse; bulk cement, brick, steel |
| Tri-axle semi-trailer (13.6 m tautliner or flatbed) | **~28–30 t** | 13.6 m, ~33 pallets | Mixed loads; sheeting; palletised goods |
| Rigid 8–10 t flatbed with drop-sides | **8–10 t** | 6–8 m | Regional distribution; last mile on marginal roads |
| Rigid with truck-mounted crane ("brick crane") | **6–10 t** | 6–8 m | Delivery to sites with no offloading plant — see `03` |
| Tipper (6 m³ / 10 m³ / 18 m³) | 10–30 t | — | Sand, aggregate, spoil |
| 4×4 LDV / bakkie | **~1 t** | 2.4 m | Emergency, tools, personnel |
| 6 m / 12 m shipping container (as store) | 21 t / 26 t gross | 13.8 m³ / 33 m³ | Doubles as secure site store |
| Low-bed / abnormal trailer | 30–60 t+ | — | Plant, tanks, transformers, long trusses |

**Loading rules of thumb:**
- **Cement**: 50 kg bags, 20 bags per tonne. A 30 t load = **600 bags** = 30 t exactly, occupying roughly half a tri-axle deck. Always pair with light goods.
- **Bricks**: at ~3.3 kg per stock brick, a 30 t load is roughly **9 000 bricks**.
- **Roof sheeting**: cubes out long before it masses out; sheets over 6 m may need a dedicated or extendable trailer.
- **Reinforcement**: order **cut and bent** with a bar schedule. Straight 13 m bar needs a longer trailer, wastes deck space and generates offcuts you will pay to freight.

## 6. Abnormal loads and permits

Any load exceeding legal mass or dimension limits requires an **Abnormal Load Permit** from the Roads Authority (application form published; enquiries `permits@ra.org.na`, +264 61 284 7000). South African equivalents are issued provincially under the national abnormal-load framework, and a cross-border abnormal move needs a permit on both sides plus route approval.

Triggers, in practice:
- **Over-mass** — above the permissible GVM of 56 000 kg, or exceeding axle limits, or failing the Reg. 260 bridge formula `P = 2 100 L + 18 000`.
- **Over-width** — wide trusses, tanks, plant.
- **Over-length** — long-span portal frames, long trusses, piles.
- **Over-height** — plant on a low-bed; bridge and power-line clearances become the constraint.

**Programme consequence:** abnormal-load permitting adds **weeks**, may restrict travel to daylight or specific days, may require escort vehicles, and may prescribe a longer route. Design decisions that avoid an abnormal load — splicing a truss, specifying a bolted rather than welded frame, choosing two smaller tanks — are usually cheaper than the permit and the delay. Decide this at design stage, not when the fabricator calls.

> ⚠️ Never plan an abnormal move assuming permits will be "sorted out". Confirm route approval **before** the fabricator cuts steel.

## 7. What actually drives a freight quotation

A transporter's price is built from:

1. **Distance and route** — tarred versus gravel changes tyre and suspension cost sharply; some operators refuse gravel or price a surcharge.
2. **Vehicle utilisation and backload** — a lane with no return cargo is priced as a round trip. Northern Namibia is a classic empty-return lane, which is why Windhoek→Okongo costs far more per km than Windhoek→Walvis Bay. **Ask about backloads**; a transporter with a return load will discount.
3. **Fuel** — the dominant variable cost; many quotes include a fuel-price escalation clause.
4. **Standing time / demurrage** — the charge that catches unprepared sites. If a truck waits because there is nobody to offload, you pay by the hour or the day. Budget for it and design it out.
5. **Road user charges and permits** — mass-distance and cross-border charges levied by the Namibian Road Fund Administration and South African equivalents. `needs-verification` for current rates.
6. **Insurance** — goods-in-transit cover; check the limit, the excess, and whether it covers the last mile on an unproclaimed road (often it does not).
7. **Load and offload provision** — whether the price includes a crane, a Moffett forklift, or a driver who will "help".
8. **Risk premium** — theft-prone cargo and remote destinations carry a loading.

**Quotation checklist.** Get every quote to state: vehicle type and payload; whether the price is per trip or per tonne; free loading and offloading time; demurrage rate; whether the last mile beyond the tar is included; insurance limit and excess; fuel escalation; and who pays for a failed delivery caused by site conditions.
