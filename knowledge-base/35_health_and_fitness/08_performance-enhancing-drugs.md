# Performance-enhancing drugs — pharmacology, harms, law and anti-doping

**Summary.** This file describes what performance-enhancing drugs are, what the controlled literature shows about their effects, what the documented harms are, how common their use is, what the law says in several jurisdictions, and how anti-doping works. The effects on muscle mass and strength are real and, for androgens, large — which is precisely why the harms and the systematic misrepresentation of drug-assisted physiques as drug-free matter so much. Adverse effects are documented across cardiovascular, hepatic, endocrine, reproductive and psychiatric domains, and the grey market compounds every risk with contamination and mislabelling.

> ⚠️ **Scope boundary.** This file contains **no cycles, no doses for non-medical use, no sourcing information, no post-cycle protocols, and no harm-minimised usage instructions.** Where doses from published clinical trials appear, they are reported as study parameters that establish the size of an effect or a harm, not as recommendations. Non-medical use of these substances is illegal in many jurisdictions, prohibited in organised sport, and carries documented risks including death. If you are using or considering using these substances, speak to a physician — many countries have confidential services specifically for this, and clinicians increasingly recognise that a non-judgemental approach produces better outcomes than moralising.

## 1. Anabolic-androgenic steroids (AAS)

### What they are

Synthetic derivatives of testosterone, or testosterone itself in esterified form. All are agonists of the androgen receptor. The nomenclature "anabolic-androgenic" reflects the two effect classes: **anabolic** (nitrogen retention, muscle protein synthesis, erythropoiesis, bone density) and **androgenic** (development and maintenance of male secondary sexual characteristics, prostate, skin, hair). No compound separates these completely; the "anabolic:androgenic ratio" quoted in the literature derives from rodent assays (levator ani versus ventral prostate weight) and translates poorly to humans.

**Mechanism.** Testosterone and derivatives bind the intracellular androgen receptor, which translocates to the nucleus and acts as a transcription factor, upregulating muscle protein synthesis and satellite cell proliferation. Additional mechanisms include anti-glucocorticoid action (competing at the glucocorticoid receptor, reducing catabolism), increased IGF-1 expression in muscle, increased myonuclear number, and increased erythropoiesis. Some derivatives are 5α-reduced to more potent androgens; some aromatise to oestradiol.

**Structural modifications** exist to change pharmacokinetics: esterification of the 17β-hydroxyl group slows release from an injection depot; 17α-alkylation permits oral administration by resisting hepatic first-pass metabolism — and is the modification directly responsible for the hepatotoxicity of oral compounds.

### What the controlled literature shows

**Bhasin and colleagues (1996)** is the landmark trial. Forty-three normal men were randomised to four groups: placebo without exercise, testosterone without exercise, placebo with exercise, testosterone with exercise. Testosterone enanthate 600 mg weekly for 10 weeks; the exercise groups lifted three times weekly.

- **Testosterone without exercise beat placebo without exercise**: triceps CSA +424 ± 104 vs −81 ± 109 mm² (p < 0.05); quadriceps CSA +607 ± 123 vs −131 ± 111 mm² (p < 0.05); bench press +9 ± 4 vs −1 ± 1 kg; squat +16 ± 4 vs +3 ± 1 kg.
- **Testosterone with exercise** produced fat-free mass gains of **6.1 ± 0.6 kg**, triceps CSA +501 ± 104 mm², quadriceps CSA +1174 ± 91 mm², bench press +22 ± 2 kg, squat +38 ± 4 kg — greater than either non-exercise group.
- Neither mood nor behaviour was altered in any group over the ten weeks.

Two things follow. First, **testosterone alone, without any training at all, produced greater muscle growth and strength gain than training alone.** That is the size of the effect. Second, the effects are additive with training — which is why the combination is what is used.

**Bhasin and colleagues (2001)** established dose-dependence. Sixty-one eugonadal men had endogenous testosterone suppressed with a GnRH agonist and received graded weekly testosterone doses for 20 weeks. Fat-free mass increased **+3.4, +5.2 and +7.9 kg** at 125, 300 and 600 mg/week respectively. Change in FFM was highly dose-dependent (p = 0.0001) and correlated with log testosterone concentration (r = 0.73). Leg press strength, leg power, thigh and quadriceps volumes, haemoglobin and IGF-1 all correlated positively with testosterone concentration; fat mass and HDL cholesterol correlated negatively.

That last clause is important: **HDL cholesterol falls in a dose-dependent manner with testosterone concentration.** The benefit and the harm scale together.

### Documented adverse effects

**Cardiovascular.** The strongest single dataset is **Baggish and colleagues (2017)**, a cross-sectional study of 140 experienced male weightlifters aged 34–54 (86 with ≥2 years cumulative AAS use, 54 non-users), assessed by echocardiography and coronary CT angiography:

- **Reduced left ventricular systolic function**: LVEF 52 ± 11% (users) versus 63 ± 8% (non-users), p < 0.001.
- **Reduced diastolic function**: early relaxation velocity 9.3 ± 2.4 versus 11.1 ± 2.0 cm/s, p < 0.001.
- **Within users**, those currently on-drug (n = 58) had worse systolic (LVEF 49 ± 10% vs 58 ± 10%, p < 0.001) and diastolic function than those currently off-drug (n = 28) — suggesting partial reversibility of the functional deficit.
- **Coronary artery plaque volume** was higher in users: median 3 [IQR 0, 174] versus 0 [0, 69] mm³, p = 0.012.
- **Lifetime AAS dose was strongly associated with coronary atherosclerotic burden**: 0.60 SD units increase in plaque volume rank per 10-year increase in cumulative use duration (95% CI 0.16–1.03, p = 0.008).

The mechanisms are multiple: profound HDL suppression and LDL elevation, direct myocardial effects, left ventricular hypertrophy with impaired relaxation, increased haematocrit and blood viscosity (thrombotic risk), elevated blood pressure, and endothelial dysfunction.

A Norwegian cross-sectional study (Henriksen et al., 2024) of 90 men with ≥12 months cumulative AAS use is worth noting for a different reason: among current users with established LVEF <50% (n = 25) or left ventricular hypertrophy (n = 21), **44% and 43% respectively had never engaged health services** about AAS-related effects. Substantial cardiac disease is going undetected in this population.

**Hepatic.** 17α-alkylated oral compounds carry the highest risk: cholestatic jaundice, elevated transaminases, peliosis hepatis (blood-filled hepatic cysts), hepatic adenoma and, rarely, hepatocellular carcinoma. Injectable non-alkylated compounds are considerably less hepatotoxic. Note that elevated creatine kinase and AST from heavy training can confound liver-enzyme interpretation in lifters.

**Endocrine — HPTA suppression.** Exogenous androgens suppress hypothalamic GnRH and pituitary LH and FSH release by negative feedback. Consequences: suppressed endogenous testosterone production, testicular atrophy, impaired spermatogenesis (frequently azoospermia), and reduced fertility.

The **Solanki et al. (2023) scoping review** on anabolic steroid-induced hypogonadism (ASIH) provides the recovery picture:
- Recovery depends on **age and degree of exposure**.
- **Testicular atrophy**: near-full recovery expected over months to years.
- **Spermatogenesis**: full recovery expected over months to years.
- **Libido**: returns to baseline over several months, typically less potent than during use.
- **Gynaecomastia**: recovery is **unlikely** — this generally requires surgery.
- **Biochemical**: near-complete testosterone recovery over months; complete gonadotropin recovery expected over 3–6 months.
- **Psychological**: data insufficient and conflicting; a transient withdrawal period possibly followed by persisting milder symptoms.

**Pope and Kanayama (2022)** add the important caveat that mounting evidence suggests **ASIH may persist for months or years after last use, and in some cases may be partially or completely irreversible**, particularly after prolonged use. They also note that many endocrinologists have limited experience diagnosing and treating it — meaning that people who develop this condition often struggle to get competent care.

**Other endocrine effects:** gynaecomastia from aromatisation to oestradiol; acne and seborrhoea; androgenic alopecia acceleration in genetically susceptible individuals; insulin resistance; and in women, **irreversible** virilisation — deepened voice, clitoromegaly, hirsutism, and menstrual disruption. The irreversibility in women is a critical and often-omitted point.

**Psychiatric.** Reported effects include hypomania and mania, increased aggression and irritability, depression (particularly during withdrawal), psychosis, and suicidality. The evidence base is mixed: the Bhasin 1996 trial found no mood or behaviour changes at 600 mg/week over 10 weeks in a controlled setting, while observational studies of high-dose, multi-compound, long-duration users report substantial psychiatric morbidity. The most parsimonious reading is that effects are dose-dependent, individually variable, and much more likely at the doses and durations used non-medically than at those studied in trials.

**Dependence** is a documented phenomenon — a substantial minority of long-term users meet criteria for dependence, driven by both the withdrawal syndrome (hypogonadism, depression) and by body image pathology.

**Mortality and healthcare burden.** Danish nationwide register studies of men sanctioned for AAS use provide the best population-level data:
- **Windfeld-Mathiasen et al. (2024, JAMA)** reported on mortality among users of anabolic steroids in this cohort. (The specific hazard ratio is not reproduced here because the research-letter abstract was not retrievable — see Open questions.)
- **Horwitz et al. (2026)** examined healthcare costs in 1 183 males sanctioned for AAS use in Denmark 2006–2017 against 59 150 matched controls: AAS users incurred **a mean excess of €3 299 per person** over up to 10 years of follow-up (95% CI 1 857–4 742, p < 0.0001), approximately **€537 per user per year — a 45% increase over controls** whose average total cost was €7 393. Costs were driven mainly by hospital care and the difference widened steadily over time. The authors note this population is young and would otherwise be expected to have low healthcare use.
- An older Swedish study (Petersson et al., 2006) found standardised mortality ratios of **20.43 (95% CI 10.56–35.70)** in AAS-positive patients versus **6.02 (3.77–9.12)** in AAS-negative comparison patients, with substantially elevated rates of institutionalised care for substance abuse, psychiatric disorder and central thoracic pain. The design (patients tested during medical care) limits generalisability, and the authors explicitly say the nature of the association remains unclear.

## 2. SARMs (selective androgen receptor modulators)

Non-steroidal androgen receptor ligands designed for tissue-selective agonism — the intended therapeutic promise being anabolic effects on muscle and bone without prostate, skin and hair effects. Examples in the literature include ostarine (enobosarm), ligandrol (LGD-4033), RAD-140 and andarine.

**Regulatory status: no SARM has FDA approval for any indication.** They are frequently sold online as "research chemicals" or falsely labelled as dietary supplements.

**Vasireddi and colleagues (2025)** systematically reviewed 72 articles (2003–2022):
- Estimated **prevalence among athletes: 1–3%**.
- Preclinical and clinical studies report **significant increases in lean body mass** — but also **bone remodelling changes, testosterone suppression, and kidney, liver and prostate enlargement**.
- Thirteen case reports described 15 cases of SARM-related harm. All were men, median age 32 (range 19–52), more than half identified as athletes, all took SARMs orally, mean course 8 weeks.
- **Five of the 15 explicitly denied "illicit drug use"** — implying users often believe SARM use is legal.
- Athletes most commonly purchased online, and **most of these compounds have been shown to be contaminated with other substances**, contributing to the adverse effects.
- **Athletes reported consuming SARMs at much higher doses than have been clinically studied**, plausibly increasing the risk of the reported harms: **liver injury, impaired insulin sensitivity, cardiovascular events, and tendon damage.**
- Case reports specifically associate SARMs with **drug-induced liver injury, myocarditis and tendon rupture**.

The review's conclusion is unambiguous: athlete SARM use is "increasingly widespread and unsafe", and public health bodies should advocate for regulation of these grey-market compounds.

The key point for a general reader: **SARMs are frequently marketed as a safer alternative to steroids. The evidence does not support that framing.** They suppress the HPTA, they are hepatotoxic, they are almost universally obtained from unregulated sources with documented contamination, and the human safety data are thin because the clinical development programmes for most of them were discontinued.

## 3. Growth hormone and IGF-1

**Growth hormone (GH)** is a 191-amino-acid peptide from the anterior pituitary. It acts directly on tissues and indirectly via hepatic IGF-1 production. It promotes lipolysis, increases protein synthesis, increases sodium and water retention, and antagonises insulin action.

**The controlled evidence is starkly at odds with its reputation.** **Liu and colleagues (2008)** systematically reviewed 44 articles describing 27 study samples in healthy, fit young adults (303 GH recipients; mean age 27; mean VO2max 51 mL/kg/min):

- **Lean body mass increased by 2.1 kg (95% CI 1.3–2.9 kg)** in GH recipients.
- **Strength and exercise capacity did not appear to improve.**
- **Lactate levels during exercise were significantly higher** in two of three studies measuring it — that is, exercise capacity may have been *worse*.
- GH recipients experienced **soft tissue oedema and fatigue** more frequently.
- Conclusion: **"Claims that growth hormone enhances physical performance are not supported by the scientific literature."**

The likely explanation for the lean mass gain without strength gain is that a substantial fraction of the "lean mass" increase is **water retention**, not contractile tissue.

**Documented adverse effects:** soft tissue oedema, arthralgia, carpal tunnel syndrome, insulin resistance and frank diabetes, and — with prolonged supraphysiological exposure — acromegalic features including bone and soft tissue overgrowth of the hands, feet, jaw and internal organs, and cardiomyopathy. Concerns about cancer risk relate to IGF-1's mitogenic effects; the evidence in this context is not conclusive but the mechanism is plausible.

**GH secretagogues and "peptides"** (GHRP-2, GHRP-6, ipamorelin, hexarelin, sermorelin, CJC-1295, and others) stimulate endogenous GH release. They are mostly unapproved, sold through grey-market channels, and lack meaningful human efficacy or safety data for performance purposes. **BPC-157** and **TB-500**, marketed for tissue healing, have essentially no controlled human evidence at all. Contamination and mislabelling in this market are severe.

**IGF-1 and its analogues** (mecasermin, IGF-1 LR3) carry hypoglycaemia risk and the same theoretical cancer concerns, with even less human data.

## 4. Insulin

Used non-medically for its anabolic and anti-catabolic properties — insulin drives glucose and amino acid uptake into muscle and inhibits protein breakdown.

**This is the single most acutely lethal drug in this file.** Insulin has a narrow therapeutic index in a non-diabetic person, and an error produces **hypoglycaemia**, which progresses through confusion and seizure to coma, irreversible brain damage and death, potentially within an hour. Deaths in bodybuilders from insulin misuse are documented in the forensic literature. There is no meaningful margin for error, no reliable way to self-monitor during sleep, and no antidote available at the moment of onset without help.

Its actual evidence base for muscle gain in an already well-fed trainee is weak: ordinary carbohydrate and protein feeding already produces a substantial insulin response, and muscle protein synthesis is more responsive to amino acid availability than to insulin above a modest threshold.

## 5. Clenbuterol and beta-2 agonists

**Clenbuterol** is a long-acting beta-2 adrenergic agonist licensed in some countries as a bronchodilator (not in the US or UK for human use) and used veterinarily. Its non-medical use is for fat loss.

Mechanism: beta-2 agonism increases lipolysis, thermogenesis and metabolic rate. In livestock, high-dose beta-agonists have a documented repartitioning effect (more lean, less fat). **In humans, the anabolic effect at tolerable doses is small to absent**; the metabolic effect is real but modest and subject to rapid receptor downregulation.

**Adverse effects:** tachycardia, palpitations, arrhythmia, tremor, anxiety, insomnia, hypokalaemia, and — documented in animal and some human data — **cardiac hypertrophy and myocardial necrosis**. The therapeutic-to-toxic margin is narrow. Clenbuterol poisoning outbreaks from contaminated meat are well documented.

**Salbutamol, formoterol, salmeterol and terbutaline** are the therapeutically relevant beta-2 agonists. Inhaled use within specified thresholds is permitted under the WADA framework without a TUE; systemic use is prohibited. There is essentially no evidence that inhaled therapeutic doses enhance performance in athletes without asthma.

## 6. Thyroid hormones

**Levothyroxine (T4)** and **liothyronine (T3)** are used non-medically to accelerate fat loss by raising metabolic rate.

The mechanism works — thyroid hormone is a primary determinant of resting metabolic rate. The problem is that it is not selective: **exogenous thyroid hormone in a euthyroid person accelerates loss of lean tissue as well as fat**, and it suppresses the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis, so endogenous production falls and recovery after cessation takes time.

**Adverse effects:** tachycardia and arrhythmia (including atrial fibrillation), anxiety, tremor, heat intolerance, muscle wasting, **accelerated bone loss and osteoporosis**, and, at high doses, thyrotoxicosis. In someone with an undiagnosed cardiac condition, the arrhythmia risk is serious.

## 7. Diuretics

Used for rapid weight loss to make a weight class, and by physique competitors to reduce subcutaneous water for a "dry" appearance. They are also used as **masking agents**, diluting urine to reduce the detectable concentration of other prohibited substances — which is why WADA prohibits them at all times regardless of the reason for use.

Classes include loop diuretics (furosemide), thiazides, and potassium-sparing agents (spironolactone, amiloride).

**Adverse effects:** dehydration, hypotension, syncope, and — the serious one — **electrolyte disturbance**, particularly hypokalaemia and hyponatraemia, which cause cardiac arrhythmia and can be fatal. Acute kidney injury, muscle cramping and rhabdomyolysis are documented. Deaths of physique competitors associated with diuretic use are recorded in the forensic and case-report literature.

Rapid dehydration for weight-making is also independently dangerous: it impairs thermoregulation, reduces plasma volume, degrades performance, and has caused deaths in combat sports and wrestling.

## 8. EPO and blood doping

**Erythropoietin (EPO)** is a glycoprotein hormone produced by the kidney that stimulates red blood cell production. Recombinant EPO and its analogues (darbepoetin, CERA) raise haematocrit and therefore oxygen-carrying capacity, improving endurance performance substantially — the effect on VO2max and time-trial performance in the controlled literature is one of the larger ergogenic effects known.

**Blood doping** achieves the same end mechanically: autologous transfusion (removing and later reinfusing one's own blood) or homologous transfusion (another person's blood).

**Adverse effects:** raised haematocrit increases blood viscosity, raising the risk of **thrombosis, stroke, pulmonary embolism and myocardial infarction** — a risk that increases sharply during the dehydration and haemoconcentration of prolonged endurance exercise, and during sleep when heart rate is lowest. The cluster of unexplained deaths among European professional cyclists in the late 1980s and early 1990s is widely attributed to early, unmonitored EPO use. Homologous transfusion adds risks of transfusion reaction and blood-borne infection; both add infection risk from non-sterile technique. Pure red cell aplasia from anti-EPO antibodies has been reported.

**Newer agents** — HIF stabilisers (roxadustat and similar) — achieve EPO elevation pharmacologically and are prohibited.

## 9. Stimulants

A broad class including amphetamines, methylphenidate, cocaine, modafinil, ephedrine, pseudoephedrine, DMAA and its analogues.

**Effects:** increased alertness and vigilance, reduced perception of effort and fatigue, increased power output in some protocols, appetite suppression. The effects on time-to-exhaustion and on repeated sprint performance are real for amphetamines.

**Adverse effects:** hypertension, tachycardia and arrhythmia, hyperthermia (particularly dangerous because stimulants blunt the perception of fatigue and heat strain, allowing an athlete to exercise past a safe thermal limit), stroke, myocardial infarction, anxiety, insomnia, psychosis, and dependence. Fatal heat stroke in cyclists using amphetamines is part of the historical record of the sport.

**DMAA (1,3-dimethylamylamine)** and its successors (DMBA, DMHA) appeared in the pre-workout supplement market and have been linked to haemorrhagic stroke and death; multiple regulators have banned them, and they continue to reappear under new names.

Caffeine, covered in file `07`, is the legal and by far the safest member of this class. It was on the WADA prohibited list until 2004 and is currently on the **Monitoring Programme** rather than the Prohibited List.

## 10. Prevalence

**Sagoe and colleagues (2014)** meta-analysed 187 studies providing 271 lifetime prevalence rates:

- **Global lifetime prevalence: 3.3% (95% CI 2.8–3.8)**, with very high heterogeneity (I² = 99.7).
- **Males: 6.4% (5.3–7.7). Females: 1.6% (1.3–1.9)** — significantly different (p < 0.001).
- Sample type (athletes), assessment method, sampling method and male sample percentage were significant predictors.
- No evidence of publication bias.

**In elite sport**, **de Hon and colleagues (2015)** compared methods and concluded that **14–39% of current adult elite athletes have intentionally used doping**, based on randomised response technique questionnaires and biological parameter models. This contrasts sharply with the **1–2% annual adverse analytical finding rate** from doping control tests, which the authors state "show a distinct underestimation of true doping prevalence."

Prevalence is markedly higher in specific subpopulations: recreational gym users in some surveys, competitive bodybuilders, strength athletes, and physique-focused social media populations.

## 12. The anti-doping framework

**The World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA)** was established in 1999 and its **World Anti-Doping Code** harmonises anti-doping rules across roughly 700 signatory sports organisations and among public authorities. The Code was first implemented for the 2004 Athens Olympics; revised versions took effect in 2009, 2015 and 2021, with a further review cycle ongoing. Governments give it effect through the **UNESCO International Convention against Doping in Sport** (2005).

**The Prohibited List** is revised annually and takes effect on 1 January. It is organised as:

- **Prohibited at all times (in- and out-of-competition):** S0 non-approved substances; S1 anabolic agents (including AAS and SARMs); S2 peptide hormones, growth factors, related substances and mimetics (including EPO, GH, IGF-1); S3 beta-2 agonists; S4 hormone and metabolic modulators (including aromatase inhibitors, SERMs, myostatin inhibitors, metabolic modulators); S5 diuretics and masking agents. Methods: M1 manipulation of blood and blood components; M2 chemical and physical manipulation; M3 gene and cell doping.
- **Prohibited in-competition:** S6 stimulants; S7 narcotics; S8 cannabinoids; S9 glucocorticoids (by specified routes).
- **Prohibited in particular sports:** P1 beta-blockers (in sports such as archery, shooting, darts, billiards and others, with archery and shooting also out-of-competition).

**Criteria for inclusion.** A substance or method is added if it meets **two of three criteria**: (1) potential to enhance sport performance; (2) actual or potential health risk to the athlete; (3) violation of the spirit of sport. Substances that mask the use of other prohibited substances are included regardless.

**Therapeutic Use Exemptions (TUEs)** allow an athlete with a legitimate medical condition to use an otherwise prohibited substance. The conditions are that the athlete would experience significant health impairment without it, that it produces no additional performance enhancement beyond return to normal health, that there is no reasonable permitted alternative, and that the need is not a consequence of prior non-therapeutic use of a prohibited substance.

**Testing.** Urine and blood samples are collected in- and out-of-competition, with **whereabouts requirements** for athletes in registered testing pools. Samples are split into A and B; a positive A sample may be confirmed by the B sample. **Strict liability** applies: the athlete is responsible for what is in their body regardless of intent, which is why supplement contamination (file `07`) is such a serious operational risk.

**The Athlete Biological Passport (ABP)** takes a different approach: rather than detecting the substance, it tracks selected biomarkers over time and flags deviations from an individual's own established baseline. The **haematological module** tracks haemoglobin concentration, reticulocyte percentage and derived indices to detect blood doping and EPO use even when the substance itself is undetectable. The **steroidal module** tracks the urinary steroid profile (including the testosterone/epitestosterone ratio and related metabolites) to detect endogenous steroid administration. Longitudinal profiling has been substantially more effective than single-sample testing for these classes.

**Sanctions** under the Code are typically four years for intentional use of a non-specified substance, with reductions for specified substances, contaminated products, prompt admission and substantial assistance, and increases for aggravating circumstances or repeat violations. Appeals go to the Court of Arbitration for Sport.

## 13. The grey market: contamination and mislabelling

Non-medical PED supply operates through underground laboratories, online vendors, and diverted pharmaceutical product. The quality problems are systematic and well documented:

- **Wrong compound.** Product labelled as one substance containing another, often a cheaper one.
- **Wrong dose.** Under-dosing is common (a profit mechanism); over-dosing occurs from poor manufacturing control.
- **Contamination.** Non-sterile production of injectables leads to abscess, cellulitis and systemic infection. Heavy metal and solvent residues have been identified.
- **Counterfeit packaging** that convincingly imitates legitimate pharmaceutical product.
- **No accountability.** There is no recall mechanism, no adverse event reporting, no batch traceability, and no legal recourse.

The SARM review (Vasireddi 2025) found that **most SARM compounds purchased online have been shown to be contaminated with other substances**, and attributed part of the observed adverse effect profile to this. Independent analytical studies of products sold as SARMs have repeatedly found undeclared anabolic steroids, other unapproved drugs, or no active ingredient at all.

And, as covered in file `07`, the problem reaches into the ordinary supplement market: **Geyer and colleagues (2004) found undeclared anabolic androgenic steroids in 14.8% of 634 non-hormonal supplements** purchased across 13 countries, at concentrations from 0.01 to 190 µg/g — enough, in the nandrolone cases, to produce positive doping tests.

## 14. Ethics

The arguments are worth stating rather than assumed.

**Against permitting use in sport:**
- **Health.** The documented harms above are substantial, and permitting use creates coercive pressure on athletes who would otherwise abstain.
- **Coercion.** In a permissive environment, an athlete who declines is competitively disadvantaged. Consent under those conditions is questionable.
- **Fairness.** Competition is meant to test trained capacity, not access to pharmacology and medical supervision — which correlates strongly with wealth and national resources.
- **Effect on non-elite populations.** Elite practice propagates downward to adolescents and recreational trainees who have neither medical supervision nor a career at stake.

**For a more permissive position (the minority view, associated with Savulescu and others):**
- Enforcement is expensive, invasive (whereabouts requirements), imperfect (14–39% prevalence versus 1–2% detection) and creates its own harms.
- Supervised use might be safer than the current unsupervised black market.
- Sport already accepts many forms of technological and physiological advantage, and the line between permitted and prohibited enhancement is drawn inconsistently (altitude tents are permitted; blood transfusions are not, though both raise haemoglobin mass).

**The point that most affects ordinary readers**, independent of the sporting ethics: **undisclosed use systematically distorts what people believe is achievable naturally.** A very large proportion of the physiques presented online as the product of training and discipline are not achievable without pharmacology. This is not a marginal problem — it is arguably the single largest source of misinformation in the fitness industry, because it corrupts the reference standard against which people judge their own progress, and it drives both supplement purchasing (people looking for the missing ingredient) and eventual drug use.

## 15. The natural limit and FFMI

**Kouri and colleagues (1995)** calculated fat-free mass index — FFM in kg divided by height in metres squared — in 157 male athletes: 83 AAS users and 74 non-users. They applied a height normalisation of +6.3 × (1.80 m − height) to standardise to a 1.80 m man.

- **Non-users' normalised FFMI extended up to a well-defined limit of 25.0.**
- A sample of **20 Mr America winners from the pre-steroid era (1939–1959)** had an estimated mean normalised FFMI of **25.4**.
- Many AAS users exceeded 25.0, and **some exceeded 30**.
- The authors proposed FFMI as a screening measure for possible steroid use in athletic, medical or forensic contexts, while explicitly stating the findings "must be regarded as preliminary."

**How to use this honestly:**

- FFMI ~25 represents roughly the upper bound of what an exceptional drug-free male athlete achieves after many years — not a target, and not the expected outcome. For a 1.80 m man that is about 81 kg of fat-free mass, or roughly 88–90 kg at 10% body fat.
- **The average committed drug-free trainee lands well below this** — an FFMI of 22–23 after several years of good training is a strong outcome for most people.
- **FFMI is not a reliable individual test for drug use.** It is a population-level indicator with overlapping distributions. Some genetically exceptional individuals exceed 25 naturally; many drug users sit below it. Using it to accuse a specific person is not supportable.
- The height-normalisation correction is itself an approximation, and body composition measurement error (file `06`) propagates directly into FFMI.
- **Women** have lower FFMI ranges; the Kouri data are male-only, and equivalent thresholds for women are not well established.

The most useful function of this literature is calibration: it puts a number on the ceiling, and it makes clear that a substantial fraction of what is publicly presented as natural sits above that ceiling.
