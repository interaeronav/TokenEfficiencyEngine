# Supplements — a graded evidence review

**Summary.** A very small number of supplements have robust, replicated evidence of a measurable ergogenic or health effect: creatine monohydrate, caffeine, beta-alanine, dietary nitrate, sodium bicarbonate, vitamin D where deficient, and protein powder as a convenience food. A larger group has weak, mixed or context-limited evidence. The largest group by far does nothing measurable, and a meaningful minority is contaminated or adulterated. Third-party certification meaningfully reduces — but does not eliminate — contamination risk, and the analytical literature has found undeclared anabolic steroids in roughly one in seven supplements sampled in a large international study. Supplements are the last 1–5% of an outcome, and only after training, sleep, energy and protein intake are in order.

> ⚠️ Supplements are regulated as foods in most jurisdictions, not as medicines. In most countries no authority verifies efficacy, and pre-market safety review is minimal or absent. Anyone subject to anti-doping testing should assume that an uncertified supplement carries genuine risk of an adverse analytical finding, and that **strict liability applies** — the athlete is responsible regardless of intent.

## 1. Strong evidence

### Creatine monohydrate

The single best-evidenced sports supplement in existence, with over 500 peer-reviewed publications.

**Mechanism.** Creatine is synthesised endogenously (~1 g/day from glycine, arginine and methionine) and obtained from meat and fish (~1–2 g/day in an omnivorous diet). Supplementation raises intramuscular total creatine and phosphocreatine, expanding the phosphagen buffer described in file `01`. This allows more rapid ATP resynthesis during high-intensity work, which permits more total work per session, which drives greater training adaptation over time. There is also evidence for effects on satellite cell activity, cell volumisation and myogenic gene expression, though the performance mechanism is the well-established one.

**Dose and loading.** The ISSN position stand (Kreider et al., 2017) supports 3–5 g/day (or ~0.1 g/kg/day) for maintenance. **Loading (20 g/day in 4 divided doses for 5–7 days) saturates muscle stores faster but is not required** — the same saturation is reached in about 3–4 weeks at 3–5 g/day. Loading is worth it if there is a competition in two weeks; otherwise it just increases early GI discomfort and water weight.

**Form.** Monohydrate. Every other form — hydrochloride, ethyl ester, buffered ("Kre-Alkalyn"), liquid, nitrate — is more expensive and none has shown superiority. Creatine monohydrate is stable in solid form and in properly stored solutions; the "creatine degrades in water" claim applies to prolonged storage, not to mixing and drinking.

**Safety.** The ISSN stand states that short- and long-term supplementation **up to 30 g/day for 5 years is safe and well-tolerated** in healthy individuals and in patient populations ranging from infants to the elderly. The 2021 ISSN "common questions and misconceptions" paper systematically addresses the persistent myths: creatine does not cause kidney damage in people with normal renal function; it is not an anabolic steroid; the hair loss claim rests on a single small study of rugby players showing a change in DHT, never replicated with hair loss as an outcome; it does not cause dehydration or cramping (if anything the opposite in the heat); and it is not harmful in adolescents at recommended doses. Some early water retention (1–2 kg) is intracellular and is expected.

**Non-athletic effects.** The ISSN stand also documents evidence relating to post-exercise recovery, injury prevention, thermoregulation, rehabilitation, and concussion/spinal cord neuroprotection, plus clinical investigation in neurodegenerative disease, diabetes, osteoarthritis, fibromyalgia, ageing and depression.

**Cognition.** The brain contains creatine and expresses creatine kinase. There is a meta-analytic literature on creatine and cognitive function in adults; the pattern is that effects are clearest under **stress conditions** — sleep deprivation, mental fatigue, hypoxia — and in populations with low baseline creatine (vegetarians, older adults). Effects in well-rested omnivorous young adults appear small. Higher doses than the muscle dose may be needed for brain creatine elevation, and this is an active research area. Treat "creatine for cognition" as promising, dose-uncertain, and not yet settled.

**Who responds most.** Vegetarians and vegans, who start with lower muscle creatine, show the largest increases. Roughly 20–30% of people are relative non-responders because their baseline stores are already near saturation.

### Caffeine

The ISSN caffeine stand (Guest et al., 2021) is unusually clear:

- Acutely enhances muscular endurance, movement velocity, muscular strength, sprinting, jumping and throwing, plus a wide range of aerobic and anaerobic sport-specific actions.
- **Aerobic endurance shows the most consistent moderate-to-large benefit.**
- **3–6 mg/kg body mass** is the established effective range; minimum effective dose may be as low as 2 mg/kg; **9 mg/kg produces a high incidence of side-effects without added benefit.**
- Typically taken ~60 min pre-exercise; caffeinated gum acts faster.
- Works in both trained and untrained individuals.
- **Inter-individual variation** in response and side-effects is partly genetic (CYP1A2 and ADORA2A variants) and partly habituation-related.
- Ergogenic for cognitive function (attention, vigilance) in most individuals, and may improve performance under sleep deprivation.
- Supported for endurance in heat (3–6 mg/kg) and at altitude (4–6 mg/kg).
- Alternative delivery — gum, mouth rinse, gels, chews — works, primarily for aerobic exercise.

**Practical cautions.** Caffeine has a half-life of roughly 5–6 hours, longer in some individuals and with oral contraceptive use. Afternoon and evening doses measurably degrade sleep quality even when subjective sleep seems unaffected — which is self-defeating for anyone training seriously (file `09`). Habituation reduces some but not all of the ergogenic effect; the practice of cycling off is common but the evidence that it restores full responsiveness is limited.

### Protein powder

Whey, casein, soy, pea, rice, egg and blends. **There is nothing special about powder.** It is a food with a favourable protein-to-energy ratio, long shelf life, low cost per gram of protein, and high convenience. The Morton (2018) meta-analysis's finding of benefit from "protein supplementation" is a finding about **total protein intake**, not about powder — and the benefit ceased above 1.62 g/kg/day.

Choose on the basis of: cost per gram of protein, leucine content, digestibility, tolerance (lactose), dietary pattern, and third-party certification. Whey isolate versus concentrate matters only for lactose intolerance and marginal purity. "Time-release" and exotic blends are marketing.

### Vitamin D

Vitamin D status affects bone health, muscle function and immune function. Deficiency is common at high latitudes, in indoor athletes, in people with darker skin at high latitudes, and in those with limited sun exposure.

**The pattern that matters: correcting deficiency improves outcomes; supplementing sufficient people does not.** Trials in already-sufficient athletes generally show no performance benefit. This is the same pattern seen across the micronutrient literature.

Practical position: **test 25-hydroxyvitamin D rather than guessing.** If deficient, supplement to sufficiency under medical guidance. Do not megadose — very high doses have been associated with adverse outcomes including, paradoxically, increased fall risk in some elderly trials.

**[NA]** In Namibia and comparable low-latitude, high-insolation regions, deficiency is less prevalent than in Northern Europe but still occurs in people with dark skin who avoid sun, in those who cover extensively, and in indoor workers.

### Beta-alanine

The ISSN stand (Trexler et al., 2015):

- 4 weeks at **4–6 g/day** significantly augments muscle carnosine, acting as an intracellular pH buffer.
- Safe in healthy populations at recommended doses.
- **Paraesthesia (tingling)** is the only reported side-effect, attenuated by divided lower doses (1.6 g) or sustained-release formulations.
- 4–6 g/day for **at least 2–4 weeks** improves performance, **with the most pronounced effects in open-endpoint tasks and time trials lasting 1–4 minutes.**
- Attenuates neuromuscular fatigue, particularly in older subjects.
- More research is needed on strength, on endurance beyond 25 minutes, and on other health benefits.

The 1–4 minute window is the crucial qualifier. Beta-alanine is a genuinely effective supplement for 400 m–1500 m running, 100–400 m swimming, rowing, combat sports rounds and repeated high-intensity efforts. It does very little for a 5-rep set of squats or a marathon.

### Dietary nitrate / beetroot

Nitrate is reduced to nitrite by oral bacteria and then to nitric oxide, improving blood flow, reducing the oxygen cost of submaximal exercise and improving muscle contractile efficiency.

The evidence has become **less** impressive as it has matured. A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis of 23 RCTs (401 participants) found beetroot juice improved **time to peak power** in Wingate-based studies (SMD −0.92, 95% CI −1.29 to −0.54, p < 0.001) and **handgrip strength** (SMD 0.40, 95% CI 0.05–0.74, p = 0.027), but **no significant effects on countermovement jump, peak or mean power, 10 m or 20 m sprint, perceived exertion, heart rate or blood lactate**. Certainty was low for most outcomes and very low for 20 m sprint and blood lactate. The authors concluded the evidence does not support routine use for sprint performance.

The better-established use case remains **submaximal endurance in less-trained individuals** — the effect appears to shrink in elite endurance athletes, who already have high nitric oxide bioavailability. Note also that **antibacterial mouthwash abolishes the effect** by killing the oral bacteria that perform the first reduction step.

### Sodium bicarbonate

**Grgic and colleagues (2021)** conducted an umbrella review of eight meta-analyses of moderate-to-high methodological quality. Conclusions:

- Moderate-quality evidence for ergogenic effects on **peak and mean power in the Wingate test** and **Yo-Yo test performance**.
- Low-quality evidence for endurance events lasting **~45 s to 8 min**, muscle endurance and 2000 m rowing.
- Effect sizes ranged from trivial (0.09) to large (1.26); for most outcomes effects were comparable (pooled ES ~0.36–0.40).
- **No significant effect on general mean power, muscle strength or repeated-sprint ability.**
- More research is needed in women.

The practical limitation is well known: gastrointestinal distress at effective doses is common and can be severe enough to impair performance outright. Split dosing, enteric-coated capsules and taking it with a carbohydrate-rich meal reduce but do not eliminate the problem. This is a supplement to trial in training well before competition.

## 2. Weak, mixed or context-limited evidence

### HMB (beta-hydroxy-beta-methylbutyrate)

A leucine metabolite. The ISSN position stand (Wilson et al., 2013) is positive — 38 mg/kg/day enhancing hypertrophy, strength and power in trained and untrained populations, attenuating exercise-induced damage, and safe chronically.

**The honest addendum:** several of the studies underpinning the strongest claims reported effect sizes that are not physiologically plausible — including gains in lean mass and strength in trained athletes exceeding what supraphysiological testosterone produces (compare file `08`, where 600 mg/week of testosterone enanthate plus training produced 6.1 ± 0.6 kg of fat-free mass over 10 weeks). Independent replications in trained populations have been largely null. Several of the positive studies share authorship and funding relationships with HMB manufacturers.

**Current position:** HMB may have a small anti-catabolic role in specific clinical or highly catabolic contexts (bed rest, elderly sarcopenia, extreme energy deficit). For a healthy trained person eating adequate protein, the evidence for benefit is weak and the plausibility low, since sufficient leucine intake already provides HMB's precursor.

### BCAAs (leucine, isoleucine, valine)

**Wolfe (2017)** is definitive on the theory. Muscle protein synthesis requires all EAAs. In the post-absorptive state, the only source of the other EAAs is muscle protein breakdown, so the theoretical maximum stimulation of MPS from BCAAs alone is bounded by the breakdown rate — roughly 30% above synthesis, and realistically less because EAA oxidation cannot be fully suppressed. An extensive literature search found **no human studies quantifying the MPS response to orally ingested BCAAs alone**, and the two intravenous infusion studies found BCAAs **decreased** both muscle protein synthesis and breakdown — a decrease in turnover, with the catabolic state persisting. Wolfe concludes that the claim that dietary BCAAs stimulate MPS or produce an anabolic response in humans is **unwarranted**.

Practically: if you consume adequate complete protein, BCAAs add nothing. A whey shake contains more BCAAs than a BCAA supplement, plus the other EAAs that actually make it work. The only defensible niche is training fasted where whole protein is not tolerated, and even then EAAs (all nine) would be the rational choice over BCAAs.

### Citrulline / citrulline malate

Two meta-analyses reach different conclusions on different outcomes:

- **Vårvik, Bjørnsen and Gonzalez (2021)**, 8 studies, 137 participants: 6–8 g of citrulline malate 40–60 min pre-exercise increased repetitions to failure by **3 ± 5 reps (6.4% ± 7.9%)** versus placebo (p = 0.022), a **small SMD of 0.196**. Subanalysis showed a trend for lower body (p = 0.051) and no significant effect for upper body (p = 0.131).
- **Aguiar and Casonatto (2022)**, 4 studies, 138 assessments: **no overall effect on muscle strength** (SMD 0.13, 95% CI −0.21 to 0.46), and none for upper or lower limbs separately.

So: possibly a small effect on repetition volume, no effect on strength, and small study counts throughout. Note the wide standard deviation on the rep effect (3 ± 5 reps) — the effect is inconsistent between individuals and studies. Not worthless, not impressive.

### Ashwagandha (*Withania somnifera*)

An adaptogenic herb with a growing trial literature reporting effects on strength, VO2max, testosterone, cortisol and stress/anxiety measures.

**The reason for caution:** a large proportion of the positive trials are conducted in India, are funded by or affiliated with the manufacturers of the specific extract tested, and report **implausibly large effects**. One recent trial reported a 15.31 kg increase in bench press 1RM and a 30.56 mL/kg/min increase in VO2max over 90 days from 300 mg/day of a sustained-release extract — figures that exceed what any pharmacological intervention produces, and that should be treated as a red flag for methodological or reporting problems rather than as a finding.

There is a more credible signal for **anxiolytic and stress-reduction effects**, and plausibly for sleep quality, which could indirectly benefit training. Independent, well-powered, non-industry replications in the performance domain are lacking.

Safety note: cases of hepatotoxicity associated with ashwagandha have been reported, and it may affect thyroid hormone levels. It is not recommended in pregnancy.

### Collagen and gelatin

**Shaw and colleagues (2017)** gave eight healthy men 5 g or 15 g of vitamin C-enriched gelatin one hour before six minutes of rope-skipping, three times daily for three days. The 15 g group showed **doubled amino-terminal propeptide of collagen I** in blood, indicating increased collagen synthesis, and serum from supplemented subjects increased collagen content and improved mechanics in engineered ligaments.

This is a genuinely interesting mechanistic result, but note: n = 8, three days, biomarker and *in vitro* outcomes, no clinical endpoint. Whether this translates into fewer injuries or faster tendon rehabilitation in practice is **not established**. The protocol that follows from the study — ~15 g of gelatin or collagen peptides with vitamin C, roughly one hour before loading the target tissue — is cheap and harmless, so it is a reasonable bet for someone rehabilitating a tendon, framed as a bet rather than as a treatment.

Collagen is a **poor-quality protein** for muscle purposes (very low in essential amino acids, no tryptophan) and should not count toward a protein target.

### Glutamine

The most abundant free amino acid in the body, with strong theoretical rationale for immune function and gut integrity — and consistently null results in healthy, adequately fed athletes. Supplementation has not been shown to improve muscle mass, strength, recovery or immune outcomes in this population. It has genuine clinical use in critical illness, burns and short bowel syndrome. For a healthy trainee it is a waste of money.

### ZMA (zinc, magnesium aspartate, vitamin B6)

Marketed for testosterone and sleep. The original positive trial has not been replicated; subsequent trials in non-deficient athletes show no effect on testosterone or performance. Zinc and magnesium repletion matters **if deficient**; ZMA specifically has no demonstrated benefit beyond that.

### Testosterone boosters generally

A large product category with essentially no supporting evidence in eugonadal men. **Tribulus terrestris** is the archetype: a 2025 systematic review of 10 studies (483 participants, 50% rated low methodological quality by Jadad score) found that **eight of ten studies reported no significant change in androgen profile**. The two showing an intra-group increase in total testosterone found a change of only 60–70 ng/dL, of low clinical magnitude, in hypogonadal men. The review's conclusion: **"no robust evidence was found for increasing testosterone levels."**

The same pattern applies across the category — D-aspartic acid (initial positive trial not replicated; subsequent trials null or showing decreases at higher doses), fenugreek, longjack/tongkat ali, maca, deer antler velvet. Where blood testosterone does move, the change is typically within normal diurnal variation and has no demonstrated functional consequence.

**The uncomfortable pattern:** a "test booster" that produces a visible physique change is a product that probably contains something undeclared. See section 4.

### Fat burners

Thermogenic products typically combine caffeine with some subset of green tea catechins, synephrine, yohimbine, capsaicinoids and various herbs.

- **Caffeine** does increase energy expenditure and fat oxidation acutely, by a small amount, with tolerance developing.
- **Green tea catechins (EGCG)** have a small effect in meta-analyses — on the order of a few hundred kJ/day — which is within the noise of daily intake variation.
- **Synephrine, yohimbine** and similar sympathomimetics have small acute metabolic effects and real cardiovascular side-effect profiles.
- **Everything else** in the category has minimal or no evidence.

The honest summary: the entire measurable effect of a well-formulated fat burner is roughly equivalent to a cup of coffee plus an appetite suppressant effect, and is trivial next to the energy deficit itself. This category also has one of the highest adulteration rates (section 4) — historically with sibutramine, DMAA, DMBA, phenolphthalein and thyroid hormones.

### Other commonly asked-about items, briefly

| Product | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Multivitamins** | Insurance against dietary gaps; no demonstrated benefit in people eating adequately. Not harmful at RDA-level doses |
| **Omega-3 (EPA/DHA)** | Reasonable evidence for cardiovascular and anti-inflammatory effects; modest evidence for muscle protein synthesis in older adults; small evidence for DOMS. A defensible general-health supplement, especially for non-fish-eaters |
| **Beta-hydroxybutyrate / exogenous ketones** | Expensive; performance evidence is null-to-negative in most trials; significant GI distress |
| **Arginine** | Poorly absorbed (extensive first-pass metabolism); citrulline is the better nitric oxide precursor. Null for performance |
| **Turmeric / curcumin** | Poor bioavailability without enhancers; modest evidence for DOMS and joint pain; not an established ergogenic |
| **Nootropic pre-workouts** | The caffeine works. The proprietary blend is usually a way to avoid disclosing how little of everything else is present |
| **Deer antler velvet, colostrum, "growth hormone boosters"** | No credible evidence |
| **Alkaline water, structured water, hydrogen water** | No credible mechanism or evidence |

## 3. Third-party testing and contamination

### The contamination data

**Geyer and colleagues (2004)** conducted the reference international study for the IOC. Between October 2000 and November 2001 they purchased **634 non-hormonal nutritional supplements** in 13 countries from 215 suppliers, and analysed them by GC-MS for 11 anabolic androgenic steroids.

- **94 of 634 samples (14.8%) contained anabolic androgenic steroids not declared on the label.**
- A further 66 samples (10.4%) gave unreliable data due to matrix effects.
- By country of purchase, the highest positive rates were the Netherlands (25.8%), Austria (22.7%), the UK (18.8%) and the USA (18.8%).
- All positive supplements came from companies located in only five countries: the USA, the Netherlands, the UK, Italy and Germany.
- **21.1% of supplements from prohormone-selling companies were positive**, versus **9.6% from companies not selling prohormones**.
- Concentrations ranged from 0.01 µg/g to 190 µg/g.
- Critically: consuming supplements containing nandrolone prohormones totalling **more than 1 µg** produced **positive doping results for norandrosterone for several hours**.

That last point is the operational one. A contamination level far too low to have any pharmacological effect is more than enough to end a career.

Contamination is not confined to steroids. Stimulants (DMAA, DMBA, higenamine, ephedrine analogues), sibutramine, sildenafil analogues, phenolphthalein, and undeclared thyroid hormones have all been repeatedly identified in the analytical literature, most often in weight-loss, pre-workout and "male enhancement" products.

### Certification schemes

| Scheme | What it does | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Informed Sport / Informed Choice** (LGC) | Batch-by-batch testing for WADA-prohibited substances; certified batches are listed and searchable | Tests for banned substances, **not** for efficacy or label accuracy of the active ingredient. Only certified *batches* are covered |
| **NSF Certified for Sport** | Tests for over 280 banned substances, verifies label claims for content, and audits manufacturing facilities (GMP) | Same scope limits; smaller product range |
| **Banned Substance Control Group (BSCG)** | Similar batch testing model | Less widely recognised |
| **USP Verified / general GMP certification** | Verifies identity, potency, purity and manufacturing quality | Does **not** test for sports-banned substances specifically |

**What certification does and does not tell you.** It tells you the tested batch did not contain detectable quantities of the substances screened for. It does not tell you the product works, that the dose is meaningful, that the label is accurate outside NSF's verification, or that a different batch is equally clean.

For athletes under anti-doping jurisdiction the practical rule is: **use only certified batches, keep the batch numbers and packaging, and prefer single-ingredient products from major manufacturers.** Proprietary blends and products marketed for "test support", "hardening", "recomposition" or rapid fat loss carry the highest risk. Strict liability means a contamination defence rarely succeeds.

## 4. Regulation by country

| Jurisdiction | Framework | Practical effect |
|---|---|---|
| **United States** | Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act (DSHEA) 1994. Supplements are regulated as foods, not drugs | **No pre-market approval for safety or efficacy.** FDA must demonstrate a product is unsafe to remove it — a post-market, reactive model. Structure/function claims permitted with a disclaimer. The most permissive major market |
| **European Union** | Food Supplements Directive 2002/46/EC plus the Nutrition and Health Claims Regulation 1924/2006 | Positive lists of permitted vitamins and minerals; **health claims must be authorised by EFSA before use**, and EFSA has rejected the overwhelming majority of submitted claims. Considerably stricter than the US on claims |
| **United Kingdom** | Retained EU-derived framework post-Brexit, administered by the Food Standards Agency / FSS | Broadly similar to the EU; divergence is developing |
| **South Africa** **[ZA]** | Complementary medicines are regulated by SAHPRA under the Medicines and Related Substances Act 101 of 1965, with the complementary medicines regulatory framework introduced from 2013 and phased in | Moving toward a registration model with call-up phases by category; enforcement capacity is a limiting factor. Labelling and claim requirements exist but compliance is uneven |
| **Namibia** **[NA]** | Medicines and Related Substances Control Act 13 of 2003, administered by the Namibia Medicines Regulatory Council | Framework exists; a substantial proportion of the supplement market is imported from South Africa and elsewhere, and enforcement resource is limited. Verify against the Council's current registers |

The practical consequence for a consumer in southern Africa: much of the available product originates in the most permissive regulatory environments and arrives via distribution chains with limited additional verification. Third-party certification and major-manufacturer sourcing matter more, not less.

## 5. How to read a supplement study critically

A checklist:

1. **Who funded it, and who are the authors?** Industry funding does not invalidate a study, but it predicts direction of results. Check for author affiliations with the manufacturer of the specific tested extract.
2. **What was the control?** Placebo-controlled, double-blind, or an open comparison? Was the placebo genuinely indistinguishable (caffeine and beta-alanine are hard to blind — caffeine is bitter and stimulating, beta-alanine tingles)?
3. **How many participants, and were they trained?** A 10-person untrained study tells you very little about a trained population.
4. **Is the effect size plausible?** Compare against known reference points. If a herb produces more lean mass than 600 mg/week of testosterone (6.1 kg in 10 weeks, Bhasin 1996), something is wrong with the study.
5. **What is the outcome measure?** An acute biomarker (blood flow, MPS at 3 hours, hormone concentration) is not a training outcome. Many supplement papers report only surrogate endpoints.
6. **Was the primary outcome pre-registered?** Unregistered studies with many outcomes and one significant result are almost certainly reporting noise.
7. **Has it been replicated independently?** The single most useful question. Most positive supplement findings do not replicate.
8. **Is there a meta-analysis, and what is its heterogeneity and GRADE rating?** A pooled estimate with I² above 75% and "very low" GRADE certainty is not a strong basis for a purchase.
9. **Does the marketing match the study?** A study using 6 g of an ingredient does not support a product containing 300 mg of it inside a proprietary blend.
10. **What is the dose in the product versus the dose in the study?** "Proprietary blend" is a mechanism for hiding under-dosing. If the total blend weight is 2 g and it lists 12 ingredients, most are present in cosmetic quantities.
