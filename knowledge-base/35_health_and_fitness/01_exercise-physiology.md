# Exercise physiology — muscle, energy systems, adaptation and fatigue

**Summary.** Skeletal muscle produces force by cyclical actin–myosin cross-bridge cycling, driven by ATP and controlled by motor units that are recruited in an orderly size sequence. Energy to resynthesise ATP comes from three overlapping systems — phosphagen, glycolytic and oxidative — that operate simultaneously with different power outputs and capacities, not in sequence. Training adaptations occur at every level: contractile protein accretion, mitochondrial biogenesis, capillarisation, cardiac remodelling, substrate handling, and — often first and largest — the nervous system. Two beliefs that dominated popular training theory for decades have not survived scrutiny: that lactate is a waste product causing fatigue, and that acute post-exercise hormone spikes drive hypertrophy. Neither is supported.

## 1. Skeletal muscle structure

Skeletal muscle is a hierarchy of parallel bundles. A whole muscle is wrapped in **epimysium**; within it, **fascicles** wrapped in **perimysium**; within each fascicle, individual **muscle fibres** (multinucleated cells) wrapped in **endomysium**. Each fibre contains hundreds to thousands of **myofibrils**, and each myofibril is a chain of **sarcomeres** — the contractile unit, bounded by Z-discs, containing interdigitating thin (actin) and thick (myosin) filaments.

The connective tissue sheaths are not passive packaging. They are continuous with tendon, transmit force laterally between fibres, and contribute a substantial fraction of passive tension at long muscle lengths. This lateral transmission is why a muscle with damaged fibres can still produce force, and why tendon and aponeurosis stiffness materially affect rate of force development.

**Architecture** matters more than most training discussions acknowledge. Fibres may run parallel to the line of pull (e.g. sartorius, biceps brachii — favouring shortening velocity and excursion) or at an angle to it (**pennate**: vastus lateralis, gastrocnemius, deltoid — packing more physiological cross-sectional area into a given volume and favouring force). Pennation angle increases with hypertrophy, which is one reason muscle volume and maximal force do not scale one-to-one.

### Fibre types

Fibres are classified by their dominant **myosin heavy chain (MHC)** isoform:

- **Type I** — slow-twitch, oxidative. High mitochondrial and capillary density, high myoglobin, fatigue-resistant, lower peak power, smaller motor units.
- **Type IIa** — fast-twitch oxidative-glycolytic. Intermediate: fast contractile speed with respectable oxidative capacity.
- **Type IIx** — fast-twitch glycolytic. Highest power and speed, lowest fatigue resistance. In humans the "IIb" designation from rodent literature is inaccurate; human MHC-IIb protein is essentially absent, and what older texts called IIb is IIx.

Hybrid fibres (I/IIa, IIa/IIx) are common and represent transitional states. Training shifts fibre type along the continuum: essentially any resistance or endurance training shifts IIx toward IIa. Conversion between Type I and Type II is far more limited in humans; the practical position is that you train the fibres you have, and that fibre-type composition is largely a fixed constraint on athletic ceiling.

Hypertrophy is not fibre-type-neutral. Type II fibres show substantially greater hypertrophic potential — in the Roberts and colleagues (2015) strength-training trial, the measured gain was in Type II fibre cross-sectional area (17% in the control-recovery group). This is one mechanistic reason why training that reliably recruits high-threshold motor units matters.

### The sliding filament mechanism

Contraction shortens the sarcomere without shortening the filaments themselves. The sequence:

1. An action potential travels along the sarcolemma and down the **T-tubules**.
2. The voltage sensor (dihydropyridine receptor) mechanically couples to the **ryanodine receptor** on the sarcoplasmic reticulum, releasing Ca²⁺ into the cytosol.
3. Ca²⁺ binds **troponin C**, which shifts **tropomyosin** off the myosin-binding sites on actin.
4. A myosin head, already energised by prior ATP hydrolysis, binds actin to form a **cross-bridge**.
5. The **power stroke**: the head pivots, sliding the thin filament toward the sarcomere centre, releasing ADP and Pᵢ.
6. A new ATP binds myosin, detaching it. Hydrolysis re-cocks the head. The cycle repeats while Ca²⁺ and ATP are present.
7. **SERCA** pumps Ca²⁺ back into the SR (an ATP-consuming step — relaxation costs energy too), and the muscle relaxes.

Rigor mortis is the direct demonstration of step 6: without ATP, cross-bridges cannot detach.

Force depends on the number of attached cross-bridges, which depends on filament overlap — the **length–tension relationship**. Too short, and thin filaments collide and overlap each other; too long, and overlap is insufficient. Additionally, **eccentric** (lengthening) actions produce more force than isometric or concentric at the same activation, partly through cross-bridge mechanics and partly through the giant elastic protein **titin**, which stiffens on Ca²⁺ binding. This is why you can lower more than you can lift, and it underlies eccentric-overload training methods.

## 2. Motor units and the size principle

A **motor unit** is one alpha motor neurone and every fibre it innervates. All fibres in a unit share a type. Innervation ratios vary enormously with the precision demanded: extraocular muscles may have ratios near 1:10, gastrocnemius closer to 1:1000–2000.

**Henneman's size principle**: motor units are recruited in order of motor neurone size, which correlates with the size and force of the unit. Small, low-threshold, Type I units fire first; as force demand rises, progressively larger, higher-threshold, Type II units are added. The ordering is highly consistent within a movement, though it can differ *between* movements involving the same muscle.

Two practical consequences dominate resistance training:

1. **Force output, not load, drives recruitment.** A heavy load recruits high-threshold units immediately. A light load recruits them only as fatigue accumulates and the low-threshold units can no longer meet the demand. This is the mechanistic core of the "effective reps" idea in file `02`, and it explains why low-load training taken close to failure produces hypertrophy comparable to heavy training (Schoenfeld 2017).
2. **Rate coding supplements recruitment.** Above roughly 80–90% of maximal voluntary force in most muscles, essentially all units are recruited, and further force increases come from increased firing frequency. In small muscles full recruitment occurs at lower relative forces than in large ones.

Force is also modulated by **doublet discharges** (a rapid pair of action potentials that disproportionately raises force) and by **synchronisation** across units — both trainable, both contributing to rate of force development more than to maximal force.

## 3. Energy systems — the real time courses

The three systems are not a relay race. They all run continuously; what changes is their *relative contribution*.

### Phosphagen (ATP-PCr) system

Stored ATP in muscle is tiny — enough for perhaps 2–3 s of maximal work. **Phosphocreatine** buffers it: creatine kinase transfers a phosphate from PCr to ADP, regenerating ATP essentially instantaneously and without oxygen. The adenylate kinase reaction (2 ADP → ATP + AMP) adds a small further contribution.

- Power: highest of the three systems.
- Duration at maximal output: PCr is largely depleted within ~10 s of all-out effort.
- Recovery: PCr resynthesis is oxygen-dependent, with a half-time of roughly 20–30 s in healthy muscle; ~95% restoration typically takes 3–5 min. This is the direct physiological argument for long rest intervals in strength training.

Creatine supplementation raises muscle total creatine, expanding this buffer — the mechanistic basis for its well-established ergogenic effect (file `07`).

### Glycolytic system

Glucose (from blood) or glucose-1-phosphate (from muscle glycogen) is broken to pyruvate, yielding a net 2 ATP per glucose (3 per glycogen-derived glucose unit, since the hexokinase step is bypassed). Under high flux, pyruvate is reduced to **lactate** by lactate dehydrogenase, regenerating NAD⁺ so glycolysis can continue.

- Power: intermediate, roughly half that of the phosphagen system.
- Dominance window: roughly 10 s to 2 min of maximal effort.
- Limits: substrate is not the constraint over these durations; the constraint is intramuscular acidosis and accumulating inorganic phosphate, plus the metabolic disturbance itself.

The old teaching that lactate *causes* acidosis is wrong on the chemistry. Protons come predominantly from ATP hydrolysis under conditions where mitochondrial ATP resynthesis cannot keep pace; lactate production actually *consumes* a proton. Lactate is a correlate of the acidosis, not its cause.

### Oxidative system

Pyruvate enters mitochondria, is decarboxylated by pyruvate dehydrogenase to acetyl-CoA, and enters the citric acid cycle; reducing equivalents (NADH, FADH₂) drive the electron transport chain and oxidative phosphorylation. Fatty acids enter by beta-oxidation, also producing acetyl-CoA. Complete oxidation of one glucose yields roughly 30–32 ATP; a typical 18-carbon fatty acid yields well over 100.

- Power: lowest, but capacity is effectively unlimited on stored substrate (even a lean athlete carries tens of thousands of kJ as fat).
- Dominance: beyond ~2 min, and at all times at rest and in recovery.
- Fuel mix shifts with intensity: fat oxidation is proportionally highest at low intensities and absolute fat oxidation peaks around 45–65% VO2max in trained individuals, falling as carbohydrate takes over at higher intensities (the "crossover concept").

The practical implication for a lifter is that the oxidative system, though invisible in a heavy set, governs the between-set recovery of PCr and therefore the quality of the whole session. Aerobic fitness improves repeatability of high-power efforts.

## 4. VO2max and its determinants

**VO2max** is the maximal rate of oxygen consumption during incremental exercise to exhaustion. By the Fick equation:

> VO₂ = Q̇ × (a-v̄)O₂ difference = (HR × SV) × (a-v̄)O₂ difference

so its determinants are cardiac output (heart rate × stroke volume) and the arteriovenous oxygen difference (oxygen extraction).

In healthy people at sea level, **central factors dominate** — specifically maximal stroke volume and therefore maximal cardiac output. Maximal heart rate is largely genetically determined, declines with age, and is *not* trainable (it may fall slightly with endurance training). Stroke volume is highly trainable. Peripheral factors — capillary density, mitochondrial volume, myoglobin — are more limiting for *sustainable* fractional utilisation than for VO2max itself, though they matter greatly for performance.

Common age-prediction formulae for maximal heart rate (the familiar 220 − age, and the better-fitting Tanaka equation, 208 − 0.7 × age) carry a standard deviation of roughly ±10–12 beats/min. Prescribing training zones from a predicted maximum is therefore imprecise for any individual.

VO2max is highly heritable, has a large trainable component in the untrained, and shows striking individual response variability — the HERITAGE Family Study documented essentially non-responders alongside responders gaining well over 40% in the same standardised programme. Its clinical significance is covered in file `10`: cardiorespiratory fitness is among the strongest available predictors of all-cause mortality (Mandsager 2018).

## 5. Lactate, the lactate threshold, and the modern picture

Lactate has been comprehensively rehabilitated. Brooks' **lactate shuttle** framework, summarised in his 2018 *Cell Metabolism* review, establishes three roles:

1. **A major fuel.** Lactate produced in glycolytic fibres is transported (via monocarboxylate transporters) to oxidative fibres, cardiac muscle, brain and liver, where it is oxidised. It is a preferred substrate for the heart and, during exercise, a significant fuel for the brain.
2. **The major gluconeogenic precursor.** Via the Cori cycle in the liver.
3. **A signalling molecule** ("lactormone") influencing gene expression, including mitochondrial biogenesis pathways, and acting on receptors such as HCAR1.

Lactate forms continuously under fully aerobic conditions; its appearance in blood reflects the balance of production and clearance, not the onset of anaerobiosis. Brooks notes that clinicians increasingly read raised blood lactate as a *strain* rather than a *stress* biomarker.

**Thresholds.** Two are commonly identified in the ramp test:

- **LT1 / aerobic threshold / first lactate turn point** — the first sustained rise above baseline, typically ~2 mmol/L, often near the top of "zone 2" training. Sustainable for hours in trained athletes.
- **LT2 / anaerobic threshold / maximal lactate steady state (MLSS) / critical power** — the highest intensity at which production and clearance still balance. Above it, blood lactate rises inexorably and exercise terminates within tens of minutes. Often approximated at ~4 mmol/L (OBLA), though the true individual MLSS may sit anywhere from ~2.5 to ~6 mmol/L, so the fixed-4 convention is a rough proxy.

For endurance performance, **fractional utilisation** (the percentage of VO2max sustainable at threshold) and **movement economy** discriminate between athletes with similar VO2max far better than VO2max itself. Two runners with identical VO2max can differ by many minutes over a marathon on economy and threshold alone.

## 6. Cardiac adaptations

Chronic training remodels the heart in a load-dependent way — the classic **Morganroth hypothesis**, now understood as a spectrum rather than a dichotomy:

- **Endurance training** imposes chronic volume overload: elevated venous return and stroke volume for prolonged periods. The result is **eccentric hypertrophy** — increased left ventricular end-diastolic dimension with proportionally increased wall thickness, greater compliance, greater stroke volume, and lower resting heart rate.
- **Predominantly resistance training** imposes intermittent pressure overload (very high arterial pressures during a maximal effort, especially with a Valsalva manoeuvre). The classic description is **concentric hypertrophy** — increased wall thickness with less chamber dilation. Modern imaging studies find the effect is more modest than originally claimed in drug-free lifters, and studies of AAS-using lifters (Baggish 2017) show that a substantial part of the historically reported "strength athlete's heart" pathology is drug-associated rather than training-associated.

"Athlete's heart" is a physiological adaptation and is generally benign and reversible on detraining. Distinguishing it from hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is a genuine clinical problem and requires specialist assessment, not a training textbook.

Other cardiovascular adaptations: increased plasma volume (rapid — within days), increased total haemoglobin mass (slower), increased capillary density in trained muscle, improved endothelial function and arterial compliance, and lowered resting and submaximal heart rate.

## 7. Mitochondrial biogenesis

Endurance training increases mitochondrial volume density, cristae surface area and enzyme content. The signalling is well characterised:

- **Energetic stress** raises the AMP:ATP ratio, activating **AMPK**.
- **Ca²⁺ transients** activate CaMK.
- **Reactive oxygen species** and **p38 MAPK** contribute.
- These converge on **PGC-1α**, the master transcriptional coactivator, which upregulates nuclear respiratory factors (NRF-1, NRF-2) and mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM), coordinating the nuclear and mitochondrial genomes.

Mitochondrial adaptations respond both to volume of low-intensity work and to intensity: high-intensity interval training is a potent stimulus per unit time. Ma and colleagues' 2023 meta-analysis found HIIT superior to conventional training for VO2max even in elite athletes (SMD 0.58, 95% CI 0.30–0.87), though with only nine studies and 176 participants — a small evidence base.

There is a well-documented **interference effect** in which concurrent high-volume endurance training attenuates strength and hypertrophy adaptation, plausibly through AMPK–mTOR antagonism plus simple fatigue and recovery competition. The practical magnitude is modest and is largest for lower-body power and for very high endurance volumes; it is covered in file `03`.

## 8. The endocrine response to exercise — and what it does not explain

Resistance and high-intensity exercise acutely raise circulating **testosterone**, **growth hormone**, **cortisol**, **catecholamines** and, more variably, **IGF-1**. These transient elevations last minutes to an hour or two.

For thirty years, programme design advice was built on maximising this response — short rest intervals, high volume, large muscle mass involvement, "the hormonal response window". That framework has not survived testing.

- West and Phillips' work and, most decisively, **Morton and colleagues (2016)** examined 49 resistance-trained men through 12 weeks of whole-body training and found **no significant correlation between the acute post-exercise rise in any purported anabolic hormone and the change in muscle size or strength**.
- Studies pairing a small-muscle exercise with either a high-hormone or low-hormone systemic environment likewise fail to show a hypertrophic advantage from the hormonal milieu.
- Meanwhile, **local** signalling — mechanotransduction through integrins and focal adhesion kinase, mechanically sensitive phospholipase D and phosphatidic acid production, mTORC1 activation, satellite cell donation of myonuclei — does track with hypertrophy.

The honest summary: **chronically low androgen status impairs muscle mass** (see the dose-response data in file `08`, where supraphysiological testosterone produces large effects), but **acute within-normal-range fluctuations after a training session do not meaningfully drive adaptation**. Programming a session to "spike growth hormone" is a category error.

**Cortisol** deserves a separate note. It rises acutely with exercise, which is normal and necessary — it mobilises substrate. Chronically elevated cortisol from inadequate recovery, energy deficiency or psychological stress is catabolic and immunosuppressive, but a single elevated post-workout measurement means nothing. The training-relevant question is chronic load and recovery balance, covered in file `09`.

## 9. Neural adaptations

The first weeks of strength training produce strength gains that far outstrip measurable muscle growth. The mechanisms:

- **Increased agonist drive** — greater voluntary activation, higher motor unit discharge rates, more doublets.
- **Reduced antagonist co-contraction** — less braking by opposing muscles.
- **Improved intermuscular coordination** — better timing and sequencing across the whole kinetic chain, a genuinely skill-like adaptation.
- **Reduced neural inhibition** — decreased Golgi tendon organ and other protective inhibition.
- **Cortical and spinal changes** — altered corticospinal excitability and motor cortical representation; the evidence for specific sites is mixed and evolving.
- **Cross-education** — training one limb produces measurable strength gains in the untrained contralateral limb, typically on the order of half the trained-limb gain. This is unambiguously neural and is clinically useful during unilateral immobilisation.

Neural adaptation is highly **task-specific**. Strength gained in a back squat transfers imperfectly to a leg press and poorly to a novel movement. This is the physiological basis of the specificity principle in file `02`.

## 10. The physiology of fatigue

Fatigue is best defined as an exercise-induced reduction in the capacity to produce force, and it is not one thing.

**Peripheral fatigue** (distal to the neuromuscular junction):
- **Substrate depletion.** PCr depletion in short maximal efforts; muscle glycogen depletion in prolonged work — the classic "hitting the wall" at roughly 90–120 min of hard endurance exercise, corresponding to glycogen exhaustion in the recruited fibres.
- **Metabolite accumulation.** Inorganic phosphate is probably the single most important, impairing cross-bridge force and Ca²⁺ release; H⁺ contributes but its role has been substantially downgraded from the older "lactic acid" account, particularly at physiological temperatures.
- **Excitation–contraction coupling failure.** Impaired SR Ca²⁺ release, including through ryanodine receptor dysfunction.
- **Structural damage** from unaccustomed eccentric work — Z-band streaming, membrane disruption, and the delayed force loss of DOMS.

**Central fatigue** (proximal): reduced voluntary drive to the muscle, demonstrable by superimposed twitch interpolation. Contributors include afferent feedback from group III/IV muscle afferents signalling metabolic disturbance, altered serotonergic and dopaminergic activity, ammonia accumulation, hypoglycaemia and hyperthermia.

**Integrative models.** Noakes' "central governor" and Marcora's psychobiological model both propose that endurance performance is limited by a regulated, anticipatory reduction in drive rather than by catastrophic peripheral failure — task disengagement rather than mechanical incapacity. The models remain contested; the strong version (that peripheral limits are never reached) is not well supported, but the weak version — that perceived effort and motivation modulate the point of task failure — is now mainstream.

**Delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS)** deserves demoting. It peaks 24–72 h after unaccustomed eccentric work, reflects inflammation and connective tissue involvement, and — critically — **does not index the hypertrophic stimulus**. Damas and colleagues (2016) showed that the largest muscle damage and the largest myofibrillar protein synthesis response occurred in the *first* week of training, when there was *no* measurable hypertrophy; only after damage attenuated (week 3 onward) did protein synthesis correlate with subsequent growth (r ≈ 0.9). Chasing soreness is chasing the wrong signal.
