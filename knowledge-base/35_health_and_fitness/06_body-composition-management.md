# Body composition management — gaining, losing, measuring, and the risks at both extremes

**Summary.** Fat loss is driven by a sustained energy deficit; the practical questions are how large, for how long, and at what cost. Slower loss preserves lean mass better — in elite athletes, 0.7% of body mass per week preserved and even increased lean body mass, while 1.4% per week did not. Adequate protein and continued resistance training are the two interventions that most reliably protect muscle in a deficit. Refeeds and diet breaks have modest but real supporting evidence. Body composition measurement is far less precise than the two-decimal-place readouts suggest, and no field method resolves a 1% change. Both extremes of body composition carry documented health risks; low energy availability in particular produces the multi-system syndrome now formally described as **Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs)**.

> ⚠️ **This file is not a prescription for restriction.** It describes the physiology and the trade-offs of changing body composition. Anyone with a history of disordered eating, or who finds that tracking food or body metrics generates distress or compulsion, should not implement any of it without professional support. Body composition is not a health outcome in itself.

## 1. Rate of fat loss and its trade-offs

**Garthe and colleagues (2011)** is the most directly relevant controlled trial. Twenty-four elite athletes were randomised to lose either 0.7% of body mass per week (slow reduction, SR) or 1.4% per week (fast reduction, FR), both with four resistance-training sessions per week. Both groups lost 5.5–5.6% of body mass; the slow group took 8.5 ± 2.2 weeks, the fast group 5.3 ± 0.9 weeks.

The outcomes diverged sharply:
- **Lean body mass increased 2.1% ± 0.4% in the slow group** (p < 0.001) and was **unchanged in the fast group** (−0.2% ± 0.7%), with a significant between-group difference (p < 0.01).
- **Fat mass fell 31% ± 3% in the slow group versus 21% ± 4% in the fast group** — the slower approach lost *more* fat despite matched total weight loss, because the fast group lost lean tissue alongside it.
- Energy restriction was 19% ± 2% (slow) versus 30% ± 4% (fast).

The authors' conclusion is the practical rule: athletes wanting to gain lean body mass and increase 1RM strength during weight loss **should aim for a weekly loss of about 0.7% of body mass**.

The ISSN diets stand adds the necessary nuance: **"the higher the baseline body fat level, the more aggressively the caloric deficit may be imposed. Slower rates of weight loss can better preserve lean mass in leaner subjects."** A person at 35% body fat has large fat reserves to draw from and can tolerate a deficit of 25–30%; a person at 10% body fat cannot, because at low body fat the body increasingly draws on lean tissue.

**Trade-offs at faster rates:**
- Greater lean mass loss.
- Greater reduction in training performance (both output and recovery).
- Larger and faster adaptive thermogenesis.
- Greater hunger, worse mood, poorer sleep.
- Higher dropout and higher probability of a binge-restrict cycle.

**Trade-offs at slower rates:**
- Longer duration in a deficit, with the psychological cost that implies.
- Slower visible progress, which some people find demotivating.
- More opportunity for life events to interrupt.

## 2. Setting the energy deficit

A practical procedure:

1. **Estimate maintenance from measured intake, not from a formula.** Track intake and body mass for 10–14 days at stable weight. That intake is maintenance. Formulae are a fallback when tracking is not possible.
2. **Choose a target rate**, expressed as a percentage of body mass per week: 0.5–1.0%/wk is the standard range. Use the lower end if lean, the upper end if carrying substantial excess fat.
3. **Convert to an energy deficit.** Approximately 32 MJ (~7 700 kcal) per kilogram of body fat is the classical figure. A 1%/week loss for an 80 kg person is 0.8 kg/week, implying roughly a 3 700 kJ/day (~880 kcal/day) deficit — which is aggressive. This arithmetic is a starting estimate; the true deficit needed will drift upward over time as body mass and NEAT fall.
4. **Adjust from measured outcome.** Use a **7-day rolling average of daily body mass**, not single weigh-ins. Day-to-day fluctuation of 1–2 kg from glycogen, water, sodium, gut contents and (in women) menstrual cycle phase is normal and swamps genuine change.
5. **Re-estimate every 3–4 weeks.** The deficit that produced 0.7%/week in week 1 will not do so in week 12.

**Where the deficit comes from** — food restriction or added activity — matters less than its size, but activity-based deficits are limited in practice by time and recovery, and by the compensation described in file `05`. A mixed approach is usually more sustainable.

## 3. Protein and muscle retention in a deficit

Two interventions dominate lean mass retention:

**1. Adequate protein.** The ISSN position is that **2.3–3.1 g/kg fat-free mass per day** may be required to maximise lean mass retention in lean, resistance-trained subjects under hypocaloric conditions. For someone at 80 kg with 15% body fat (68 kg FFM), that is roughly 156–211 g/day — considerably above the ~1.6 g/kg body mass maintenance figure. Higher protein also raises the thermic effect of food and improves satiety, both of which help.

**2. Continued resistance training.** Resistance training is the signal that tells the body that muscle is functionally necessary. Removing it during a deficit is the fastest route to losing lean mass. Practical guidance:
- **Maintain intensity (load), reduce volume if necessary.** Heavy loading is the primary retention signal; volume can be trimmed to manage recovery. Cutting load while maintaining volume is exactly backwards.
- Expect performance to decline somewhat in a deficit. Maintaining loads is a good outcome; progressing them is a bonus, and is realistic mainly for novices or those returning after a layoff.
- Avoid the common error of *adding* volume during a cut on the theory that more work burns more fat. This raises recovery demand at the exact moment recovery capacity is lowest.

**3. Sleep.** Under-appreciated and quantitatively large. Nedeltcheva and colleagues (2010) restricted sleep from 8.5 to 5.5 hours during identical moderate caloric restriction: the proportion of weight lost as fat **fell by 55%** (1.4 vs 0.6 kg) and loss of fat-free mass **rose by 60%** (1.5 vs 2.4 kg), accompanied by increased hunger and a shift toward oxidising less fat. Sleep is a body-composition intervention. See file `09`.

## 4. Refeeds and diet breaks — the evidence

Two distinct interventions, often conflated:

- A **refeed** is a short (1–2 day) planned increase in energy intake, usually specifically carbohydrate, within an otherwise ongoing deficit.
- A **diet break** is a longer (1–2 week) return to approximately maintenance intake.

### Diet breaks: MATADOR

**Byrne and colleagues (2018)** randomised 51 men with obesity to 16 weeks of continuous energy restriction, or intermittent restriction delivered as 8 × 2-week blocks of restriction alternating with 7 × 2-week blocks of energy balance (30 weeks total). Both restricted to 67% of maintenance during restriction phases.

Results for per-protocol completers (19 continuous, 17 intermittent):
- **Weight loss: 14.1 ± 5.6 kg (intermittent) vs 9.1 ± 2.9 kg (continuous), p < 0.001** — despite matched time in deficit.
- **Fat mass loss: 12.3 ± 4.8 vs 8.0 ± 4.2 kg, p < 0.01.** FFM loss was similar (1.8 ± 1.6 vs 1.2 ± 2.5 kg, p = 0.4).
- Absolute REE reduction did not differ (−502 ± 481 vs −624 ± 557 kJ/d, p = 0.5), but **after adjusting for body composition changes, the reduction was significantly smaller in the intermittent group** (−360 ± 502 vs −749 ± 498 kJ/d, p < 0.05).
- Mean weight change during the 2-week energy-balance blocks was 0.0 ± 0.3 kg — the breaks did not undo progress.

Caveats: obese, non-athletic men; substantial dropout; the intermittent protocol took nearly twice as long in calendar time. But the mechanistic claim — that periodic energy-balance blocks attenuate metabolic adaptation — is supported.

### Refeeds: the trained-population evidence

**Campbell and colleagues (2020)** randomised 27 resistance-trained men and women to a ~25% deficit delivered either continuously for 7 weeks, or as 5 days restricted plus 2 consecutive days of elevated carbohydrate. Body mass and fat mass fell similarly in both groups. However, **fat-free mass, dry fat-free mass and RMR were better maintained in the refeed group** (FFM: −0.4 kg vs −1.3 kg, p = 0.006).

A published critique noted that on reanalysis only dry fat-free mass differed significantly between groups, and the authors replied. Treat the refeed effect as **plausible and modestly supported, not established**.

### Practical synthesis

- Diet breaks are worth using on any deficit lasting more than roughly 8–12 weeks: 1–2 weeks at maintenance, then resume.
- Refeeds are worth using weekly or fortnightly in leaner individuals or during longer diets, delivered mainly as carbohydrate.
- The **psychological** benefit — a structured, guilt-free break from restriction — may be as important as the metabolic one and is much better attested anecdotally than experimentally.
- Neither is a licence for uncontrolled overeating. A "cheat day" that erases four days of deficit is not a refeed.

## 5. The physiology of weight regain

Most weight lost is regained. The mechanisms are biological as well as behavioural:

- **Reduced RMR** beyond that explained by tissue loss (Fothergill 2016: −499 ± 207 kcal/day at six years).
- **Reduced NEAT** — spontaneous movement falls and stays low.
- **Leptin falls** disproportionately to fat mass, and leptin is the primary long-term adiposity signal to the hypothalamus. Low leptin drives hunger, reduces satiety, reduces thyroid output and reduces sympathetic tone.
- **Ghrelin rises** and stays elevated, increasing hunger.
- **Satiety hormones fall** (PYY, GLP-1, CCK).
- **Adipocyte hyperplasia is not reversed.** Fat cell number, once increased, is essentially permanent; cells shrink but remain, and shrunken adipocytes appear to be metabolically primed for refilling.
- **Hedonic and environmental drivers** — food cues, stress, social eating — are unchanged by the diet.

The practical conclusion is not despair but planning: **the maintenance phase is the intervention**, and it requires the same deliberate structure as the loss phase. The strongest predictors of successful maintenance in observational registries are continued self-monitoring, high physical activity, regular breakfast, and consistent eating patterns across weekdays and weekends. Fothergill's finding that those maintaining the greatest loss showed the greatest metabolic slowing (r = 0.59, p = 0.025) makes the point starkly: the people succeeding are the ones fighting hardest, not the ones for whom it became easy.

## 6. Lean bulking versus aggressive surplus, and the p-ratio

The **p-ratio** (partitioning ratio) is the proportion of a change in body mass that comes from lean tissue versus fat. In a surplus, a favourable p-ratio means more of the gain is muscle.

Determinants of the p-ratio, roughly in order of influence:
1. **Training stimulus.** Without a hypertrophic stimulus, a surplus is simply fat gain.
2. **Starting body fat.** Leaner individuals partition more favourably; higher body fat percentage tends to worsen partitioning (the "Forbes curve"). This is the strongest physiological argument for not starting a bulk from a high body fat percentage.
3. **Training age.** Novices partition far better than advanced trainees, because their capacity to add muscle is much greater.
4. **Magnitude of the surplus.** Beyond the rate at which muscle can actually be built, additional energy is stored as fat. Since muscle gain is capped at roughly 0.25–1.5% of body mass per month depending on training age (file `02`), a surplus far exceeding that ceiling produces fat and nothing else.
5. **Protein intake, sleep, and — as file `08` documents — androgen status**, which is a large part of why drug-assisted trainees can use large surpluses productively and drug-free trainees cannot.

**Practical guidance:**
- A surplus of roughly **5–15% above maintenance** — or about 0.25–0.5% of body mass gained per week — is the defensible range. Novices can use the upper end; advanced trainees should use the lower.
- Start a gaining phase from a **moderate body fat level** (roughly 10–15% for men, 18–25% for women) rather than from either extreme.
- Expect roughly a 1:1 to 1:2 muscle-to-fat ratio in an intermediate trainee under good conditions. Claims of pure muscle gain in a surplus are not credible outside of novices and returning trainees.
- **"Dirty bulking"** — a large uncontrolled surplus — produces faster scale weight gain and a worse composition, then requires a longer subsequent deficit. It is a net loss of time.

## 7. Measurement methods and their real error bars

Every method estimates body composition from something else. The numbers carry more uncertainty than their presentation implies.

| Method | What it measures | Typical error (individual) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **DXA** | Three-compartment: bone mineral, lean soft tissue, fat mass, by differential X-ray attenuation | ~±3–5 percentage points versus criterion methods; **precision** (same scanner, same day) is better, roughly ±1–2 pp | Best widely available method. Affected by hydration, glycogen, gut contents, recent food and exercise. Different manufacturers and software versions are **not** interchangeable |
| **Hydrostatic weighing** | Body density via water displacement, converted by a two-compartment equation | ~±2–3 pp | Historical criterion. Requires full expiration and residual volume measurement; residual volume estimation is a major error source |
| **BodPod (air displacement plethysmography)** | Body density via air displacement | ~±3–4 pp, with occasional larger outliers | More comfortable than hydrostatic weighing; sensitive to body hair, clothing, temperature and breathing |
| **BIA (bioelectrical impedance)** | Body water via electrical impedance, then estimates fat from assumed hydration | ~±4–8 pp, worse in consumer devices | **Highly sensitive to hydration, meal timing, exercise, skin temperature, menstrual cycle.** Consumer scales are essentially unusable for tracking real change |
| **Skinfolds** | Subcutaneous fat thickness at sites, converted by regression equations | ~±3–5 pp with a skilled tester; far worse with an unskilled one | Cheap and, in expert hands, **excellent for tracking change over time** even if the absolute number is wrong. Equation choice matters enormously |
| **Ultrasound** | Subcutaneous fat thickness directly | ~±2–4 pp with standardised protocols | Growing use; less operator-variable than skinfolds for thickness, but conversion to whole-body percentage still relies on equations |
| **Circumference formulas** (US Navy, YMCA) | Girths at defined sites | ~±5–8 pp or worse | Free, no equipment, poor accuracy. Reasonable for tracking direction, useless for an absolute number |
| **3D scanning** | Surface geometry, converted by model | Variable; improving | Promising for tracking shape change; validation still developing |
| **Visual estimation / photographs** | — | Very poor for absolute values | But surprisingly useful for tracking change under standardised lighting and pose. Free |

**The rules that follow from this table:**

1. **Compare like with like.** Never compare a DXA number to a skinfold number to a BIA number. Compare the same method, same device, same operator, same conditions.
2. **Track trends, not points.** A single measurement's absolute accuracy is poor; the same method's ability to detect *change* is much better.
3. **Standardise conditions ruthlessly** — same time of day, fasted, same hydration state, before training, and (for women) same phase of the menstrual cycle.
4. **No field method resolves a 1% change.** If your DXA says you went from 14.2% to 13.8%, that is within measurement error.
5. **The mirror, the tape measure, the scale trend and training performance together give more usable information than any single body-composition scan.**

## 8. Body fat percentage ranges

Reference ranges, with the caveat that they vary by source and by measurement method:

| Category | Men | Women | Description |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Essential fat** | 2–5% | 10–13% | Structural minimum. Below this is incompatible with normal function |
| **Athletic / very lean** | 6–13% | 14–20% | Visible abdominal definition in men; athletic appearance in women. Sustainable at the upper end; the lower end is a contest condition, not a lifestyle |
| **Fitness / healthy** | 14–17% | 21–24% | Lean and healthy; some definition |
| **Average** | 18–24% | 25–31% | Population typical in many countries |
| **Obese** | ≥25% | ≥32% | Associated with elevated cardiometabolic risk |

**What these look like** (men, approximately): visible abdominal definition begins around 12–15%; clearly visible abdominal separation at 10–12%; visible vascularity and separation across most muscle groups at 8–10%; competition condition at 4–7% and not sustainable. Women: abdominal definition around 18–20%; athletic definition around 16–19%; competition condition around 10–13% and typically accompanied by amenorrhoea.

Two important corrections to popular belief:
- **Body fat percentage at a given appearance varies enormously with muscle mass.** Two men at 15% can look completely different if one carries 60 kg of lean mass and the other 75 kg.
- **Online estimation from photographs is unreliable** in both directions, and consistently underestimates in fitness media, where a person at 12% is routinely described as "6%".

## 9. Health risks at both extremes

### High body fat

Elevated adiposity — particularly visceral and ectopic fat — is associated with insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes, dyslipidaemia, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, several cancers, obstructive sleep apnoea, osteoarthritis, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and reduced fertility.

Two honest qualifications: **BMI is a poor individual measure** (it misclassifies muscular people as overweight or obese), and **cardiorespiratory fitness independently modifies risk** substantially — a fit person with higher body fat has considerably lower mortality risk than an unfit person of the same body fat (file `10`). Fat distribution matters more than total: waist circumference and waist-to-height ratio outperform BMI for cardiometabolic risk prediction.

### Low body fat and low energy availability — REDs

The **2023 IOC consensus statement on Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (Mountjoy et al.)** is the current authoritative framework. Its core points:

- **REDs is a syndrome of impaired health and performance caused by exposure to low energy availability (LEA)** — inadequate energy intake relative to exercise energy expenditure — affecting **both female and male athletes**.
- Since the 2018 consensus, more than 170 original research publications have advanced the field, including growing evidence for the role of **low carbohydrate availability specifically**, the **interplay between mental health and REDs**, and the impact of LEA in **males**.
- The statement introduces updated **Health and Performance Conceptual Models** and a novel **Physiological Model** distinguishing *problematic* from *adaptable* LEA exposure, moderated by individual factors.
- It introduces the **REDs Clinical Assessment Tool version 2 (REDs CAT2)**, which supports detection and clinical diagnosis based on **accumulated severity and risk stratification**, with associated training and competition recommendations.
- It provides **guidelines for safe and effective body composition assessment** as a prevention measure — recognising that body composition assessment itself can be a trigger for disordered behaviour.

**Documented consequences of sustained low energy availability** include: impaired bone health and reduced bone mineral density with elevated stress fracture risk; menstrual dysfunction and amenorrhoea in women and suppressed testosterone in men; impaired immune function; gastrointestinal dysfunction; cardiovascular changes including bradycardia; impaired growth and development in adolescents; impaired iron status; psychological effects including depression, anxiety, irritability and impaired concentration; and reduced training response, endurance performance, coordination, and increased injury risk.

> ⚠️ REDs is a clinical condition requiring medical assessment. Amenorrhoea in an athlete is **never normal** and is not a sign of successful training. It warrants medical evaluation.

**The "Female Athlete Triad"** (low energy availability, menstrual dysfunction, low bone mineral density) is the older, narrower framework; REDs supersedes and broadens it while retaining the triad's core.

### Disordered eating and eating disorders

Prevalence of disordered eating is elevated in athletes generally and markedly in **leanness-dependent sports** — bodybuilding, endurance running, gymnastics, dance, combat sports with weight classes, and aesthetic sports.

Warning signs worth naming: rigid food rules and escalating restriction; distress or guilt when routine is disrupted; compulsive exercise, including exercising through injury or illness; preoccupation with body checking (mirror, weighing, photos, measurements); social withdrawal around food; loss of menstrual function; binge-restrict cycling; and the use of the language of "discipline" to describe behaviour that has become compulsive.

**Muscle dysmorphia** — a body image disorder characterised by preoccupation with being insufficiently muscular — is a genuine clinical condition, occurs predominantly in men, and is strongly associated with anabolic steroid use (file `08`).

These are medical conditions with meaningful mortality. They require professional treatment, not a better training programme.

## 10. Contest preparation — what it actually costs

Physique competition preparation is an extreme, time-limited application of everything above. The published case-study literature documents the cost honestly.

**Kistler and colleagues (2014)** tracked a natural male bodybuilder through 26 weeks of preparation: body mass fell from **88.6 to 73.3 kg** and body fat from **17.5% to 7.4%**. Cardiovascular measures largely *improved* (blood pressure 128/61 → 113/54 mmHg; pulse wave velocity 7.9 → 5.8 m/s), and relative VO2 rose. But indexed cardiac output fell (2.5 → 1.8 L/m²) driven by a fall in **resting heart rate from 71 to 44 bpm**, and heart rate variability shifted markedly — physiological signs consistent with substantial energy deficiency, not with improved fitness.

**Robinson and colleagues (2015)** documented a structured 14-week preparation that explicitly avoided dehydration, prolonged fasting, severe restriction, excessive cardiovascular exercise, diuretics and steroids: body mass fell 11.7 kg, of which **6.7 kg was fat and 5.0 kg was fat-free mass** — a 43% lean-tissue share even with a scientifically designed approach. Mood state was not negatively affected in this case, which the authors present as evidence that conventional harsh practices are not necessary.

Documented costs of contest preparation in the broader literature include: substantial reductions in testosterone and leptin, menstrual dysfunction in women, reduced bone mineral density, reduced resting metabolic rate beyond that predicted, mood disturbance and irritability, sleep disruption, obsessive food thoughts, reduced libido, and post-contest rebound weight gain that frequently overshoots the starting point.

If someone chooses to compete, the risk-reduction measures that have support are: a long preparation (20+ weeks) with a modest deficit; maintaining resistance training intensity; high protein; regular diet breaks and refeeds; a structured **reverse diet** and planned post-contest recovery phase; and monitoring by a physician, including for women, menstrual status and bone health.

## Open questions

- **Measurement error figures** in section 7 are synthesised from method-validation literature generally rather than from a single retrieved systematic comparison. Marked `needs-verification` as specific numbers; the relative ordering of methods is well established.
- **Body fat percentage category boundaries** vary between sources (ACE, ACSM, Jackson-Pollock norms) and are convention rather than consensus. `needs-verification`.
- The **32 MJ (~7 700 kcal) per kg of body fat** figure is a classical approximation that ignores the composition of tissue lost and adaptive changes; dynamic models (e.g. the NIH Body Weight Planner) are more accurate.
- The **refeed** literature in trained populations is a single small RCT plus a published reanalysis dispute. Effect size and mechanism remain uncertain.
- **REDs prevalence data** by sport and by sex were not extracted in this pass; see the 2023 IOC statement directly.
