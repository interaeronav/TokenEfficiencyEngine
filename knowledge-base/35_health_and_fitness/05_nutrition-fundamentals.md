# Nutrition fundamentals — energy, macronutrients, micronutrients and dietary patterns

**Summary.** Energy balance governs body mass, but it is a dynamic system with feedback loops, not a static arithmetic identity, and both sides of the equation move when you change one of them. Protein is the macronutrient with the clearest quantitative recommendation for trained people (~1.6 g/kg/day as a meta-analytic break-point; ISSN range 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day). Carbohydrate availability governs high-intensity performance and glycogen resynthesis; dietary fat has essential requirements and a floor below which endocrine function suffers. Meal timing matters far less than total intake, and the "anabolic window" is closer to a day than to thirty minutes. No single dietary pattern is metabolically superior for body composition once energy and protein are matched — adherence is the variable that determines outcome.

## 1. Energy balance and its real-world messiness

The first law of thermodynamics holds: body energy stores change as intake minus expenditure. But treating this as a simple arithmetic prescription fails for four reasons.

**1. Expenditure is not independent of intake.** Reduce intake and expenditure falls — through reduced body mass (less tissue to maintain and move), reduced thermic effect of food (less food to process), and adaptive thermogenesis (below). Increase intake and expenditure rises. The system resists displacement in both directions, asymmetrically: it defends against loss more vigorously than against gain.

**2. Measurement error on both sides is large.** Self-reported food intake is systematically under-reported, commonly by 20–30% and sometimes far more, and the error is worse in people with higher body fat. Consumer wearables overestimate energy expenditure substantially, with errors frequently exceeding 25% for total daily expenditure. Two large errors that do not cancel.

**3. Absorption is not 100%.** Whole almonds deliver measurably less metabolisable energy than the Atwater factors predict, because of incomplete cell wall breakdown. Resistant starch and fibre are partially fermented rather than absorbed. Food processing changes digestibility. These effects are individually small but real.

**4. Compensation is behavioural.** Adding exercise often reduces spontaneous non-exercise activity, and often increases appetite. This is why exercise alone is a comparatively weak weight-loss intervention and a comparatively strong weight-*maintenance* intervention.

None of this refutes energy balance; it means that intake and expenditure must be treated as **estimates to be calibrated against measured outcome** (body mass trend over weeks), not as known quantities.

## 2. TDEE components

**Total daily energy expenditure (TDEE)** decomposes into four parts:

| Component | Typical share of TDEE | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **BMR / RMR** (basal/resting metabolic rate) | ~60–70% | Energy to maintain life at rest. Predicted by fat-free mass more than anything else. Organs (liver, brain, heart, kidney) account for a disproportionate share of resting metabolism relative to their mass |
| **TEF** (thermic effect of food) | ~8–15% | Energy cost of digestion, absorption and storage. Protein ~20–30% of its own energy, carbohydrate ~5–10%, fat ~0–3%, alcohol ~10–30% |
| **EAT** (exercise activity thermogenesis) | ~0–30% | Deliberate exercise. Usually far smaller than people assume: an hour of hard training might be 1 700–2 500 kJ (400–600 kcal) |
| **NEAT** (non-exercise activity thermogenesis) | ~5–30%, highly variable | Fidgeting, posture, walking, occupational activity. **The most variable component between and within individuals** — differences of several thousand kJ/day between people of similar size |

**BMR prediction equations** (Mifflin-St Jeor, Harris-Benedict, Katch-McArdle) carry roughly ±10% error for an individual, and the multiplier applied for activity level adds substantially more. Use them as a starting estimate only.

### Adaptive thermogenesis

When energy intake is restricted, measured resting metabolic rate falls **more than predicted from the change in body composition**. This residual is adaptive thermogenesis (also called metabolic adaptation).

The clearest data come from **Fothergill and colleagues (2016)**, who followed 14 of the 16 original "Biggest Loser" contestants for six years. At the end of the 30-week competition they had lost 58.3 ± 24.9 kg and RMR had fallen 610 ± 483 kcal/day. Six years later, 41.0 ± 31.3 kg had been regained, yet RMR remained **704 ± 427 kcal/day below baseline**, with metabolic adaptation (the residual after adjusting for body composition and age) of **−499 ± 207 kcal/day**. Those who maintained more weight loss showed *greater* concurrent metabolic slowing.

Three honest caveats: this was an extreme intervention (very large deficits plus enormous exercise volumes), n = 14, and the finding has not been consistently replicated in less extreme weight-loss cohorts, where adaptation is typically far smaller (on the order of 50–150 kcal/day). Adaptive thermogenesis is real; it is not a metabolic prison, and it does not make weight loss impossible. It does mean that maintaining a reduced body mass requires ongoing behavioural effort, indefinitely.

Contributors to adaptation include reduced leptin and thyroid hormone signalling, increased mitochondrial efficiency in skeletal muscle, reduced sympathetic tone, and — quantitatively the largest and most under-appreciated — **reduced NEAT**.

## 3. Protein

### Quality and the leucine threshold

Protein quality is a function of **essential amino acid (EAA) content** and **digestibility**. The current reference measure is DIAAS (Digestible Indispensable Amino Acid Score), which supersedes PDCAAS. Animal proteins (whey, casein, egg, milk, meat) score highest; plant proteins are generally lower, limited by specific amino acids — lysine in cereals, methionine in legumes.

**Leucine** is the key signalling amino acid. It activates mTORC1 via Sestrin2 and the GATOR complexes, triggering translation initiation. The **leucine threshold** hypothesis holds that a per-meal dose must exceed roughly 2–3 g of leucine to maximally stimulate muscle protein synthesis. The ISSN position stand states acute protein doses should contain **700–3000 mg of leucine** alongside a balanced complement of EAAs.

Two important qualifications:
- **Leucine alone is not sufficient.** Building protein requires all EAAs. Leucine is the trigger; the other EAAs are the bricks. This is precisely why BCAA supplements fail (file `07`).
- **The threshold is dose-, age- and context-dependent.** Older adults show **anabolic resistance** — they need a larger per-meal protein dose (often cited as ~40 g rather than ~20 g) to achieve the same MPS response.

Practically: ~20–40 g of a high-quality protein per meal, or ~0.25–0.40 g/kg body mass per meal, achieves the threshold for most people. Whey is the most leucine-dense common source (~10–11% leucine). Plant-based eaters can hit the threshold by using higher total doses, combining complementary sources, or choosing higher-leucine plant proteins (soy, pea).

### How much protein for muscle

**Morton and colleagues (2018)** meta-analysed 49 studies with 1 863 participants. Protein supplementation significantly increased 1RM strength (+2.49 kg, 95% CI 0.64–4.33), fat-free mass (+0.30 kg, 95% CI 0.09–0.52), muscle fibre cross-sectional area (+310 µm², 95% CI 51–570) and mid-femur CSA (+7.2 mm², 95% CI 0.20–14.30). A two-phase break-point analysis found **no further FFM gain beyond a total protein intake of 1.62 g/kg/day**.

Two moderators are worth noting: the benefit of supplementation **decreased with age** (−0.01 kg per year, p = 0.002) and was **greater in resistance-trained individuals** (+0.75 kg, 95% CI 0.09–1.40, p = 0.03).

The 1.6 g/kg figure has a genuine confidence interval — the paper reports the break-point's upper CI extending toward ~2.2 g/kg — so a practical target of **1.6–2.2 g/kg/day** is defensible and conservative. Going far beyond that is not harmful in healthy people but is not productive for muscle either.

**Where higher may help:**
- **In an energy deficit**, particularly for lean, trained individuals. The ISSN diets-and-body-composition stand cites **2.3–3.1 g/kg fat-free mass/day** to maximise lean mass retention under hypocaloric conditions, and notes emerging evidence that intakes above 3 g/kg may amplify the thermic, satiating and lean-mass-preserving effects.
- **In older adults**, to overcome anabolic resistance.
- **During very high training volumes** or in energy-restricted athletes.

**Safety.** In people with normal renal function there is no evidence that high protein intakes cause kidney damage; long-term studies at 2.5–3.3 g/kg have found no adverse renal or hepatic markers. In people with existing chronic kidney disease, protein intake is a genuine clinical concern requiring medical supervision.

### Distribution across the day

The ISSN position: doses of **20–40 g (0.25–0.40 g/kg) every 3–4 h** appear to most favourably affect MPS, and are associated with improved body composition and performance outcomes. Practically that means roughly **3–5 protein feedings per day**.

The magnitude of the distribution effect is modest compared with hitting the daily total. If someone is at 1.8 g/kg/day but consuming it in two meals, moving to four meals is a refinement, not a rescue. Do not let distribution optimisation crowd out the far more important question of the total.

**Pre-sleep protein.** ~30–40 g of casein before sleep acutely increases overnight muscle protein synthesis and metabolic rate without inhibiting lipolysis (Jäger 2017). The effect on long-term hypertrophy is plausible but less firmly established than the acute data.

## 4. Carbohydrate

Carbohydrate is not essential in the strict biochemical sense — the body can produce glucose from gluconeogenic precursors — but it is the dominant fuel for high-intensity work and it is a strong performance variable.

**Roles:**
- **Fuel for high-intensity exercise.** Above roughly 65–70% VO2max, carbohydrate supplies the majority of energy. Glycogen depletion in recruited fibres is a direct cause of fatigue in prolonged exercise.
- **Glycogen storage.** Roughly 300–600 g total in a trained adult (~80% muscle, ~20% liver), stored with roughly 3 g of water per gram. This water is why carbohydrate manipulation produces rapid scale-weight swings that are not fat.
- **Training quality in resistance work.** Resistance training uses less glycogen than endurance work, but a hard high-volume session can deplete a meaningful fraction. Very low-carbohydrate conditions typically reduce training volume tolerance.
- **Protein sparing** — adequate carbohydrate reduces the need for gluconeogenesis from amino acids.
- **Thyroid and leptin signalling** — carbohydrate availability influences T3 conversion and leptin, part of why very-low-carbohydrate dieting can amplify the adaptive response.

**Intake guidance (Kerksick 2017, ISSN nutrient timing):**
- Maximising glycogen: **8–12 g/kg/day**, relevant to high-volume endurance athletes, not to general trainees.
- Rapid refuelling with <4 h between sessions: **1.2 g/kg/h** of high-GI carbohydrate; optionally with caffeine (3–8 mg/kg), or **0.8 g/kg/h carbohydrate + 0.2–0.4 g/kg/h protein**.
- During exercise >60–70 min at >70% VO2max: **~30–60 g/h** in a 6–8% carbohydrate-electrolyte solution, taken every 10–15 min. Rates above ~60 g/h require multiple transportable carbohydrates (glucose + fructose) to exceed the SGLT1 transport ceiling; well-adapted endurance athletes can tolerate up to ~90–120 g/h with combined sources, though gut training is required.

For a general resistance trainee, carbohydrate intake is best set as **whatever remains after protein and fat targets are met**, typically 3–6 g/kg/day. There is no metabolic requirement to keep it low.

**Glycaemic index** is of limited practical use in an athletic context. Mixed meals blunt the glycaemic response, and total carbohydrate quantity matters far more than its GI for body composition.

## 5. Fat

**Essential requirements.** Two fatty acids cannot be synthesised: **linoleic acid** (omega-6) and **alpha-linolenic acid** (omega-3). ALA is poorly converted to the physiologically important long-chain omega-3s **EPA** and **DHA** — conversion efficiency is commonly cited as under 10% for EPA and under 1% for DHA — so direct sources (oily fish; algal oil for vegetarians and vegans) matter.

**Functions:** cell membrane structure, steroid hormone substrate, fat-soluble vitamin (A, D, E, K) absorption, satiety, and energy density.

**How much.** A practical floor is around **0.5–1.0 g/kg/day**, or roughly 20–25% of energy intake. Below about 15–20% of energy, several problems appear: fat-soluble vitamin absorption suffers, satiety drops, food choice becomes restrictive, and there is evidence of reduced circulating testosterone in men on very low-fat diets — though the magnitude of the testosterone effect is modest and its functional significance is contested.

**Types.**
- **Saturated fat** — the cardiovascular evidence is more contested than the older consensus implied, but the mainstream position (replacing saturated with polyunsaturated fat reduces cardiovascular events) remains the safer default.
- **Monounsaturated** — olive oil, avocado, nuts. Consistently associated with favourable outcomes, most strongly in the Mediterranean-diet literature.
- **Polyunsaturated** — omega-6 (linoleic acid, abundant in seed oils) and omega-3 (EPA/DHA/ALA). The "omega-6 is inflammatory" claim is weaker than commonly stated; the intervention evidence does not show harm from linoleic acid.
- **Trans fats** — industrially produced trans fats are unambiguously harmful and are now banned or restricted in many jurisdictions. Avoid entirely.

## 6. Micronutrients and common deficiencies

Trained populations are not immune to deficiency; in some ways they are more exposed, through higher requirements, energy restriction, and restrictive food choices.

| Nutrient | Who is at risk | Consequence of deficiency |
|---|---|---|
| **Iron** | Menstruating women, endurance athletes (foot-strike haemolysis, GI losses, hepcidin elevation), vegetarians | Impaired oxygen carriage, fatigue, reduced VO2max. Serum ferritin is the usual screen |
| **Vitamin D** | High-latitude populations, indoor athletes, darker skin at high latitude, covered clothing | Bone health, muscle function, immune function. Supplementation improves performance only where deficiency exists |
| **Calcium** | Low energy availability, dairy avoidance | Bone mineral density; interacts with REDs (file `06`) |
| **Vitamin B12** | Vegans, some vegetarians | Anaemia, irreversible neurological damage if prolonged. **Supplementation is non-optional for vegans** |
| **Iodine** | Non-iodised salt users, vegans | Thyroid function |
| **Zinc** | Low-energy diets, heavy sweat losses, vegetarians | Immune function, wound healing |
| **Magnesium** | Common marginal intake in Western diets | Weakly evidenced links to cramping and sleep; supplementation benefits are modest and mostly confined to deficiency |
| **Folate** | Women of childbearing age | Neural tube defects — supplementation is a public health recommendation pre-conception |

Three principles:
1. **Correcting a deficiency improves performance; supplementing beyond sufficiency generally does not.** This is the single most important pattern in the micronutrient literature.
2. **Test, don't guess** — for iron and vitamin D in particular, blood testing is cheap and the answer changes the recommendation.
3. **Antioxidant megadoses may impair adaptation.** High-dose vitamin C and E supplementation has been shown to blunt some training adaptations, apparently because exercise-induced reactive oxygen species are part of the adaptive signal. Food-level antioxidant intake is fine; gram-level supplementation is not advisable around training.

**[NA]** In arid southern Africa, sweat sodium and total fluid losses are elevated year-round in outdoor work and training; iodine and vitamin A status vary regionally with food fortification programmes. Vitamin D deficiency is less common than at high latitudes but is not absent, particularly in people who avoid sun exposure.

## 7. Hydration

The ACSM position stand (Sawka et al., 2007) is still the reference:

- **Goal before exercise:** start euhydrated with normal plasma electrolytes. Pre-hydrate several hours before if needed, to allow absorption and normalisation of urine output.
- **Goal during exercise:** prevent **>2% body mass loss** from water deficit and avoid excessive electrolyte disturbance. Above roughly 2%, endurance performance measurably declines; the effect is amplified in heat.
- **Individual variability is large** — sweat rates and sweat sodium concentration vary enormously. The stand explicitly recommends **customised fluid replacement**, estimated by weighing before and after training.
- **Electrolyte and carbohydrate beverages** offer benefits over plain water under specific conditions (long duration, heat, high sweat rates).
- **After exercise:** replace the fluid and electrolyte deficit; urgency depends on time to the next session.

> ⚠️ **Overdrinking is a real hazard.** Exercise-associated hyponatraemia results from consuming fluid in excess of sweat losses, diluting plasma sodium. It is potentially fatal and has killed marathon runners. "Drink as much as possible" is bad advice. Drink to thirst plus a plan informed by measured sweat rate.

**Everyday hydration:** the "8 glasses a day" rule has no scientific basis. Total water comes from beverages *and* food (typically 20–30% from food). Urine colour is a crude but usable field indicator. Caffeine at habitual intakes is not meaningfully dehydrating.

**[NA]** In Namibian and Gulf conditions — high ambient temperature, very low humidity, high solar load — sweat evaporates so efficiently that lifters and runners routinely underestimate their losses because they do not feel wet. Weighing before and after outdoor sessions is the only reliable method.

## 8. Fibre and the gut microbiome

**Fibre** is the non-digestible carbohydrate fraction. Practically it divides into **soluble/viscous** (oats, legumes, psyllium — slows gastric emptying, lowers LDL, improves glycaemic control) and **insoluble** (wheat bran, vegetable structural fibre — increases faecal bulk and transit).

Typical recommendations sit around **25–38 g/day** for adults, and most Western populations fall well short. Higher fibre intake is among the more consistently supported dietary associations in the epidemiological literature, linked to lower all-cause and cardiovascular mortality and lower colorectal cancer risk. It also improves satiety, which makes an energy deficit easier to sustain.

**The microbiome.** The gut hosts a large and metabolically active microbial community. Fermentation of fibre and resistant starch produces **short-chain fatty acids** (butyrate, propionate, acetate) that feed colonocytes and have systemic metabolic and immune effects.

Honest assessment of the state of the field: the microbiome is genuinely important and genuinely poorly understood. Associations with obesity, metabolic disease and mood are abundant; causal, actionable interventions in healthy humans are scarce. Commercial microbiome testing services currently have **no demonstrated ability to generate useful personalised dietary recommendations**. What is supported: eat a diverse range of plant foods and adequate fibre; fermented foods appear beneficial; broad-spectrum antibiotics disrupt the community. Specific probiotic strains have narrow, condition-specific evidence (antibiotic-associated diarrhoea, some IBS symptoms), not general benefit.

**Athlete caveat:** high fibre intake immediately before or during endurance exercise increases GI distress. Reducing fibre in the 24 h before competition is standard practice.

## 9. Meal timing and the anabolic window

The "anabolic window" — the claim that protein must be consumed within 30–60 minutes post-exercise or gains are lost — has not survived.

The ISSN protein stand states the anabolic effect of exercise is **long-lasting (at least 24 h)** though it diminishes with time post-exercise, and that optimal timing "is likely a matter of individual tolerance", with benefits derivable from pre- or post-workout ingestion. The nutrient-timing stand emphasises that **meeting the total daily protein intake, with evenly spaced feedings, should be viewed as the primary area of emphasis**.

The most important modifier: **the size and timing of the pre-exercise meal determine how urgent post-exercise feeding is.** If someone ate 40 g of protein two hours before training, amino acids are still appearing in circulation during and after the session, and rushing a shake is pointless. If someone trained fasted at 06:00, eating soon afterwards is sensible.

Where timing genuinely matters:
- **Glycogen resynthesis** when two sessions are separated by less than ~4–8 h. Here the timing evidence is strong (1.2 g/kg/h; Kerksick 2017).
- **During prolonged endurance exercise** — carbohydrate intake during the event is genuinely performance-relevant.
- **Caffeine and other acute ergogenic aids** — timing is intrinsic to the mechanism.
- **Pre-sleep protein** — a distinct and reasonably well-supported case.

Where it does not: the precise clock time of meals for a resistance trainee meeting daily protein and energy targets.

**Meal frequency** for body composition: the ISSN diets stand notes that in non-exercising contexts, changing meal frequency has limited impact on weight loss and body composition, with stronger evidence that it affects appetite and satiety. The claim that frequent small meals "stoke the metabolic fire" is false — TEF scales with total food intake, not with the number of eating occasions.

## 10. Dietary patterns compared

The honest headline, from the ISSN diets-and-body-composition stand: **"a wide range of dietary approaches (low-fat to low-carbohydrate/ketogenic, and all points between) can be similarly effective for improving body composition."** The determinants of success are the energy deficit, adequate protein, and — dominating everything — adherence.

### Mediterranean

High in vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, nuts, olive oil and fish; moderate in dairy and wine; low in red and processed meat. This is the dietary pattern with the **strongest hard-outcome evidence** in nutrition, most notably from the PREDIMED randomised trial of primary cardiovascular prevention. (PREDIMED was republished in 2018 after methodological issues with randomisation at some sites were identified; the reanalysis retained the principal finding, but the episode is worth knowing about.) For general health it is the most defensible default pattern. It is not a body-composition intervention per se.

### Low-carbohydrate and ketogenic

**DIETFITS (Gardner 2018)** randomised 609 adults to healthy low-fat versus healthy low-carbohydrate for 12 months. Achieved macronutrient distributions were genuinely different (48% vs 30% carbohydrate; 29% vs 45% fat). Weight change: **−5.3 kg (low-fat) vs −6.0 kg (low-carb), between-group difference 0.7 kg (95% CI −0.2 to 1.6 kg)** — not significant. Neither genotype pattern nor baseline insulin secretion predicted which diet worked better for whom (interaction p = 0.20 and p = 0.47), which deflates a great deal of "personalised nutrition" marketing.

For athletes specifically: ketogenic diets impair high-intensity performance, and the adaptation period (2–6 weeks) involves reduced training quality. There is evidence that even after full keto-adaptation, exercise economy at high intensities is impaired. Low-carbohydrate approaches are legitimate for people who find them satiating and sustainable; they are a poor choice for someone whose primary goal is high-intensity performance.

### Plant-based

Well-planned vegetarian and vegan diets support muscle gain and athletic performance. The considerations are practical:
- **Protein quantity and quality** — lower DIAAS scores and lower leucine density mean higher total intake and deliberate source combination. Roughly 10–20% more total protein is a reasonable adjustment.
- **Vitamin B12 supplementation is mandatory for vegans.** Not optional, not negotiable.
- **Iron, zinc, iodine, calcium, and long-chain omega-3 (EPA/DHA)** all require attention; algal oil is the direct vegan omega-3 source.
- **Creatine and carnosine** stores are lower in vegetarians, which means **creatine supplementation produces larger effects** in this group — one of the few contexts where a supplement response is predictably amplified.

Health outcomes for plant-forward diets are generally favourable in epidemiology, though confounded by the health-conscious profile of people who adopt them.

### Intermittent fasting and time-restricted eating

**TREAT (Lowe 2020)** randomised 116 adults with overweight/obesity to 16:8 time-restricted eating versus three structured meals for 12 weeks. Weight change: TRE −0.94 kg (95% CI −1.68 to −0.20); control −0.68 kg (95% CI −1.41 to 0.05); **between-group difference −0.26 kg (95% CI −1.30 to 0.78, p = 0.63)** — no advantage. Notably, in the in-person subgroup there was a significant *disadvantage* in appendicular lean mass index for the TRE group (−0.16 kg/m², 95% CI −0.27 to −0.05, p = 0.005), suggesting a disproportionate share of the (small) loss was lean tissue.

The ISSN diets stand reaches the same conclusion: **"the collective body of intermittent caloric restriction research demonstrates no significant advantage over daily caloric restriction for improving body composition."**

Intermittent fasting is therefore best framed as **an adherence tool** — a structure that some people find makes an energy deficit easier to sustain — and not as a metabolically superior approach. For a trainee pursuing hypertrophy, compressing all protein into an 8-hour window works against the distribution recommendations in section 3, and the TREAT lean-mass finding is a reason for caution.

### Very-low-energy and "detox" approaches

Very-low-energy diets (below ~3 400 kJ / 800 kcal/day) produce rapid weight loss with disproportionate lean tissue loss, and belong under medical supervision only. Juice cleanses and "detoxes" have no physiological basis — the liver and kidneys perform detoxification continuously and are not enhanced by any commercial product. These are covered here only so the entry exists to say they are unsupported.

## Open questions

- The **leucine threshold** figure of ~2–3 g per meal is widely cited but the ISSN stand gives a much wider range (700–3000 mg). The precise threshold, and whether it is a threshold at all rather than a continuum, is not settled.
