# Resources and further reading — an annotated register

**Summary.** This is the reading and verification layer for the domain: the textbooks that carry the underlying science, the position stands that carry the consensus, the practitioners and outlets worth following, the journals where the primary literature appears, and a working method for spotting misinformation. Every entry is annotated with what it is good for and, where relevant, what its limitations or biases are. Free and paid status is marked. **URLs were checked in August 2026;** several publisher sites block automated requests and return 403 or 429 to a script while resolving normally in a browser — this is noted where observed.

## 1. Textbooks

| Title | Author(s) / edition | What it is good for | Caveats | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ***Science and Development of Muscle Hypertrophy*** | Brad Schoenfeld (3rd ed., Human Kinetics) | **The** reference on hypertrophy. Mechanisms, training variables, nutrition, and a chapter-by-chapter synthesis of the evidence with explicit statements of what is and is not established. Heavily referenced | Schoenfeld is an author on a large fraction of the studies it cites — not a fault, but be aware the field and the book share a centre of gravity | Paid |
| ***NSCA's Essentials of Strength Training and Conditioning*** | Haff & Triplett (eds.), 4th ed., Human Kinetics | The standard textbook of the field and the basis of the CSCS certification. Broad: physiology, biomechanics, testing, programme design, exercise technique with photographs, facility management | Committee-written and consequently conservative; slower to update than the primary literature. Some sections lag current evidence by several years | Paid |
| ***Exercise Physiology: Nutrition, Energy, and Human Performance*** | McArdle, Katch & Katch (9th ed., Wolters Kluwer) | The classic physiology text. Comprehensive on energy systems, bioenergetics, cardiorespiratory function, environmental physiology, and the historical development of the field | Very large; expensive; more depth than most practitioners need | Paid |
| ***Science and Practice of Strength Training*** | Zatsiorsky, Kraemer & Fry (3rd ed., Human Kinetics) | The conceptual framework of strength training from the Soviet sports science tradition — the "three methods" of maximal effort, repeated effort and dynamic effort; training loads; timing. Source of much of the vocabulary of modern periodisation | Terse and theoretical; light on how to implement anything. Some concepts predate and are not well supported by the modern trial literature | Paid |
| ***The Muscle and Strength Pyramid: Training*** and ***: Nutrition*** | Eric Helms, Andy Morgan, Andrea Valdez (2nd ed., self-published) | The best available bridge from the literature to practice. Explicitly hierarchical: adherence → volume/intensity/frequency → progression → exercise selection → rest → tempo, with the nutrition volume ordered analogously. Honest about uncertainty | Self-published, so no external editorial layer. Intentionally practical rather than exhaustive | Paid (inexpensive) |
| ***The Lean Muscle Diet*** and Alan Aragon's writing generally | Alan Aragon (with Lou Schuler) | Aragon's value is as a sceptic and a synthesiser of nutrition evidence. Practical, anti-dogmatic, strong on debunking | The book is aimed at a general audience; the depth is in his research review (section 3) | Paid |
| ***Starting Strength: Basic Barbell Training*** | Mark Rippetoe (3rd ed., Aasgaard) | Genuinely excellent as a technical teaching text for the five barbell lifts. The coaching-cue analysis, particularly of the squat and press, is unmatched in detail and clarity, and the novice linear progression works | ⚠️ **Read with the dogmatism filter on.** The book presents a single "correct" technical model (notably the low-bar squat and the hip-drive cue) as universally optimal, dismisses machines and isolation work, dismisses much of the exercise science literature, and makes physiological claims — particularly around hormonal responses to squatting and deadlifting — that the evidence in file `01` does not support. Use it for the technique chapters, not for its model of adaptation | Paid |
| ***Practical Programming for Strength Training*** | Rippetoe & Baker | The novice/intermediate/advanced framework and the timescale-of-progression concept are genuinely useful and widely adopted | Same caveats as above | Paid |
| ***Scientific Principles of Hypertrophy Training*** / ***Strength Training*** | Israetel, Hoffmann, Feather, Davis et al. (Renaissance Periodization) | The volume-landmark framework (MV / MEV / MAV / MRV) used in file `03`, presented systematically | The specific numbers are practitioner heuristics, not meta-analytic outputs. The RP ecosystem is also a commercial product business, which shapes presentation | Paid |
| ***Supertraining*** | Siff & Verkhoshansky | A vast compendium of the Eastern European strength training tradition. Historically important | Poorly organised, poorly edited, and much of it is untested theory presented with confidence. Read as history, not as a manual | Paid |
| ***Essentials of Sport Nutrition and Supplements*** | Antonio, Kalman, Stout et al. (ISSN, Springer) | The ISSN's own textbook; useful companion to the position stands | Reflects ISSN's generally supplement-friendly orientation | Paid |
| ***Why We Sleep*** | Matthew Walker | Popular and readable introduction to sleep science | ⚠️ Contains documented factual errors and overstatements, catalogued in detail by external critics. The core message (sleep matters enormously) is right; specific claims should be checked | Paid |

## 5. How to spot fitness misinformation

A working checklist. Any one item is a yellow flag; three or more is a reliable signal.

**Red flags in the claim itself**

1. **Certainty exceeding the evidence.** "Studies prove" and "the science is settled" are rare in a field where 20-person 8-week trials are the norm. Confident, unhedged claims about contested variables (optimal volume, periodisation model, meal timing) are a signal.
2. **A single mechanism doing all the work.** "It raises growth hormone", "it activates autophagy", "it spikes insulin". Mechanism is hypothesis; outcome is evidence (file `00`, the acute-marker fallacy).
3. **Effect sizes that beat pharmacology.** If a herb or a training method produces more muscle than 600 mg/week of testosterone did in Bhasin (1996) — 6.1 kg of fat-free mass in 10 weeks — the claim, not the physiology, is wrong.
4. **"Detox", "toxins", "reset your metabolism", "insulin resistance from carbs alone".** These phrases signal a vocabulary borrowed from marketing rather than physiology.
5. **A named villain.** Seed oils, gluten, lectins, "big food", "the fitness industry doesn't want you to know". Single-villain explanations of complex multifactorial outcomes are almost always wrong.
6. **Personalisation claims without validated tests.** Genetic tests for training response, microbiome tests for diet, "metabolic typing" — none currently has demonstrated predictive validity.
7. **Before-and-after photographs as evidence.** Lighting, posing, pump, tan, hydration, and time of day account for most visible difference in a same-day pair. Photographs cannot separate intervention from regression to the mean, from other simultaneous changes, or from undisclosed pharmacology.

**Red flags in the source**

8. **Undisclosed conflict of interest** — the person recommending the supplement sells it, or is sponsored by the manufacturer of the specific extract in the cited trial.
9. **Credential inflation or irrelevance.** A physician is not automatically an authority on hypertrophy; a competitive bodybuilder is not automatically an authority on physiology. Check whether the person's expertise matches the claim.
10. **No engagement with contrary evidence.** Credible sources cite the studies that disagree with them and say why they weight them as they do. Advocates cite only supporting studies.
11. **Physique as argument.** As file `08` documents at length, an impressive physique is compatible with knowing very little, and — given undisclosed PED prevalence — is frequently not attributable to the method being sold.
12. **Urgency and scarcity.** Countdown timers, "closing enrolment", "the 3 foods you must stop eating today".

**Green flags**

13. **Explicit uncertainty.** "The evidence here is weak", "this is my inference, not a finding", "this study has n = 12".
14. **Effect sizes and confidence intervals**, not just direction and p-values.
15. **Distinguishing untrained from trained populations**, and acute from chronic outcomes.
16. **Updating publicly.** Someone who says "I used to think X, this study changed my mind" is engaging with evidence.
17. **Hierarchical thinking.** Sources that put adherence, energy, protein and progressive overload above supplements and exercise minutiae are correctly ordered.
18. **Citations you can check** — a DOI or PubMed ID, not "studies show".

**A practical verification procedure**

1. Find the specific study, not the article about the study.
2. Read the abstract's results section, not the conclusion — conclusions routinely overstate results.
3. Check n, duration, and whether participants were trained.
4. Check the effect size against the confidence interval. If the interval crosses zero or spans trivial-to-large, the study did not establish much.
5. Check whether a meta-analysis exists. If so, read that instead.
6. Check funding and conflicts of interest, usually at the end of the paper.
7. Ask whether the marketed protocol matches the studied protocol (dose, timing, population).

## 6. Suggested reading order for this domain

For someone starting from scratch and wanting a solid working understanding:

1. **`00_overview.md`** here — the evidence standard and the failure modes.
2. **Helms, Valdez & Morgan, *The Muscle and Strength Pyramid*** — both volumes. This gives the practical hierarchy fastest.
3. **`02_hypertrophy-and-strength-science.md`** and **`03_programme-design.md`** here, alongside it.
4. **The four core ISSN position stands** (protein, creatine, nutrient timing, diets and body composition) — free, and they settle most nutrition questions.
5. **Stronger By Science** articles on volume, frequency and statistics — free, and they teach you how to read the literature.
6. **Schoenfeld, *Science and Development of Muscle Hypertrophy*** — when you want the mechanisms and the full evidence base.
7. **McArdle/Katch or the NSCA *Essentials*** — as reference works, consulted rather than read through.
8. **MASS Research Review** — as an ongoing subscription once the foundations are in place.
