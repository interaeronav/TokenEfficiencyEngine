# Recovery, sleep and health — what works between sessions

**Summary.** Sleep is the highest-value recovery intervention by a wide margin, and the evidence is quantitative: sleep restriction during dieting cut the proportion of weight lost as fat by 55% and increased fat-free mass loss by 60%; sleep extension improved sprint times and shooting accuracy in collegiate basketball players; adolescent athletes sleeping under 8 hours were 1.7 times more likely to be injured. Most commercial recovery modalities produce small effects on *perceived* recovery and soreness with little effect on performance, and one — post-exercise cold water immersion — actively blunts hypertrophy and strength adaptation. Injury management has moved from RICE toward the PEACE & LOVE framework, which de-emphasises rest and anti-inflammatories in favour of optimal loading. The cardiometabolic and mortality dose-response for physical activity is steep at the low end and flattens well before extreme volumes.

## 1. Sleep architecture and why it matters

Sleep cycles through stages roughly every 90 minutes, 4–6 times per night:

- **N1** — light transitional sleep, minutes.
- **N2** — the largest share of total sleep time, characterised by sleep spindles and K-complexes; involved in motor memory consolidation.
- **N3 — slow-wave sleep (SWS)**, deep sleep. Concentrated in the **first half of the night**. This is when the largest pulsatile growth hormone release occurs, when anabolic processes and tissue repair are most active, and when glymphatic clearance of metabolic waste from the brain is highest.
- **REM** — rapid eye movement sleep. Concentrated in the **second half of the night**. Involved in procedural memory, motor learning consolidation and emotional regulation. Muscle atonia is present.

The distribution matters practically: **cutting sleep short at the end of the night preferentially removes REM**, while going to bed very late removes slow-wave sleep. Neither is a fair trade.

Physiologically relevant features: core temperature falls during sleep and rises before waking; growth hormone is released in pulses tied to SWS; cortisol follows a circadian rhythm peaking shortly after waking (the cortisol awakening response); and testosterone in men rises during sleep, which is why sleep restriction lowers it.

**Sleep architecture in athletes** is affected by late-evening training (raised core temperature and sympathetic activation delay sleep onset), competition anxiety, travel and time-zone shift, evening caffeine, and — in many sports — early morning training schedules.

## 2. Sleep and performance, recovery and body composition

### Sleep restriction

**Nedeltcheva and colleagues (2010)** is the cleanest body-composition experiment. Ten overweight adults underwent two 14-day periods of identical moderate caloric restriction, once with 8.5 hours of sleep opportunity and once with 5.5 hours, in a randomised crossover:

- Sleep curtailment **decreased the proportion of weight lost as fat by 55%** (1.4 vs 0.6 kg, p = 0.043).
- It **increased loss of fat-free body mass by 60%** (1.5 vs 2.4 kg, p = 0.002).
- It was accompanied by markers of enhanced neuroendocrine adaptation to caloric restriction, increased hunger, and a shift in substrate use toward oxidising less fat.

Same diet, same deficit, opposite outcome. The conclusion — that sleep contributes to maintenance of fat-free mass during energy restriction, and that insufficient sleep may compromise the efficacy of typical dietary interventions — is directly actionable.

Other well-documented consequences of sleep restriction: reduced time to exhaustion; impaired reaction time and decision-making (the deficits are comparable to alcohol intoxication at severe restriction); increased perceived exertion at the same workload; impaired glucose tolerance (a week of 4-hour nights produces measurable insulin resistance in healthy young men); increased ghrelin and reduced leptin, driving hunger; reduced testosterone in men; impaired immune function; and impaired motor skill consolidation.

### Sleep extension

**Mah and colleagues (2011)** studied 11 Stanford men's varsity basketball players. After a 2–4 week baseline at habitual sleep, they extended to a minimum 10 hours in bed for 5–7 weeks:

- Objective nightly sleep increased by **110.9 ± 79.7 min** (p < 0.001).
- Timed sprint improved from **16.2 ± 0.61 s to 15.5 ± 0.54 s** (p < 0.001).
- **Free throw percentage increased 9%**; **3-point field goal percentage increased 9.2%** (p < 0.001).
- Psychomotor vigilance reaction time and Epworth Sleepiness Scale scores decreased (p < 0.01).
- Profile of Mood States improved: increased vigour, decreased fatigue (p < 0.001).

Small n and no control group, so treat the magnitudes cautiously — but a 9% improvement in shooting accuracy is larger than almost any supplement, programme variable or recovery modality in this knowledge base.

### Sleep and injury

**Milewski and colleagues (2014)** surveyed 112 adolescent athletes (mean age 15) and correlated responses with school injury records. In multivariate analysis, **hours of sleep per night and school grade were the best independent predictors of injury**. Athletes averaging **fewer than 8 hours per night were 1.7 times more likely to have been injured** (95% CI 1.0–3.0, p = 0.04). Each additional grade in school raised injury odds 1.4-fold (95% CI 1.2–1.6, p < 0.001).

This is an observational, retrospective study with a self-reported exposure, so causation is not established. But it is consistent with the experimental data on reaction time, decision-making and tissue repair.

## 3. Sleep hygiene — the evidence

Interventions with reasonable support:

| Intervention | Evidence quality | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Consistent sleep and wake times, including weekends** | Good | Probably the single highest-yield behaviour. Circadian stability improves sleep efficiency |
| **Adequate duration (7–9 h for adults; more for adolescents and heavy trainers)** | Good | Athletes in heavy training plausibly need more; the specific requirement is individual |
| **Cool, dark, quiet room** | Moderate | Core temperature must fall for sleep onset; ~18–20°C is commonly recommended |
| **Avoiding caffeine within ~6–8 h of bed** | Good | Half-life 5–6 h. Caffeine measurably degrades sleep architecture even when subjective sleep quality seems unaffected |
| **Avoiding alcohol before bed** | Good | Alcohol shortens sleep onset but **suppresses REM** and fragments the second half of the night. It is a sleep-quality destroyer, not a sleep aid |
| **Light exposure — bright light in the morning, dim in the evening** | Good | The primary circadian entrainment signal |
| **Blue-light-blocking glasses / screen restriction** | Weak-moderate | Effect is smaller than commonly claimed; content-driven arousal probably matters more than the wavelength |
| **CBT-I (cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia)** | Strong | **The first-line treatment for chronic insomnia**, superior to medication for long-term outcomes. Available in app-delivered form |
| **Napping** | Moderate | 20–30 min naps improve alertness without grogginess; longer naps risk sleep inertia and can affect night-time sleep. Useful for athletes with early sessions |
| **Sleep-tracking wearables** | Weak | Poor stage-classification accuracy against polysomnography. Useful for tracking duration and consistency; the "sleep score" is largely noise, and there is a documented phenomenon ("orthosomnia") of anxiety about tracker data worsening sleep |
| **Melatonin** | Moderate for circadian shifting | Genuinely useful for jet lag and delayed sleep phase, at low doses (0.5–1 mg) timed correctly. Not an effective general hypnotic; commonly sold at doses far above what is needed |

## 4. Stress and cortisol

Cortisol is a glucocorticoid released from the adrenal cortex under HPA-axis control. Its acute rise with exercise is normal and necessary — it mobilises glucose and fatty acids.

The training-relevant issue is **chronic** elevation, driven by inadequate recovery, energy deficiency, sleep restriction and psychological stress. Consequences include impaired protein synthesis and increased breakdown, impaired glucose tolerance, suppressed immune function, impaired sleep (a self-reinforcing loop), and reduced testosterone.

Two corrections to common belief:

- **A single elevated post-workout cortisol reading is meaningless.** The acute rise is the intended response. Cortisol should be interpreted only in terms of chronic patterns and, if clinically indicated, by a physician.
- **"Adrenal fatigue" is not a recognised medical diagnosis.** Systematic reviews have found no substantiation for it. The genuine clinical entity is adrenal insufficiency, which is rare and diagnosed by specific testing. The symptoms attributed to "adrenal fatigue" — fatigue, poor sleep, low mood — are real, and are usually explained by inadequate sleep, energy deficiency, overtraining, depression, thyroid dysfunction, anaemia or sleep apnoea. Those are diagnosable and treatable.

**Psychological stress affects physical recovery measurably** — studies have shown that high life stress impairs recovery of strength after a bout of resistance exercise. This is a real, not merely metaphorical, training variable.

## 5. Overreaching and overtraining

The **ECSS/ACSM joint consensus statement (Meeusen et al., 2013)** is the reference. Its framework:

| State | Description | Recovery time |
|---|---|---|
| **Acute fatigue** | Normal post-session fatigue | Hours to a day |
| **Functional overreaching (FOR)** | Short-term performance decrement without severe psychological or lasting negative symptoms; **eventually leads to performance improvement after recovery** (supercompensation) | Days to ~2 weeks |
| **Non-functional overreaching (NFOR)** | Performance decrement with negative symptoms; no supercompensation | Weeks to months |
| **Overtraining syndrome (OTS)** | "Prolonged maladaptation" of the athlete and of biological, neurochemical and hormonal regulation | Months to years |

The consensus is unusually candid about the diagnostic problem:

- **"The distinction between NFOR and OTS is very difficult and will depend on the clinical outcome and exclusion diagnosis."** The athlete often shows the same clinical, hormonal and other signs.
- It is generally thought OTS symptoms are more severe than NFOR, **"however, there is no scientific evidence to either confirm or refute this suggestion."**
- Diagnosis requires **excluding organic disease and infection**, and factors including **dietary caloric restriction (negative energy balance), insufficient carbohydrate and/or protein intake, iron deficiency, magnesium deficiency and allergies**, together with identifying triggering events.
- On markers: "several markers (hormones, performance tests, psychological tests, and biochemical and immune markers) are used, but **none of them meet all the criteria to make their use generally accepted.**"

Practical implications:

1. **There is no test for overtraining.** Anyone selling one is overstating the evidence.
2. **The most common causes of what people call overtraining are not training volume** — they are energy deficiency, sleep restriction, life stress, and undiagnosed illness. Check those first.
3. **True OTS is rare** and generally occurs in high-level endurance athletes; most recreational trainees who feel "overtrained" are under-recovered, under-fed or under-slept.
4. **Prevention is the intervention**: monitor performance trends, session RPE, sleep, mood and body mass; build in planned reduced-load weeks; keep energy availability adequate.

Warning signs worth acting on: performance decrement across multiple sessions despite normal effort; persistently elevated RPE at usual loads; disrupted sleep; loss of motivation and mood disturbance; elevated resting heart rate or reduced heart rate variability sustained over days; recurrent minor illness; loss of appetite; and persistent joint or tendon discomfort.

## 6. Recovery modalities and their evidence

**Dupuy and colleagues (2018)** meta-analysed 99 studies of post-exercise recovery techniques. The overall picture: active recovery, massage, compression garments, immersion, contrast water therapy and cryotherapy all produced **small to large decreases in DOMS (−2.26 < g < −0.40)**, with no change for other methods. **Massage was the most powerful technique for recovering from DOMS and fatigue.** For markers: creatine kinase SMD −0.37 (95% CI −0.58 to −0.16), IL-6 −0.36 (−0.60 to −0.12), CRP −0.38 (−0.59 to −0.14), with massage and cold exposure most effective.

The critical caveat that runs through everything below: **these effects are on soreness, perceived fatigue and biomarkers — not, for the most part, on performance or on long-term adaptation.** Feeling recovered and being recovered are different measurements.

### Cold water immersion — the important one

**Roberts and colleagues (2015)** ran two linked studies. In the training study, 21 physically active men strength trained for 12 weeks (2 days/week) with either 10 min of CWI or active recovery after each session:

- **Strength and muscle mass increased more in the active recovery group than the CWI group (p < 0.05).**
- Isokinetic work (+19%), **Type II muscle fibre cross-sectional area (+17%)** and **myonuclei per fibre (+26%)** increased in the active recovery group (all p < 0.05) — **but not in the CWI group.**

In the acute study (9 men, single-leg strength exercise), satellite cell numbers expressing NCAM (+10–30%) and Pax7 (+20–50%) increased 24–48 h after exercise with active recovery; satellite cell counts were significantly greater after active recovery than after CWI. p70S6 kinase phosphorylation increased in both conditions but was greater after active recovery.

The authors' conclusion is explicit: **"The use of CWI as a regular post-exercise recovery strategy should be reconsidered."**

A 2026 network meta-analysis (Yu et al., 87 studies, 2 313 participants) reinforces this: for **resistance-training strength**, **passive recovery ranked highest** with moderate certainty, and CWI protocols did not improve on it (Hedges' g = 0.00, 95% CrI −0.38 to 0.38). It found possible benefits of cold, long-duration protocols for resistance-training *power* and team-sport power, but at **low certainty** with wide credible intervals. Moderate-temperature medium-duration protocols did improve muscle damage biomarkers (g = −0.51) and perceived recovery (g = −0.66) across modalities. The review notes 85% male participants, moderate-to-high heterogeneity, and that 69–85% of comparisons rested on indirect evidence.

**Practical synthesis:** CWI reduces soreness and improves how recovered you *feel*. It appears to blunt the hypertrophy and strength adaptation signal when used routinely after resistance training. Reasonable use: during competition congestion (tournaments, multi-day events) where next-day performance matters more than long-term adaptation, and in heat for thermoregulation. Unreasonable use: routinely after every hypertrophy session.

The same logic — and the same caution — applies to high-dose antioxidant supplementation, which blunts adaptation by a related mechanism (file `07`).

### Massage

The best-performing modality in the Dupuy meta-analysis for DOMS and perceived fatigue, with useful effects on inflammatory markers. Mechanisms are debated — mechanical effects on tissue, altered pain perception, parasympathetic activation, and a substantial contextual/placebo component. It does not measurably "flush lactate" (lactate clears in minutes regardless; see file `01`).

Evidence for improved subsequent performance is much weaker than the evidence for improved soreness and mood. That is still worth something: an athlete who feels better trains better.

### Foam rolling

**Wiewelhove and colleagues (2019)**, 21 studies:
- **Pre-rolling** (warm-up): sprint +0.7% (g = 0.28), flexibility +4.0% (g = 0.34); jump (−1.9%, g = 0.09) and strength (+1.8%, g = 0.12) negligible.
- **Post-rolling** (recovery): attenuated decreases in sprint (+3.1%, g = 0.34) and strength (+3.9%, g = 0.21); reduced muscle pain perception (+6.0%, g = 0.47); jump trivial.
- Foam rollers showed a tendency toward larger recovery effects on strength than roller massage sticks.
- Overall conclusion: effects are **"rather minor and partly negligible, but can be relevant in some cases"**, and the evidence **"seems to justify the widespread use of foam rolling as a warm-up activity rather than a recovery tool."**

A separate meta-analysis (Skinner et al., 2020; 32 studies) found a **large effect on range of motion (d = 0.76, 95% CI 0.55–0.98)** with no detrimental effect on performance.

So: cheap, harmless, genuinely useful for acute range of motion and mildly useful for soreness. It does not break up fascia, release adhesions, or change tissue structure — the acute ROM effect is neural, and it is transient.

### Sauna and heat exposure

**Laukkanen and colleagues (2015)** followed 2 315 middle-aged Finnish men for a median 20.7 years. Compared with one sauna session per week, **4–7 sessions per week** was associated with:
- **Sudden cardiac death HR 0.37 (95% CI 0.18–0.75)**, p for trend = 0.005.
- Similar inverse associations for fatal coronary heart disease, fatal cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality (all p for trend ≤ 0.005).
- Sessions longer than 19 min versus under 11 min: SCD HR 0.48 (95% CI 0.31–0.75), p for trend = 0.002.

This is observational and in a population with a strong sauna culture; confounding by health status, affluence and leisure time is a serious concern (people who are ill go to the sauna less). The association is nonetheless strong and dose-dependent.

For athletes specifically, **post-exercise heat exposure** has mechanistic support (heat shock proteins, plasma volume expansion, improved heat acclimation) and some evidence for endurance benefit. Whether it interferes with hypertrophy in the way cold does is unclear; the evidence currently suggests it does not, and may help.

### Compression garments

The Dupuy meta-analysis places compression among the effective techniques for DOMS and perceived fatigue. Effects on subsequent performance are small and inconsistent. Low-risk, moderate-cost, worth using for travel and congested schedules; not a priority.

### Active recovery

Low-intensity exercise after a hard session. Effective for DOMS in the meta-analysis, and — importantly — it is the *comparator* that outperformed CWI in the Roberts (2015) trial. Mechanistically it maintains blood flow and may aid metabolite clearance. It is free, has no downside, and is the default recommendation.

### Stretching

For **recovery**, stretching does not appear in the effective group in the Dupuy analysis. For **injury prevention**, the evidence is genuinely poor: systematic reviews of static stretching for hamstring injury prevention in football codes found deficient study quality and no RCT evidence; a systematic review of stretching for groin pain recovery found **no trials at all** in which stretching was the differentiating factor.

Where stretching does have a role: increasing range of motion (it works for that, and so does full-ROM resistance training), and possibly for subjective relaxation. **Static stretching immediately before power or strength performance transiently reduces output** and should be moved elsewhere in the session.

### Modalities with weak or no supporting evidence

Cupping, kinesiology tape (effects appear largely placebo and tactile), whole-body cryotherapy chambers (evidence is weaker than for cold water immersion, and the same adaptation-blunting concern applies), percussive massage guns (probably similar to foam rolling; less studied), electrical stimulation for recovery, hyperbaric oxygen for routine recovery, and infrared/red-light therapy (mechanistically interesting, clinically unproven for this purpose).

## 7. Injury management — RICE to PEACE & LOVE

**RICE** (Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation), coined by Gabe Mirkin in 1978, dominated for four decades. Mirkin himself publicly retracted the ice and rest components, on the grounds that inflammation is a necessary part of healing and that prolonged rest deconditions tissue.

**Dubois and Esculier (2020)** proposed the replacement framework in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine*:

**Immediately after injury — PEACE:**
- **P**rotect — unload or restrict movement for 1–3 days to reduce bleeding and prevent aggravation; **minimise rest, because prolonged rest compromises tissue strength and quality.**
- **E**levate — limb above heart level to promote fluid drainage.
- **A**void anti-inflammatory modalities — anti-inflammatory medications may negatively affect long-term tissue healing; ice is questioned on the same basis.
- **C**ompress — external mechanical pressure to limit swelling and haemorrhage.
- **E**ducate — set realistic expectations and avoid unnecessary passive treatments and investigations.

**After the first days — LOVE:**
- **L**oad — an active approach with movement and exercise; **optimal loading without pain promotes repair, remodelling and tissue tolerance.**
- **O**ptimism — the brain plays a key role; catastrophising, fear and depression are barriers to recovery and explain more variance in outcome than the pathophysiology in some studies.
- **V**ascularisation — pain-free cardiovascular activity increases blood flow to injured structures and improves function and motivation.
- **E**xercise — restore mobility, strength and proprioception; the evidence for exercise in reducing re-injury is strong.

**What this changes in practice:**
- Ice is downgraded to a short-term analgesic, not a treatment.
- NSAIDs are not routinely recommended for musculoskeletal soft-tissue injury; they may impair tendon and muscle healing and blunt the training adaptation signal.
- Rest is minimised to the shortest necessary window.
- Loading begins early, guided by pain, and is progressed.
- Psychological factors are treated as a genuine part of the management, not an afterthought.

For **tendinopathy** specifically (file `04`), the same principle applies with more force: progressive loading — isometrics for analgesia, then heavy slow resistance or eccentric protocols — is the evidence-based treatment. Rest makes it worse over months.

> ⚠️ Any injury with suspected fracture, dislocation, complete tendon or ligament rupture, neurological signs (numbness, weakness, loss of bowel or bladder function), significant deformity, or inability to bear weight requires urgent medical assessment. PEACE & LOVE applies to minor soft-tissue injury, not to everything.

## 8. Mobility and flexibility training

Covered from a programming angle in file `03`. The recovery-relevant points:

- **Full range-of-motion resistance training improves flexibility comparably to static stretching**, while also building strength through the range. It is the most efficient intervention available.
- **Static stretching** increases range of motion; the acute mechanism is predominantly increased stretch tolerance rather than a change in tissue length, though chronic programmes can produce structural adaptation (increased fascicle length).
- **Dynamic stretching** is the right pre-training tool; it does not carry the acute performance decrement of static stretching.
- **PNF (contract-relax) stretching** produces the largest acute ROM gains but requires a partner and is more uncomfortable.
- **More flexibility is not automatically better.** Hypermobility is associated with joint instability and injury. The target is sufficient controllable range for the tasks required, not maximal passive range.

## 9. Cardiovascular and metabolic health benefits — the dose-response

This is where the evidence base for exercise is strongest and where the effect sizes dwarf everything else in this domain.

**Arem and colleagues (2015)** pooled six cohorts from the NCI Cohort Consortium: **661 137 men and women**, median age 62, with **116 686 deaths** over a median 14.2 years of follow-up. Compared with reporting no leisure-time physical activity:

| Activity level (relative to the 7.5 MET-h/wk guideline minimum) | All-cause mortality HR (95% CI) |
|---|---|
| Below the minimum | **0.80** (0.78–0.82) — 20% lower |
| 1–2× the minimum | **0.69** (0.67–0.70) — 31% lower |
| 2–3× the minimum | **0.63** (0.62–0.65) — 37% lower |
| 3–5× the minimum | **0.61** (0.59–0.62) — 39% lower |
| ≥10× the minimum | **0.69** (0.59–0.78) — **no evidence of harm** |

Three conclusions follow, and they are among the most useful facts in this knowledge base:

1. **The steepest part of the curve is at the very bottom.** Going from nothing to *some* activity — even below the guideline minimum — buys a 20% mortality reduction. This is the single highest-return health behaviour available to a sedentary person.
2. **Meeting the guideline gets you close to the maximum available benefit.** The additional gain from 1–2× to 3–5× is modest (31% → 39%).
3. **The "too much exercise is harmful" claim is not supported** at the levels observed here. Even at ten times the recommended minimum there was no excess risk.

**Muscle-strengthening activity** contributes independently. **Momma and colleagues (2022)** meta-analysed 16 prospective cohorts: muscle-strengthening activities were associated with a **10–17% lower risk of all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease, total cancer, diabetes and lung cancer**, independent of aerobic activity. The associations were **J-shaped**, with maximum risk reduction (~10–20%) at approximately **30–60 min/week** of muscle-strengthening activity for all-cause mortality, CVD and total cancer; the diabetes association was L-shaped with large risk reduction up to 60 min/week. Combined muscle-strengthening and aerobic activity was associated with lower all-cause, CVD and cancer mortality than neither. The authors note the influence of *higher* volumes is unclear given the J-shape.

**Cardiorespiratory fitness** is the strongest single predictor in this literature; see file `10` for Mandsager (2018).

**Mechanisms** of the cardiometabolic benefit include: improved endothelial function and nitric oxide bioavailability; reduced resting and ambulatory blood pressure; improved lipid profile (particularly raised HDL and lowered triglycerides); improved insulin sensitivity and GLUT4 translocation (an acute effect lasting 24–72 h after a single session, which is why frequency matters); reduced visceral adiposity; reduced systemic inflammation; improved autonomic balance and heart rate variability; and improved fibrinolytic function.

**Exercise for blood pressure** specifically: a 2026 network meta-analysis of 31 trials and 1 345 participants with hypertension found reductions in 24-hour ambulatory systolic blood pressure of **−6.18 mmHg for combined training** (95% CrI −11.45 to −1.21), **−4.73 mmHg for aerobic training** (−7.53 to −2.01) and **−5.71 mmHg for HIIT** (−11.31 to −0.002). Evidence for dynamic and isometric resistance training on 24-hour ambulatory pressure remained uncertain in that analysis.

**Mental health.** Exercise has moderate antidepressant and anxiolytic effects in meta-analyses, comparable in magnitude to some first-line treatments for mild-to-moderate depression, though the trial literature has methodological weaknesses (blinding is impossible; control conditions vary widely). It is a reasonable adjunct, not a replacement for treatment of moderate-to-severe illness.

## Open questions

- The **sleep hygiene table** synthesises evidence quality from the general sleep-medicine literature rather than from individually retrieved meta-analyses for each row. `needs-verification` on the specific gradings.
- Whether **post-exercise heat exposure** interferes with hypertrophy adaptation the way cold does is not well studied; the current position (that it does not) is provisional.
- **Whole-body cryotherapy** versus cold water immersion has not been directly compared for adaptation-blunting effects at adequate scale.
- **Sleep requirement for athletes in heavy training** is often quoted as 9–10 h; the evidence for a specific elevated requirement is weaker than the evidence that more sleep helps performance in already-restricted athletes.
- The **Mah 2011** sleep-extension study had n = 11 and no control group; the effect magnitudes should be treated as provisional despite the strong p-values.
- Whether the **Laukkanen sauna** association is causal cannot be determined from an observational cohort; reverse causation (healthier men sauna more) is a live alternative explanation.
