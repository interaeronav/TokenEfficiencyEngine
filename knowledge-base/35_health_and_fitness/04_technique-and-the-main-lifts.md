# Technique and the main lifts — mechanics, cues, faults and injury risk

**Summary.** Good technique is the position and movement path that lets a given person produce force safely and repeatably, given their anthropometry and their goal. It is not a single universal template. This file covers the major lifts by joint action and prime mover, gives setup and execution detail, names the faults that actually matter and why, treats individual anthropometry seriously (because segment lengths dictate a large part of "correct" positioning), and closes with an honest account of what causes lifting injuries — which is overwhelmingly load management, not the specific shape of a rep.

## 1. General principles before any specific lift

**Force is applied through a base of support.** The system centre of mass (lifter + bar) must stay over the base or the lift fails. This single constraint explains most of what looks like "style" — a lifter with long femurs must lean further forward in a squat to keep the bar over midfoot, and that is correct for them.

**Moment arms determine effort distribution.** The perpendicular distance from a joint to the load's line of action sets the torque that joint must resist. Lengthening the moment arm at the hip (more forward lean) shifts demand to the hip extensors and spinal erectors; shortening it shifts demand to the knee extensors. This is a dial, not a fault.

**Bracing.** The Valsalva manoeuvre — a deep breath held against a closed glottis with active abdominal and pelvic-floor co-contraction — raises intra-abdominal pressure, stiffens the trunk, and measurably increases spinal stability and force output. It also transiently raises arterial and intraocular pressure; that is the reason for caution in people with uncontrolled hypertension, known aneurysm, retinal disease or recent abdominal/inguinal surgery. Belts increase intra-abdominal pressure further and generally increase force output; they are a tool, not a crutch, and they do not weaken the trunk musculature in any demonstrated way.

**Symmetry is a poor target.** Small asymmetries are near-universal, largely irrelevant, and pursuing perfect symmetry generally wastes training resources.

## 2. Squat and knee-dominant variants

**Joint actions.** Concentric: knee extension, hip extension, ankle plantarflexion; trunk resists flexion isometrically.
**Prime movers.** Quadriceps (all four heads), gluteus maximus, adductor magnus (a major and under-recognised hip extensor at depth). Synergists: hamstrings (via hip extension, though they lengthen at the hip and shorten at the knee, so net length change is small), erector spinae, soleus, trunk musculature.

### High-bar back squat
Bar on the upper trapezius above the spine of the scapula. More upright torso, greater knee travel, greater knee flexion at depth. Emphasises quadriceps.

**Setup.** Bar set at mid-chest height. Grip as narrow as shoulder mobility comfortably allows to create a shelf of trapezius. Elbows under the bar, chest up. Unrack with both feet under the bar, step back in two or three steps. Stance roughly shoulder-width to slightly wider, toes turned out 10–30° depending on hip anatomy.

**Execution.** Big brace. Break at hips and knees together. Track knees over the line of the toes, allowing them to travel forward. Descend to at least parallel (hip crease below the top of the knee) if mobility allows. Reverse without relaxing the brace or losing the arch. Drive through the whole foot.

### Low-bar back squat
Bar across the posterior deltoids and mid-trapezius, roughly 5 cm lower. Shorter moment arm to the hip, more forward lean, less knee travel. Emphasises hip extensors and typically allows greater load — the reason powerlifters favour it. Requires more shoulder external rotation and can irritate elbows and anterior shoulder.

The Park (2026) EMG data are consistent with this: low-bar showed greater hamstring and erector spinae activation in specific comparisons, high-bar greater knee-extensor activation (most clearly rectus femoris) at selected depths.

### Front squat
Bar in the anterior rack position across the anterior deltoids and clavicles. Most upright torso, greatest quadriceps and upper-back demand, lowest absolute load. Limited by thoracic extension, wrist and shoulder mobility, and by the ability of the upper back to resist flexion — which is usually what fails first.

### Other knee-dominant options
- **Hack squat / pendulum squat.** Machine-guided; excellent quadriceps stimulus with low stabilisation and low technical demand. Good stimulus-to-fatigue ratio.
- **Leg press.** High quadriceps and gluteal loading with no spinal compression. Watch for pelvic posterior tilt ("butt wink") at depth under load.
- **Belt squat.** Loads the legs with no axial spinal load; valuable when the back is the limiting factor.
- **Goblet squat.** Best teaching tool: the anterior counterweight naturally produces an upright torso and depth.

### Common faults and their actual consequences

| Fault | Description | Consequence | Usual cause |
|---|---|---|---|
| Knee valgus under load | Knees collapse inward | Genuine concern if severe and uncontrolled, especially in a maximal effort; mild inward travel on a grinder is common and not automatically harmful | Load too heavy; weak hip abductors/external rotators; motor control |
| "Butt wink" | Posterior pelvic tilt with lumbar flexion at depth | Under near-maximal load, flexion under high compression is the plausible mechanism for disc injury; at moderate loads its significance is over-stated | Hip anatomy (retroversion, deep socket), ankle dorsiflexion limitation, exceeding available hip flexion |
| Good-morning squat | Hips shoot up, torso pitches forward | Turns the squat into a hinge; usually means quadriceps are the weak link, or the load exceeds capacity | Load too heavy; weak quadriceps; bracing failure |
| Heel rise | Heels leave the floor | Shifts load forward, reduces stability | Ankle dorsiflexion restriction; stance too narrow; trying to stay artificially upright |
| Losing the brace | Air released mid-rep | Loss of trunk stiffness under load | Poor breathing pattern; excessive rep counts on heavy sets |

### Anthropometry

Squat "correctness" is heavily individual:
- **Long femurs relative to torso** → more forward lean is unavoidable; a wider stance and slightly more toe-out usually helps; low-bar often suits.
- **Short femurs, long torso** → upright squatting comes easily; high-bar and front squat suit.
- **Hip socket depth and orientation** (acetabular anteversion/retroversion, femoral neck angle) vary substantially between individuals and are visible on imaging. This determines available hip flexion and the stance width and toe angle at which the femur clears the acetabular rim. It is bone. No amount of mobility work changes it. **A person who cannot squat below parallel with a narrow stance may do so easily with a wider one — and vice versa.**
- **Ankle dorsiflexion range** limits how far the knee can travel forward. Heeled shoes are a legitimate accommodation, not a weakness.

### Loading progression
Beginner: add 2.5–5 kg per session while form holds. Intermediate: weekly or per-block progression by double progression or by RPE-guided top sets. Advanced: block periodisation, months-long timescales. Squat depth should be standardised before load is progressed — otherwise "progress" is often just a shallower squat.

## 3. Deadlift and hip-dominant variants

**Joint actions.** Hip extension and knee extension simultaneously; the spine and scapulae are held isometrically against enormous flexion and protraction torque.
**Prime movers.** Gluteus maximus, hamstrings, quadriceps (more than commonly assumed, especially off the floor in conventional and substantially in sumo), erector spinae, latissimus dorsi (holding the bar close), trapezius, forearm flexors.

### Conventional deadlift
**Setup.** Bar over midfoot, roughly 2–3 cm from the shin. Feet hip-width, toes slightly out. Hinge to the bar, grip just outside the legs. Drop hips until shins contact the bar. Chest up, lats engaged ("bend the bar", "protect the armpits"), spine in whatever neutral means for that person. Take the slack out of the bar — a distinct audible click — before pulling.

**Execution.** Push the floor away. Hips and shoulders rise together; the bar drags up the shins and thighs. Lock out by driving hips forward to full extension — no hyperextension, no leaning back. Return by hinging first, then bending knees once the bar passes them.

### Sumo deadlift
Wide stance, hands inside the knees. Reduces the horizontal distance from the bar to the hips, shortening the range of motion and the hip moment arm, at the cost of a more demanding starting position and greater adductor and quadriceps demand. Neither variant is "cheating"; they are different movements with different mechanical demands. Sumo suits lifters with short arms, long torsos, and good hip abduction and external rotation.

### Romanian deadlift (RDL)
Starts from the top. Hips travel backwards with minimal knee bend, lowering the bar along the thighs until hamstring tension limits the descent (typically mid-shin), then hips drive forward. Hamstrings are loaded at long lengths, which is exactly where hypertrophy stimulus is strongest. The RDL is arguably the highest-value hamstring exercise available and is far less fatiguing than a heavy conventional pull.

### Other hinge variants
- **Trap-bar deadlift.** Neutral grip, load closer to the centre of mass. Reduced spinal shear, more knee involvement. The best default deadlift for a general trainee.
- **Good morning.** Bar on the back, hinge to horizontal. Very high spinal erector and hamstring demand at low absolute load. Useful and frequently mis-loaded.
- **Back extension / 45° hyperextension.** Excellent glute and hamstring work, near-zero systemic cost.
- **Deficit and block/rack pulls.** Range-of-motion manipulations for addressing specific sticking points.

### Common faults

| Fault | Consequence | Cause |
|---|---|---|
| Bar drifting away from the body | Massively increases hip moment arm; the single most common reason a pull fails or the back rounds | Weak lats; starting with the bar too far forward; hips rising too early |
| Hips rising before the bar | Converts the pull into a stiff-legged deadlift under load | Quadriceps too weak for the start position; load too heavy; poor sequencing |
| Jerking the bar off the floor | Slack in the system means a sudden shock load, often to a poor position | Not taking slack out before pulling |
| Excessive lumbar flexion under near-maximal load | Under heavy compression this is the mechanism most plausibly linked to disc injury. Note: a small degree of thoracic flexion is normal and common in elite pullers, and is not the same thing | Load exceeds capacity; poor bracing |
| Hyperextending at lockout | Unnecessary lumbar extension under load; no benefit | Misunderstanding "lockout" |

### The spinal flexion question, honestly

The dogmatic position ("any lumbar flexion under load will injure you") is not supported by the evidence. Spines flex; flexion is a normal movement; people lift objects with flexed spines constantly without injury; and elite deadlifters routinely show measurable thoracic and some lumbar flexion.

The defensible position: **the load-bearing capacity of the spine is highest in a braced near-neutral position, and capacity is trainable.** Deliberately loading a maximally flexed spine at maximal loads is a poor risk trade. Panicking about a few degrees of flexion on a heavy top set is equally unhelpful and has produced a generation of trainees who are afraid of their own backs — which the low back pain literature identifies as an independent risk factor for chronicity (file `10`).

### Anthropometry
Long arms and a short torso make conventional pulling mechanically favourable. Short arms make sumo or trap-bar the better choice. Hip structure again determines how wide a sumo stance can be. Grip is very often the limiting factor: mixed grip, hook grip, or straps are all legitimate; straps are only "cheating" if grip is the trained quality.

### Loading progression
The deadlift accumulates fatigue faster than any other lift because of its combination of load, range, and axial spinal loading. Most intermediate lifters do better deadlifting heavy once a week with a second lighter hinge session (RDL or trap-bar), rather than pulling heavy twice.

## 4. Bench press and horizontal pressing

**Joint actions.** Shoulder horizontal adduction and flexion, elbow extension, scapular retraction/protraction control.
**Prime movers.** Pectoralis major (sternal and clavicular heads), anterior deltoid, triceps brachii. Synergists/stabilisers: serratus anterior, rotator cuff, latissimus dorsi (eccentrically controlling the bar), lower body providing leg drive.

**Setup.** Eyes under or just behind the bar. Feet flat and driving into the floor. Scapulae retracted and depressed and *held there* — this is the single most important setup element. A moderate thoracic arch is normal and reduces range of motion and shoulder stress; an extreme lumbar arch is a powerlifting-specific technique, not a general recommendation. Grip such that at the bottom the forearms are roughly vertical.

**Execution.** Unrack to a locked position over the shoulders. Lower under control to the lower chest/sternum, elbows at roughly 45–75° to the torso (not flared to 90°, not pinned to the ribs). Touch, then press back and slightly toward the face, following a shallow J-curve. Maintain scapular position throughout.

### Variants
- **Dumbbell press.** Greater range of motion, independent limb loading, higher stabilisation demand, easier on the shoulders for many people.
- **Incline press (15–30°).** Biases the clavicular head of pectoralis major and anterior deltoid. Steeper than about 45° becomes a shoulder press.
- **Dip.** Excellent lower-pec and triceps builder; loads the shoulder at long lengths, which some tolerate poorly. Easy to load progressively with a belt.
- **Machine and Smith press.** Lower stabilisation demand, often better stimulus-to-fatigue for chest specifically, safer to train to failure without a spotter.

### Common faults

| Fault | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Losing scapular retraction | Shoulder rolls forward into a poor position; the most common cause of anterior shoulder pain in benchers |
| Elbows flared to 90° | Increases anterior shoulder stress; usually reduces force output too |
| Bouncing off the chest | Removes the hardest portion of the lift; occasionally causes costochondral injury |
| Uneven bar path / one-sided press | Usually a strength or setup asymmetry; correct by unilateral work rather than by chasing it on the bar |
| Feet moving / hips lifting | Loss of the kinetic chain; in competition, hips leaving the bench is a red light |

### Anthropometry
Long arms mean a longer range of motion and a mechanically harder bench; wider grips and a greater arch (within safety) partially compensate. Rib cage depth changes range of motion meaningfully. Shoulder structure — acromion shape, glenoid version — varies and influences which pressing angles are comfortable.

## 5. Overhead pressing

**Joint actions.** Shoulder flexion/abduction, elbow extension, scapular upward rotation, trunk anti-extension.
**Prime movers.** Anterior and lateral deltoid, triceps, upper trapezius and serratus anterior (upward rotation), with substantial trunk bracing demand.

**Setup and execution.** Bar in the front rack at clavicle height, grip just outside shoulder width, elbows slightly in front of the bar. Squeeze glutes and brace hard to prevent lumbar hyperextension. Move the head back slightly so the bar passes the chin, press up, then push the head "through" so the bar finishes directly over the mid-foot with the biceps beside the ears. Lockout requires full shoulder flexion and scapular upward rotation.

Variants: **push press** (leg drive assists, allows heavier loading), **seated press** (removes leg drive and much trunk contribution; better for isolating the shoulders, worse for transfer), **dumbbell press** (independent limbs, more range), **landmine press** (a hybrid angle that many people with shoulder restrictions tolerate well).

**Faults.** Excessive lumbar hyperextension (converts an overhead press into a standing incline press and loads the low back needlessly); failing to move the head out of the way (bar path deviates forward); pressing with the elbows too far behind the bar at the start; insufficient lockout.

**Anthropometry and mobility.** Full overhead position requires roughly 170–180° of shoulder flexion with adequate thoracic extension. People who cannot achieve it should not force it — use landmine, incline or neutral-grip dumbbell pressing instead of grinding against a structural limit.

## 6. Rows, pulldowns and pull-ups

**Joint actions (rows).** Shoulder horizontal extension/extension, elbow flexion, scapular retraction; trunk isometric.
**Prime movers.** Latissimus dorsi, rhomboids, middle and lower trapezius, posterior deltoid, teres major, biceps and brachialis.

### Row variants
- **Barbell row (bent-over).** High total-back stimulus; also very high spinal erector demand, which is why it accumulates fatigue quickly and is easy to cheat.
- **Chest-supported / T-bar row.** Removes the spinal-erector cost, allowing the target muscles to be trained closer to failure. Excellent stimulus-to-fatigue ratio.
- **Single-arm dumbbell row.** Greater range of motion, addresses asymmetry, allows a slight torso rotation that many find comfortable.
- **Seated cable row.** Consistent tension through the range, easy to load and progress.

Elbow path changes emphasis: elbows tucked close to the body biases latissimus dorsi; elbows flared toward 90° biases rhomboids, mid-trapezius and posterior deltoid.

### Vertical pulling
- **Pull-up (pronated grip).** Latissimus dorsi and teres major dominant; hardest variant.
- **Chin-up (supinated).** More biceps contribution; usually the strongest variant. Studies of chin-up versus pull-up EMG show broadly similar lat activation with greater biceps in the chin-up.
- **Neutral grip.** Often the most shoulder- and elbow-friendly.
- **Lat pulldown.** Allows loads below and above bodyweight, and allows precise progression — genuinely superior to pull-ups for hypertrophy programming in most people, because you can load it exactly.

**Faults.** Using momentum ("kipping" outside a CrossFit context, or heaving a barbell row with hip extension); not achieving full scapular depression and shoulder adduction at the bottom of a pulldown; shrugging at the top of a row; failing to reach a genuinely lengthened position at the start.

**Anthropometry.** Long arms make pull-ups harder and rows longer in range. Torso length affects how much lat is involved versus mid-back.

## 7. Hip thrust

**Joint actions.** Hip extension from a flexed position with the knees bent (which shortens the hamstrings and biases the glutes).
**Prime movers.** Gluteus maximus principally; hamstrings and quadriceps as synergists.

**Setup.** Upper back on a bench at roughly the level of the inferior scapular border. Feet placed so that at the top the shins are vertical. Bar across the hips with a pad. Tuck the chin and maintain a posterior pelvic tilt at lockout.

**Execution.** Drive through the heels to full hip extension. Pause briefly at the top with the glutes maximally contracted; the peak torque demand occurs at full extension, which is unusual and complementary to squats and deadlifts (where peak demand occurs at depth).

**Why it earns a place.** The resistance profile is the mirror image of the squat: the hip thrust is hardest where the squat is easiest. EMG studies consistently show high gluteal activation, particularly at end range. It is also low in systemic fatigue relative to a heavy squat.

**Faults.** Hyperextending the lumbar spine instead of extending the hip (the most common); feet too far forward (turns it into a hamstring exercise) or too close (excessive quadriceps involvement); insufficient range at the bottom.

**Caveat.** The claim that the hip thrust is definitively superior to the squat for glute hypertrophy rests substantially on EMG data and on work by researchers with a commercial interest in the exercise. EMG amplitude is a poor predictor of long-term hypertrophy. Direct hypertrophy comparisons are limited and mixed. Use both.

## 8. Lunges and unilateral work

**Variants.** Forward lunge, reverse lunge, walking lunge, Bulgarian split squat (rear-foot-elevated), step-up, single-leg RDL, single-leg press.

**Value.**
- Trains each limb independently, addressing genuine asymmetry.
- Loads the hip in a frontal- and transverse-plane-challenged position, recruiting gluteus medius and minimus.
- Achieves high muscular stimulus at low spinal load — valuable when the back is fatigued or injured.
- Reverse lunges are generally easier on the knee than forward lunges (less anterior shear at contact) and easier to control.
- The Bulgarian split squat produces very high quadriceps and gluteal stimulus but is limited by balance and is genuinely unpleasant; it is also excellent.

**Faults.** Torso collapsing forward without control; the front knee travelling excessively inward; too short a step (excessive knee travel) or too long (turns into a pure hinge); using a load that balance cannot support, which caps the stimulus below the muscular limit.

**Note on "fixing imbalances".** Unilateral work is useful, but most inter-limb differences of under about 10% are normal, and the evidence that correcting small asymmetries reduces injury risk is weak.

## 9. The Olympic lifts, in brief

The **snatch** (ground to overhead in one movement) and the **clean and jerk** (ground to shoulders, then shoulders to overhead) are the competition lifts of weightlifting.

**Phases (snatch and clean).** First pull (floor to knee, relatively slow, back angle constant) → transition/double knee bend (knees re-bend under the bar) → second pull (violent triple extension of hip, knee and ankle — the highest power output in sport) → turnover and receive (pulling under the bar into an overhead or front squat) → recovery.

**What they train.** Rate of force development and peak power. Elite weightlifters generate among the highest recorded human power outputs. Also: exceptional mobility, coordination and confidence under load.

**Honest assessment for the general trainee.** The learning cost is very high — months to years for competence, ideally with in-person coaching. The transfer to non-weightlifting outcomes over what could be achieved with jumps, throws, and heavy squats and pulls is modest and contested. If someone enjoys them, they are excellent. If the goal is hypertrophy or general strength, the opportunity cost is real.

Accessible derivatives — **hang power clean, high pull, push press, jump shrug** — capture much of the power development at a fraction of the learning cost.

Injury data (Keogh & Winwood 2017) do not show weightlifting to be unusually dangerous; the shoulder, knee, low back and wrist dominate, as elsewhere.

## 10. Machine work

Machines are not inferior. They are a different tool with a different profile.

**Advantages:** guided path lowers stabilisation demand and lets the target muscle be trained closer to failure; cam-based machines can provide resistance profiles matched to the strength curve, which free weights cannot; safer to train to failure alone; excellent for injury work-arounds; lower technical barrier; time-efficient.

**Limitations:** fixed path may not suit all body proportions (check that the axis of rotation aligns with the joint); less transfer to free-weight and sporting skills; a machine set is not a substitute for learning to brace and stabilise under load.

**Cables** deserve special mention: they provide near-constant tension, allow resistance from any angle, and are the best tool for loading muscles in positions that free weights cannot (lateral raises at the bottom, lat pullovers, cable flies at full stretch).

The evidence (Schoenfeld 2017 and the load literature generally) shows machines and free weights both produce hypertrophy; the choice should follow the goal, not tribal preference.

## 11. Injury risk — what actually causes lifting injuries

**Base rates.** Keogh and Winwood's (2017) systematic review of 20 studies found weight-training sports have **relatively low rates of injury compared with common team sports**. Bodybuilding was lowest (0.12–0.7 injuries per lifter per year; 0.24–1 per 1000 h). Strongman (4.5–6.1 per 1000 h) and Highland Games (7.5 per 1000 h) were highest. Shoulder, lower back, knee, elbow and wrist/hand dominated; strains, tendinitis and sprains were the most common types. Notably, **very few significant differences were observed by age, sex, competitive standard or bodyweight class** — which undercuts several common assumptions. The authors also note that most included studies were retrospective and only five of twenty had low risk of bias, so the estimates are soft.

### What does cause injuries

1. **Load management errors.** Doing too much, too soon, too fast, after too little. Sudden spikes in volume or intensity relative to recent training are the best-supported modifiable risk factor across sport generally.
2. **Insufficient recovery.** Inadequate sleep, chronic energy deficiency, and high non-training stress all reduce tissue tolerance.
3. **Fatigue-driven technical breakdown at high loads.** Not the technique itself, but technique degrading under a load the person cannot control.
4. **Prior injury.** The strongest single predictor of a future injury at a site is a previous injury at that site.
5. **Genuine accidents.** Dropped weights, equipment failure, slipping, losing a spotter.

### What largely does not cause injuries

- **Slight deviations from an idealised movement template.** There is no evidence that a few degrees of knee valgus on a hard rep, or minor thoracic rounding, cause injury at ordinary training loads.
- **Squatting past parallel.** Deep squatting in trained individuals is not associated with increased knee injury; if anything, deep squatting builds tissue tolerance.
- **Knees travelling past the toes.** This long-standing prohibition has no support. It increases knee moment while *decreasing* hip and lumbar moment, and preventing it forces excessive forward lean.
- **Lifting with any spinal flexion.** See section 3. Load matters far more than a small amount of curve.
- **"Muscle imbalance"** as a general explanation. Popular, largely untested.

### Tendinopathy specifically

Tendon problems are the most common chronic complaint in lifters (patellar, Achilles, elbow, rotator cuff, distal biceps). The modern model is **overuse plus insufficient adaptation time**, not inflammation — hence "tendinopathy" rather than "tendinitis". Management centres on progressive loading (isometrics for pain relief, then heavy slow resistance or eccentric loading), not rest. Complete rest deconditions the tendon and typically makes the problem worse over months. This aligns with the PEACE & LOVE framework in file `09`, which explicitly moves away from rest and anti-inflammatories toward optimism, loading and exercise.

### Practical risk reduction

- Progress load gradually; avoid sudden volume jumps after layoffs.
- Keep most work at 1–3 RIR rather than habitual maximal grinding.
- Do not test a maximum when fatigued, unwell or underslept.
- Use collars, safeties, a competent spotter, and a rack you can bail in.
- Train the full body, including the muscles that get skipped (rotator cuff, hamstrings at the knee, neck, grip, calves).
- Treat pain as information, not as something to push through. Progressive, tolerable loading beats both rest and denial.

## Open questions

- Specific EMG comparisons cited (chin-up versus pull-up biceps contribution; hip thrust gluteal activation) are described qualitatively because the primary EMG papers were not directly retrieved in this pass. Marked `needs-verification`.
- The claim that deep squatting does not increase knee injury risk in trained populations is well-supported in narrative reviews but was not verified here against a specific systematic review. `needs-verification`.
- The overhead-press shoulder-flexion range figure (~170–180°) is a textbook value, not a cited measurement.
- Direct hypertrophy comparisons of hip thrust versus squat for gluteus maximus are limited; conclusions in the popular literature outrun the trial evidence.
