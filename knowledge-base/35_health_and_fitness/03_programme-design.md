# Programme design — from needs analysis to worked programmes

**Summary.** A training programme is a set of decisions, in order: what the person actually needs, what constraints they operate under, how movement patterns will be covered, how weekly volume will be distributed, how progression will be regulated, and how fatigue will be managed. Almost every popular "programme" is a template that answers these questions implicitly for a hypothetical average person. This file makes the reasoning explicit, gives defensible volume landmarks, covers autoregulation and deloads, and works three complete programmes — beginner, intermediate hypertrophy, and strength-focused — showing why each choice was made rather than only what the sets and reps are.

## Key facts

| Decision | Default position | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Movement pattern coverage | Squat, hinge, horizontal push, vertical push, horizontal pull, vertical pull, carry/anti-rotation, plus direct arm and calf work if hypertrophy is a goal | Convention; ensures no major pattern is uncovered |
| Frequency per muscle | ≥2×/week | Schoenfeld 2016 (ES 0.49 vs 0.30, p = 0.002) |
| Weekly hard sets per muscle (hypertrophy) | ~10–20 as a working range for most trainees | Schoenfeld 2017 dose-response, extrapolated cautiously |
| Rest between hard compound sets | 2–3 min | Schoenfeld 2016 (3 min > 1 min for strength and quad hypertrophy) |
| Effort target | 0–3 RIR on most working sets | Grgic 2022 (failure not required) |
| Sessions per week | 3–5 for most people; 2 is enough for meaningful gains in beginners | WHO 2020 minimum is ≥2 muscle-strengthening days |
| Deload | Every 4–8 weeks, or autoregulated on accumulated fatigue markers | Convention; weak direct evidence |
| Cardio minimum | 150–300 min/wk moderate or 75–150 min/wk vigorous | WHO 2020 |

## 1. Needs analysis

Before any set is prescribed, six questions:

**1. What is the actual goal?** Distinguish the stated goal from the operative one. "I want to get fit" usually decomposes into some mix of: look better (hypertrophy plus fat loss), lift more (strength), perform in a sport, feel better day to day, or reduce disease risk. These lead to genuinely different programmes. A programme that serves all of them equally serves none of them well.

**2. What is the training age?** Not calendar time in a gym — time spent training with progressive overload and reasonable technique. Someone who has "trained for five years" but never progressed load is a beginner. Training age determines progression rate, volume requirement, and how much variation is needed.

**3. What are the constraints?** Days available, session length, equipment access, travel, work shift pattern, family. The best programme the person will actually complete beats the theoretically optimal one they will not. **[NA]** In Namibia and much of southern Africa, equipment access outside Windhoek and the coastal towns can be limited; a programme built around barbells and cable stacks may need a dumbbell-and-bodyweight fallback.

**4. What is the injury and medical history?** Prior injuries constrain exercise selection and loading progression. Medical conditions may constrain intensity (see file `10`). This is where a physician referral belongs.

**5. What is the recovery capacity?** Sleep duration and quality, energy availability (are they in a deficit?), psychological stress, age, and non-training physical work. A person in a 25% energy deficit sleeping six hours while working night shifts cannot absorb the volume that the meta-analyses studied in rested, fed university students.

**6. What is the person's disposition?** Some people thrive on structure and numbers; others abandon anything that feels like a spreadsheet. Autoregulation suits the former; simple fixed templates suit the latter. Adherence is the largest single determinant of long-run outcome and it is a psychological variable, not a physiological one.

## 2. Exercise categorisation

Organising exercises by **movement pattern** rather than by muscle prevents gaps and makes substitution easy.

| Pattern | Primary joint action | Examples | Prime movers |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Squat** (knee-dominant) | Knee extension with hip extension | Back squat, front squat, leg press, split squat, hack squat | Quadriceps, gluteus maximus, adductor magnus |
| **Hinge** (hip-dominant) | Hip extension with relatively little knee travel | Deadlift, Romanian deadlift, good morning, hip thrust, back extension | Gluteus maximus, hamstrings, erector spinae |
| **Horizontal push** | Shoulder horizontal adduction + elbow extension | Bench press, dumbbell press, dip, push-up | Pectoralis major, anterior deltoid, triceps |
| **Vertical push** | Shoulder flexion/abduction + elbow extension | Overhead press, push press, machine shoulder press | Deltoids, triceps, upper trapezius, serratus |
| **Horizontal pull** | Shoulder horizontal abduction/extension + elbow flexion | Barbell row, dumbbell row, chest-supported row, seated cable row | Latissimus dorsi, rhomboids, mid-trapezius, posterior deltoid, biceps |
| **Vertical pull** | Shoulder adduction/extension + elbow flexion | Pull-up, chin-up, lat pulldown | Latissimus dorsi, teres major, biceps, lower trapezius |
| **Carry / anti-movement** | Isometric trunk and shoulder-girdle stabilisation | Farmer's walk, suitcase carry, plank variants, Pallof press | Trunk musculature, grip, upper trapezius |
| **Rotation / anti-rotation** | Trunk rotation or resisting it | Cable woodchop, landmine rotation, Pallof press | Obliques, transversus abdominis |
| **Direct isolation** | Single-joint | Curl, triceps extension, lateral raise, leg curl, calf raise, face pull | As named |

Two design notes. First, **carries and anti-rotation work are the most commonly omitted patterns** and are cheap to include. Second, a programme covering all patterns still needs deliberate isolation work if hypertrophy is a goal — compound lifts do not adequately load lateral deltoid, biceps long head at long lengths, hamstrings at the knee, or calves.

## 3. Split structures and the evidence on each

The literature on split *structure* per se is thin, because splits are confounded with volume and frequency. What follows combines the frequency evidence (file `02`, section 5) with practical logistics.

### Full body

Every session covers most major patterns. Typically 2–4 sessions/week.

- **Strengths.** Highest frequency per muscle at low session count — ideal for 2–3 days/week. Misses less if a session is skipped. Excellent for beginners, where technique practice frequency matters more than volume.
- **Weaknesses.** Volume per muscle per session is limited by session length and by fatigue accumulating across the session. Hard to accumulate 15–20 sets per muscle per week on three full-body sessions without very long sessions.
- **Best for.** Beginners; time-constrained trainees; strength-focused lifters using low set counts at high intensity; anyone training 2–3 days/week.

### Upper / lower

Alternating upper- and lower-body days, typically 4 sessions/week (2 upper, 2 lower).

- **Strengths.** Hits the Schoenfeld 2016 twice-weekly frequency finding exactly. Allows adequate per-session volume. Good balance of frequency and recovery. Scales to 5–6 sessions.
- **Weaknesses.** Lower days are demanding; on a 4-day schedule the second lower session often suffers.
- **Best for.** Intermediates; probably the highest-value default for a general hypertrophy-and-strength trainee training 4 days/week.

### Push / pull / legs (PPL)

Push (chest, shoulders, triceps), pull (back, biceps), legs. Run once weekly (3 days) or twice (6 days).

- **Strengths.** At 6 days/week gives twice-weekly frequency with high volume capacity and logical grouping. Popular and well-understood.
- **Weaknesses.** At 3 days/week it is once-weekly frequency per muscle, which the frequency meta-analysis argues against. Six days/week is a large adherence and recovery commitment.
- **Best for.** Intermediate-to-advanced hypertrophy trainees who can reliably train 5–6 days/week.

### Body-part ("bro") split

One or two muscle groups per session, each trained once weekly.

- **Strengths.** Very high volume per muscle per session; high focus; historically used by successful bodybuilders (most of whom were drug-assisted, which materially changes recovery capacity — see file `08`).
- **Weaknesses.** Once-weekly frequency is the condition the frequency meta-analysis found inferior on a volume-equated basis. Sets late in a 20-set chest session are of markedly lower quality. A missed session means a muscle goes untrained for two weeks.
- **Best for.** Advanced trainees with a specific reason; not a good default.

### Practical ranking

For a general trainee, **upper/lower at 4 days** or **full body at 3 days** are the highest-value defaults. PPL×2 is excellent if 6 days is genuinely sustainable. Body-part splits are defensible but start from a disadvantage.

## 4. Weekly volume landmarks by muscle group

The table below is a **practitioner framework**, not a meta-analytic output. It is broadly consistent with the Schoenfeld (2017) dose-response but the specific per-muscle numbers are convention. The concept of graded landmarks — a minimum effective dose, a productive range, and a recoverable maximum — is most associated with Mike Israetel and Renaissance Periodization; treat the numbers as starting hypotheses to be adjusted individually.

| Muscle group | Minimum effective (sets/wk) | Productive range (sets/wk) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chest | 4–6 | 10–20 | Gets indirect volume from pressing; count it |
| Back (lats + upper back) | 6–8 | 12–22 | Large muscle group; often under-volumed. Split rows and pulldowns |
| Quadriceps | 6–8 | 10–20 | High fatigue cost per set; squats are expensive |
| Hamstrings | 4–6 | 8–16 | Needs both hip-hinge and knee-flexion work |
| Glutes | 4 | 8–16 | Large indirect volume from squats/hinges |
| Shoulders (lateral/rear delts) | 4–6 | 10–20 | Anterior delt is usually over-served by pressing; laterals and rears usually under-served |
| Biceps | 4 | 8–16 | Substantial indirect volume from pulling |
| Triceps | 4 | 8–16 | Substantial indirect volume from pressing |
| Calves | 4 | 8–16 | Highly variable individual response |
| Trunk / core | 2–4 | 4–10 | Direct work; carries count |

Rules for using this table:

1. **Count only hard sets** — sets taken within ~0–4 RIR with a genuine load. Warm-ups and easy back-off sets do not count.
2. **Count indirect volume at roughly half.** A set of barbell rows contributes meaningfully to biceps; a set of bench press contributes to triceps and anterior deltoid.
3. **Start at the low end and add.** Beginning at 20 sets/muscle/week gives you nowhere to go and buries you in fatigue. Start near the minimum effective dose, progress load, and add volume only when progression stalls despite good recovery.
4. **In an energy deficit, reduce the top end.** Recovery capacity falls with energy availability. Maintaining volume in a deficit is a good way to accumulate fatigue without adaptation.

## 5. Autoregulation

Fixed percentage-based programming assumes today's capacity equals the plan's assumption. It rarely does. Autoregulation adjusts the session to actual readiness.

### RPE and RIR

The **repetitions-in-reserve-based RPE scale** (Zourdos et al., 2016) maps perceived exertion directly onto how many reps remain:

| RPE | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 10 | Maximal; no more reps possible (0 RIR) |
| 9.5 | Could not do another rep, might have added load |
| 9 | 1 rep in reserve |
| 8 | 2 reps in reserve |
| 7 | 3 reps in reserve |
| 5–6 | 4–6 reps in reserve; warm-up territory |

Zourdos and colleagues validated this against barbell velocity, finding a strong inverse relationship between average concentric velocity and RPE/RIR in both experienced (r = −0.88, p < 0.001) and novice squatters (r = −0.77, p = 0.001). They also found experienced lifters produced slower velocities and reported higher RPE at 1RM than novices — evidence of better efficiency at high intensities, and of the calibration problem in novices.

Practical use: prescribe **load by RPE target** ("work up to a set of 5 at RPE 8") rather than by fixed percentage, or use a hybrid ("75% for 5 sets of 5, adjusted to stay at RPE 7–8"). Reliability improves with experience; novices should be given percentages or fixed loads with occasional calibration sets to failure.

### Velocity-based training (VBT)

A linear position transducer or accelerometer measures bar velocity. Because velocity at a given percentage of 1RM is highly reproducible within an individual, velocity can be used to:

- **Estimate daily 1RM** from the velocity of a submaximal warm-up (load–velocity profiling).
- **Autoregulate load** — select the load that hits a target velocity today.
- **Set velocity-loss cut-offs** — terminate the set when velocity drops by e.g. 20% from the fastest rep, as a proxy for accumulated fatigue and proximity to failure.
- **Provide feedback** — real-time velocity display measurably increases intent and output.

Caveats: velocity–load relationships are exercise-specific and individual-specific, requiring calibration; equipment cost and reliability vary widely between devices; and the evidence that VBT-guided training outperforms well-executed RPE-guided training is limited. It is a useful tool with a real cost, most justified in strength and power sport.

### Readiness-based autoregulation

Simpler forms: adjusting session volume based on a brief readiness questionnaire (sleep, soreness, mood, stress, motivation), or on warm-up performance. The evidence base is modest but the practice is low-cost and sensible. HRV-guided training has shown some benefit in endurance contexts; the resistance-training evidence is thinner and day-to-day HRV is noisy in a way that invites over-interpretation.

## 6. Deloads

A **deload** is a planned reduction in training stress to allow accumulated fatigue to dissipate so that adaptation can be expressed. The classic implementation reduces **volume** (e.g. half the sets) while maintaining or slightly reducing **intensity**, for one week.

Honest statement of evidence: **direct experimental evidence on deload frequency and structure is weak.** Very few trials compare deload schemes; the practice rests mostly on fitness–fatigue theory and coaching experience. What is well supported is that accumulated fatigue masks fitness, and that periods of reduced training do not cause meaningful detraining over a week or two.

Practical approaches:

- **Scheduled**: every 4–8 weeks, with more advanced/higher-volume trainees needing them more often. Simple and reliable.
- **Autoregulated**: deload when two or more indicators appear — performance decrement across sessions, persistent joint discomfort, disrupted sleep, elevated resting heart rate, motivation loss, unusually high RPE at usual loads.
- **Built-in**: some programmes avoid discrete deloads entirely by keeping average intensity moderate and running planned light days, so fatigue never accumulates to the point requiring one.

What a deload should not be: complete rest for a week (unnecessary, and disruptive to habit), or a week of the same volume at lighter load (that reduces stimulus without much reducing fatigue, since volume drives most fatigue).

## 7. Warm-up and mobility

### Warm-up

A defensible general warm-up sequence:

1. **5–10 min of light general activity** — raises muscle temperature, which improves enzyme kinetics, conduction velocity and compliance. This is the part with the clearest physiological rationale.
2. **Dynamic mobility through the ranges to be used** — not held stretches. Leg swings, hip circles, thoracic rotations, band pull-aparts.
3. **Specific warm-up sets** — the working exercise at ascending loads. For a heavy compound, something like empty bar × 8, 40% × 5, 60% × 3, 75% × 2, 85% × 1, then working sets. This serves motor pattern rehearsal as much as tissue preparation.
4. **Optional potentiation work** for power athletes — jumps or heavy singles before speed work.

**Static stretching before training** transiently reduces force and power output (the effect is small, roughly 1–5%, and largest with holds over 60 s). It is not harmful but it is not the right tool immediately pre-lift. Save it for after training or separate sessions.

The **RAMP** framework (Raise, Activate, Mobilise, Potentiate) is a useful organising structure and is standard in the NSCA-influenced coaching literature.

### Mobility

Distinguish:
- **Flexibility** — passive range available at a joint.
- **Mobility** — actively controllable range.
- **Stability** — ability to control position under load.

Most lifting "mobility limitations" are one of three things: genuine tissue length restriction (least common), motor control limitation (common — the range exists but is not controlled), or **bony/anthropometric constraint** (common and *not* trainable — hip socket depth and orientation, femoral neck angle, femur-to-torso ratio; see file `04`).

Effective mobility work is loaded work at end range. Full-ROM resistance training is itself an excellent mobility intervention and there is good evidence that it improves flexibility comparably to stretching. If a specific range is required (deep squat, overhead position), train the specific position under load rather than accumulating unloaded stretch time.

## 8. Worked programme 1 — the beginner

**Person:** 28-year-old, no meaningful training history, 3 days/week available, commercial gym access, no injuries, goal "get stronger and look better", not in an energy deficit.

**Reasoning:**
- Training age is zero, so **technical proficiency and frequency of practice matter more than volume**. Full body 3×/week gives each pattern three exposures per week.
- Novices progress session-to-session; **linear load progression** is appropriate and self-regulating.
- Volume is deliberately at the low end of the landmarks — a beginner adapts to very little, and starting low leaves progression room for months.
- Effort target is conservative (2–3 RIR) because novices cannot judge RIR accurately and because technique degrades near failure.
- Exercise selection favours movements that are easy to learn and easy to load in small increments.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × Reps | Effort | Rest |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Goblet or barbell back squat | 3 × 5–8 | 2–3 RIR | 3 min |
| | Bench press (or dumbbell press) | 3 × 5–8 | 2–3 RIR | 3 min |
| | Chest-supported row | 3 × 8–12 | 2 RIR | 2 min |
| | Romanian deadlift | 2 × 8–10 | 3 RIR | 2 min |
| | Plank or Pallof press | 2 × 30–45 s | — | 1 min |
| **B** | Romanian deadlift or trap-bar deadlift | 3 × 5 | 3 RIR | 3 min |
| | Overhead press | 3 × 6–8 | 2 RIR | 2–3 min |
| | Lat pulldown or assisted pull-up | 3 × 8–12 | 2 RIR | 2 min |
| | Leg press or split squat | 2 × 10–12 | 2 RIR | 2 min |
| | Face pull | 2 × 15 | 3 RIR | 1 min |
| **C** | Back squat | 3 × 5–8 | 2–3 RIR | 3 min |
| | Incline dumbbell press | 3 × 8–12 | 2 RIR | 2 min |
| | Single-arm dumbbell row | 3 × 8–12 | 2 RIR | 2 min |
| | Hip thrust or back extension | 2 × 10–12 | 2 RIR | 2 min |
| | Farmer's carry | 3 × 30 m | — | 90 s |

Run A–B–C on non-consecutive days. Add 2.5 kg to lower-body lifts and 1–2.5 kg to upper-body lifts whenever all sets hit the top of the rep range at the prescribed RIR. When a lift stalls twice, reduce by 10% and rebuild. Add 20–30 min of easy cardio on two off days to meet the WHO aerobic minimum.

**What this programme is not doing:** it is not chasing volume, not using specialised techniques, and not periodising. All of that is premature. It is buying twelve to twenty-four months of straightforward linear progress and technical competence.

## 9. Worked programme 2 — the intermediate, hypertrophy-focused

**Person:** 32-year-old, three years of consistent training, 4 days/week, goal is muscle gain with strength maintained, small energy surplus.

**Reasoning:**
- Linear session-to-session progression has ended; progression is now weekly and volume-driven.
- **Upper/lower ×2** delivers the twice-weekly frequency finding at a sustainable session count.
- Volume sits mid-range in the landmarks, with room to add.
- Each muscle gets **two distinct exercises at different joint angles** (section 6 of file `02`) rather than duplicates.
- Rest is 2–3 min on compounds per Schoenfeld 2016; shorter on isolation to control session length.
- Top set / back-off structure lets one heavy set drive strength while back-offs accumulate volume at lower fatigue cost.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × Reps | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Upper A** | Bench press | 1 × 5 top set, then 3 × 8 back-off (−12%) | 1 RIR / 2 RIR |
| | Weighted pull-up or lat pulldown | 4 × 6–10 | 1–2 RIR |
| | Seated dumbbell shoulder press | 3 × 8–12 | 1 RIR |
| | Chest-supported row | 3 × 10–12 | 1 RIR |
| | Cable lateral raise | 3 × 12–20 | 0–1 RIR |
| | Incline dumbbell curl | 3 × 10–15 | 0–1 RIR |
| | Overhead cable triceps extension | 3 × 10–15 | 0–1 RIR |
| **Lower A** | Back squat | 1 × 5 top set, then 3 × 8 back-off | 1 RIR / 2 RIR |
| | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8–10 | 2 RIR |
| | Leg press | 3 × 12–15 | 1 RIR |
| | Seated leg curl | 3 × 10–15 | 0–1 RIR |
| | Standing calf raise | 4 × 8–12 | 0–1 RIR |
| | Hanging leg raise | 3 × 10–15 | 1 RIR |
| **Upper B** | Overhead press | 4 × 5–8 | 1–2 RIR |
| | Barbell row | 4 × 8–10 | 1–2 RIR |
| | Incline dumbbell press | 3 × 10–12 | 1 RIR |
| | Single-arm lat pulldown | 3 × 12–15 | 0–1 RIR |
| | Reverse pec-deck / rear delt fly | 3 × 15–20 | 0–1 RIR |
| | EZ-bar curl | 3 × 8–12 | 0–1 RIR |
| | Dip or close-grip bench | 3 × 8–12 | 1 RIR |
| **Lower B** | Trap-bar or conventional deadlift | 3 × 4–6 | 2 RIR |
| | Bulgarian split squat | 3 × 8–12 each | 1 RIR |
| | Hip thrust | 3 × 10–12 | 1 RIR |
| | Leg extension | 3 × 12–20 | 0–1 RIR |
| | Seated calf raise | 4 × 12–20 | 0–1 RIR |

Approximate weekly hard sets: chest ~13, back ~17, quads ~13, hamstrings ~9, shoulders ~13 (plus indirect), biceps ~9 (plus indirect), triceps ~9 (plus indirect). All within the productive ranges in section 4.

**Progression:** double progression within each rep range; when the top of the range is achieved at target RIR across all sets, increase load. Every 5–6 weeks, add one set to the two muscle groups progressing slowest. Deload every 6–8 weeks by halving sets while keeping load.

## 10. Worked programme 3 — the strength-focused lifter

**Person:** 30-year-old, five years training, competing in an unequipped powerlifting meet in 16 weeks, 4 days/week, goal is maximal squat/bench/deadlift total, body mass held at the top of a weight class.

**Reasoning:**
- Strength is **load-specific and skill-specific** (file `02`, sections 10–11). Frequency of the competition lifts matters more than exercise variety.
- A **block structure** is genuinely justified here, because peaking is the goal and the competition date is fixed — the one context where periodisation evidence is strongest.
- Volume is concentrated early and traded for intensity later. Assistance work supports the competition lifts and is trimmed as the meet approaches.
- Autoregulation by RPE handles day-to-day variance; percentages provide the skeleton.

**Block 1, weeks 1–6 — accumulation.** Higher volume, moderate intensity (RPE 6–8, roughly 67–80% 1RM), 4–6 reps on competition lifts, substantial assistance work (paused variations, tempo work, front squats, close-grip bench, deficit deadlifts, rows, triceps, upper back). Competition squat 2×/week, bench 3×/week, deadlift 1–2×/week.

**Block 2, weeks 7–12 — intensification.** Volume falls ~25%, intensity rises (RPE 7.5–9, roughly 80–90% 1RM), 2–4 reps on competition lifts. Assistance narrows to variations closest to the competition lift. Introduce competition commands, competition stance and grip, and single-ply-free equipment exactly as it will be used on the day.

**Block 3, weeks 13–15 — realisation/peak.** Volume falls sharply. Heavy singles at RPE 8–9 in weeks 13–14 (openers and second attempts rehearsed), then a taper. Assistance work reduced to light upper-back and health-maintenance movements.

**Week 16 — meet week.** Two very light technical sessions early in the week, then rest. Opener should be a weight that can be hit for a smooth double on the worst possible day.

**Why this differs so much from programme 2:** the goal is a single expressed maximum on a specific date, not accumulated tissue. Almost every decision — low rep ranges, narrow exercise selection, high specificity, deliberate fatigue dissipation into the meet — follows from that.

## 11. Integrating cardiovascular training

Resistance-focused trainees frequently omit cardio entirely, which is a mistake: the mortality and cardiometabolic dose-response data (file `10`) are far stronger for aerobic fitness than for anything in a hypertrophy programme.

The **interference effect** is real but usually overstated for typical volumes. Practical mitigation:

- Separate modes by **≥6 hours** where possible, or place them on different days.
- Prefer **low-impact, low-eccentric modalities** (cycling, rowing, incline walking) over running if lower-body lifting is the priority; the eccentric loading of running competes directly with squat recovery.
- Keep most aerobic work at **low intensity** (below LT1) where the metabolic signalling conflict is smallest; add one or two short high-intensity sessions if VO2max improvement is a goal.
- The WHO 2020 minimum of 150–300 min/week of moderate activity is entirely compatible with a 4-day lifting programme.

## Open questions

- The **volume landmark table** (minimum effective dose / productive range) is a practitioner framework, not a meta-analytic output. The per-muscle numbers are convention and are marked `needs-verification` as quantitative claims.
- **Deload frequency and structure** have almost no direct experimental evidence. All recommendations here are reasoned from fitness–fatigue theory and coaching practice.
- Whether **VBT-guided training** outperforms well-executed RPE-guided training in matched conditions is not established.
- The magnitude of the **interference effect** at realistic recreational cardio volumes is smaller than commonly claimed but has not been well quantified in trained lifters.
