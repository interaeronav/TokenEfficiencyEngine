# Special populations and longevity — training across contexts

**Summary.** The menstrual cycle's effect on exercise performance is, in the largest meta-analysis to date, trivial — a median effect size of −0.06 — and the guidance that follows is individualisation, not phase-based programming. Pregnancy guidelines have moved decisively from restriction toward encouragement. Resistance training is the highest-value single intervention for older adults, and youth resistance training is endorsed by every major professional body when appropriately supervised. The longevity literature is where exercise science produces its largest effect sizes: low cardiorespiratory fitness carried an adjusted hazard ratio of 5.04 for all-cause mortality relative to elite fitness, larger than smoking, diabetes or coronary artery disease in the same cohort.

> ⚠️ This file gives **general guidance only**. Anyone who is pregnant or postpartum, over 65 and previously sedentary, under 18, or living with a diagnosed medical condition should have their exercise programme reviewed by an appropriately qualified clinician. Several conditions listed here have absolute or relative contraindications that are outside the scope of a reference document.

## 1. Women's physiology and the menstrual cycle

### What the evidence actually shows

**McNulty and colleagues (2020)** conducted the definitive systematic review and Bayesian meta-analysis: 78 included studies, 51 in the initial pairwise meta-analysis, 73 in a network meta-analysis.

- Comparing the early follicular phase against all other phases combined: a **trivial effect** for both endurance- and strength-based outcomes, with the median pooled effect size **ES₀.₅ = −0.06 (95% CrI −0.16 to 0.04)** indicating slightly reduced performance in the early follicular phase.
- The largest identified effect in the network analysis was between early and late follicular phases: **ES₀.₅ = −0.14 (95% CrI −0.26 to −0.03)**.
- SUCRA values: early follicular 30% (most likely to be the poorest phase), with all other phases between 53% and 55%.
- **Evidence quality was rated "low" (42%).**

The authors' conclusion is worth quoting in effect: because the effect size is trivial, between-study variation is large, and many included studies are of poor quality, **general guidelines on performance across the menstrual cycle cannot be formed; a personalised approach based on each individual's own response is recommended.**

This matters because "cycle syncing" — programming training phases to menstrual phases — is heavily marketed and is not supported by the evidence at population level. What *is* supported: individual women vary considerably, some experience genuinely disruptive symptoms (dysmenorrhoea, heavy bleeding, premenstrual symptoms), and tracking one's own response is sensible.

Note also the broader methodological problem, well documented in the field: **women are substantially under-represented in sports science research**, and many studies that do include women fail to control for or report cycle phase or hormonal contraceptive use.

### Practical points

- **Strength and hypertrophy training does not need phase adjustment** by default. Train consistently; adjust when symptoms warrant.
- **Iron status** deserves attention in menstruating women, particularly endurance athletes (file `05`).
- **Hormonal contraceptives** alter the hormonal environment; a companion meta-analysis by the same group examined their effects on performance and likewise found small effects. Individual response varies.
- **Amenorrhoea is never a normal training adaptation** and warrants medical assessment — see REDs in file `06`.
- **Bone health** is a specific concern where low energy availability and menstrual dysfunction coexist; peak bone mass is accrued by the late twenties and losses in this window may not be recoverable.
- **Perimenopause and menopause** bring declining oestrogen, accelerated bone mineral density loss, and changes in body composition and thermoregulation. Resistance training and adequate protein become more important, not less. Hormone therapy is a clinical decision outside this document's scope.
- Women respond to resistance training with similar *relative* hypertrophy and strength gains to men; the absolute gains are smaller because of lower starting lean mass. Women tend to show greater fatigue resistance at matched relative intensity and may recover faster between sets.

## 2. Pregnancy and postpartum

The **2019 Canadian Guideline for Physical Activity throughout Pregnancy** (Mottola et al., 2018) is a rigorously constructed, GRADE-assessed guideline. Its headline finding: **the benefits of prenatal physical activity are moderate and no harms were identified.**

The guideline's core recommendations (as commonly summarised; verify against the full document for clinical use):

- **All women without contraindication should be physically active throughout pregnancy.**
- Accumulate at least **150 minutes of moderate-intensity physical activity per week**, spread over at least three days, with daily activity encouraged.
- Incorporate **a variety of aerobic and resistance activities**; adding yoga and/or gentle stretching may be beneficial.
- **Pelvic floor muscle training** may reduce the risk of urinary incontinence.
- **Avoid the supine position** for prolonged periods in later pregnancy if it produces symptoms (dizziness, nausea, malaise).
- Women who feel light-headed, nauseated or unwell when exercising flat on their back should modify position.

**Contraindications** exist — absolute (including ruptured membranes, persistent bleeding, placenta praevia after 28 weeks, pre-eclampsia, incompetent cervix, uncontrolled hypertension or thyroid disease) and relative. **Clinical clearance is required**, and this is precisely the situation for which the disclaimer at the top of this file exists.

**Practical training adjustments:**
- Resistance training can generally continue, with load and exercise selection adjusted. Prone and supine positions become impractical; heavy Valsalva bracing is usually reduced.
- Balance changes as the centre of mass shifts; exercises with fall risk should be substituted.
- Relaxin increases ligamentous laxity; the practical implication is caution at end range rather than avoidance of training.
- Thermoregulation matters — avoid exercising in high heat and humidity, particularly in the first trimester. **[NA]** Directly relevant in Namibian summer conditions.
- **Perceived exertion (or the talk test) is a better intensity guide than heart rate**, since resting and exercising heart rate change substantially in pregnancy.

**Postpartum:** return to activity should be gradual and guided by recovery, delivery mode and complications. Specific considerations include pelvic floor recovery (pelvic floor physiotherapy assessment is standard practice in several countries and is worth seeking), diastasis recti management, and — for those breastfeeding — increased energy and fluid requirements. There is **no evidence that moderate exercise impairs milk supply or infant growth**.

## 3. Older adults and sarcopenia

**Sarcopenia** is the age-related loss of skeletal muscle mass, strength and function. It typically begins around age 30 and accelerates after 60; **dynapenia** (strength loss) proceeds faster than mass loss, because neural drive and muscle quality decline alongside quantity. Loss is disproportionately in Type II fibres, which is why power declines faster than strength.

**Consequences:** frailty, falls and fractures, loss of independence, reduced metabolic rate, insulin resistance, and — through the mechanisms in section 7 — substantially elevated mortality.

**Why resistance training is the single highest-value intervention:**

1. It is the only intervention that directly and reliably reverses the mechanism. Older adults retain the capacity to hypertrophy and to gain strength; the response is attenuated relative to young adults but is not absent, and strength gains in particular are often large.
2. The functional carry-over is direct — chair rise, stair climbing, gait speed, balance, fall risk.
3. It improves bone mineral density, which no medication does without side effects and which walking alone does not adequately achieve.
4. Its effects on insulin sensitivity, blood pressure and body composition compound the cardiometabolic benefit.
5. The mortality association (Momma 2022) is independent of aerobic activity.

**Programming adjustments:**
- **Do not under-load.** The most common error is prescribing loads too light to be a stimulus. Older adults tolerate and require meaningful loading; the "light weights for seniors" convention is a failure of prescription.
- **Emphasise power as well as strength.** Fast concentric intent (with controlled eccentric) addresses the Type II and rate-of-force-development deficit that predicts falls.
- **Protein requirements are higher** because of anabolic resistance — commonly cited as **1.2–1.6 g/kg/day** at minimum, with per-meal doses toward 40 g rather than 20 g (file `05`).
- **Vitamin D status** matters for muscle function and falls; test rather than assume.
- **Longer warm-ups, more gradual progression, more attention to joint tolerance.** Recovery between sessions is typically longer.
- **Balance and gait training** should be included explicitly.
- **Screen for medical contraindications and medication effects** (beta-blockers blunt heart rate response; several medications affect balance and blood pressure).

## 4. Adolescents

The **2014 International Consensus position statement on youth resistance training** (Lloyd et al., BJSM), adapted from the UK Strength and Conditioning Association statement and endorsed by leading professional organisations in sports medicine, exercise science and paediatrics, together with the NSCA's own updated position statement (Faigenbaum et al., 2009), establishes the consensus.

**The myths that the evidence does not support:**
- **"Resistance training stunts growth."** There is no evidence for this. The concern originated from epiphyseal plate injury reports, which were associated with unsupervised maximal lifting and poor technique, not with appropriately supervised training.
- **"Children cannot gain strength before puberty."** They can, and do — largely through neural adaptation, since the hormonal environment limits hypertrophy pre-puberty.
- **"Resistance training is more dangerous than sport."** Injury rates in supervised youth resistance training are lower than in most competitive youth sports.

**Consensus positions:**
- Appropriately prescribed and supervised resistance training offers unique benefits to children and adolescents: increased strength and power, improved motor skill, improved bone mineral density, reduced sports injury risk, improved body composition, and improved psychosocial wellbeing.
- Qualified supervision and instruction are essential, and technique should be established before load is added.
- Programmes should be individualised, developmentally appropriate and enjoyable, and should form part of a varied physical activity pattern.
- The NSCA distinguishes **children** (pre-adolescent, roughly up to 11 in girls and 13 in boys, Tanner stages 1–2) from **adolescents** (girls 12–18, boys 14–18, Tanner stages 3–4), with programming differences accordingly.

**Practical points:** emphasise movement competence and variety over load; avoid maximal single-repetition testing in young children; avoid early sport specialisation (associated with overuse injury and burnout); expect the "adolescent awkwardness" phase during rapid growth, when coordination temporarily regresses and injury risk (particularly to growth plates and apophyses) rises.

## 5. Common conditions — general guidance level

> ⚠️ Each of these requires individual clinical assessment. What follows is orientation, not a prescription.

### Hypertension

Aerobic training reliably lowers blood pressure. The 2026 network meta-analysis of 31 trials (1 345 participants) found reductions in **24-hour ambulatory systolic pressure of −6.18 mmHg for combined training** (95% CrI −11.45 to −1.21), **−4.73 mmHg for aerobic training** (−7.53 to −2.01) and **−5.71 mmHg for HIIT** (−11.31 to −0.002), with corresponding diastolic reductions. Evidence for dynamic and isometric resistance training on ambulatory pressure remained uncertain in that analysis.

**Practical points:** resistance training is generally safe in controlled hypertension but the Valsalva manoeuvre produces very large transient pressure spikes; heavy maximal lifting with prolonged breath-holding is the specific concern. Uncontrolled hypertension requires medical management before heavy resistance training. Beta-blockers blunt exercise heart rate, making RPE the appropriate intensity guide.

### Type 2 diabetes

The **ACSM/ADA joint position statement (Colberg et al., 2010)** establishes that regular physical activity improves blood glucose control and can prevent or delay T2DM, while positively affecting lipids, blood pressure, cardiovascular events, mortality and quality of life. **Structured interventions combining physical activity and modest weight loss have lowered T2DM risk by up to 58% in high-risk populations** — an effect size that exceeds metformin in the same trials.

Most benefits are realised through acute and chronic improvements in insulin action, achieved with **both aerobic and resistance training**; the combination is superior to either alone.

**Exercise and Sport Science Australia (Hordern et al., 2012)** gives a specific prescription: **at least 210 min/week of moderate-intensity or 125 min/week of vigorous-intensity exercise, with no more than two consecutive days without training**, including **two or more resistance sessions per week (2–4 sets of 8–10 repetitions)** within that total. The "no more than two consecutive days" rule reflects the fact that the insulin-sensitising effect of a single session lasts roughly 24–72 hours.

**Practical points:** hypoglycaemia risk in those on insulin or sulfonylureas requires glucose monitoring and possibly medication or carbohydrate adjustment; peripheral neuropathy requires foot care and may favour non-weight-bearing modalities; proliferative retinopathy is a relative contraindication to heavy resistance training and Valsalva; autonomic neuropathy blunts the heart rate response.

### Obesity

Exercise alone is a weak weight-loss intervention (file `05`) but a strong intervention for **weight maintenance**, cardiometabolic risk, fitness and function — and cardiorespiratory fitness modifies mortality risk substantially at any body mass (section 7).

**Practical points:** start with modalities tolerable to joints (cycling, water-based, incline walking, machines); resistance training helps preserve lean mass during weight loss and improves function disproportionately; avoid framing exercise primarily as a means of energy expenditure, which sets up disappointment when the arithmetic underdelivers.

### Chronic low back pain

The evidence base is clear on several points that contradict common practice:

- **Exercise is effective** for chronic non-specific low back pain, and **no specific exercise modality has demonstrated superiority** over others. Motor control exercise, general strengthening, Pilates, yoga and aerobic exercise all produce broadly similar modest improvements. Adherence and graded exposure matter more than modality.
- **Imaging findings correlate poorly with pain.** Disc degeneration, bulges and annular fissures are highly prevalent in asymptomatic people and increase with age. Routine imaging for non-specific low back pain is not recommended and can worsen outcomes by increasing fear and catastrophising.
- **Rest is not the treatment.** Staying active is recommended by essentially all current guidelines.
- **Psychosocial factors** — fear-avoidance, catastrophising, low self-efficacy, work dissatisfaction, depression — predict chronicity better than structural findings. This is the "yellow flags" framework, and it is why the "O" (Optimism) in PEACE & LOVE (file `09`) is not filler.
- **Resistance training, including loaded spinal movement, is generally beneficial** rather than dangerous. Progressive, graded exposure to the movements a person fears is a core therapeutic principle.

> ⚠️ Red flags requiring urgent medical assessment: new bowel or bladder dysfunction, saddle anaesthesia, progressive neurological deficit, unexplained weight loss, fever, history of cancer, significant trauma, or age of onset under 20 or over 55 with new symptoms.

## 6. Training in heat and at altitude

### Heat

**Physiological challenge.** Exercise in heat imposes competing demands: skin blood flow for heat dissipation competes with muscle blood flow for oxygen delivery. Consequences include elevated heart rate at a given workload (cardiovascular drift), reduced stroke volume, reduced VO2max, higher perceived exertion, higher glycogen use, and accelerated fluid and electrolyte loss.

**Heat acclimation** is the most effective countermeasure. **Chalmers and colleagues (2014)** systematically reviewed short-term heat acclimation (≤7 exposures) and found that **aerobic-based tests of performance benefit**, while **peak anaerobic power efforts have not been demonstrated to improve**. A mixed high-intensity protocol may require only about five 60-minute sessions to improve aerobic performance in trained athletes, though the review flagged moderate risk of bias and only two RCTs among eight included studies.

Adaptations to heat acclimation: **plasma volume expansion** (rapid, within days), earlier onset of sweating, higher sweat rate, reduced sweat sodium concentration, lower core temperature and heart rate at a given workload, and improved thermal comfort. Most adaptations appear within 7–14 days and decay within roughly 1–3 weeks without exposure.

Note that **passive post-exercise heat exposure** (sauna, hot water immersion) as a substitute for active acclimation is not well supported: Solomon and Laye (2025) meta-analysed 10 studies (199 participants) and found a **trivial effect on performance in hot conditions (ratio of means 1.04, 95% CI 0.94–1.15, p = 0.46)**, with low-to-very-low GRADE certainty across all outcomes.

**Practical heat guidance:**
- Train in the coolest parts of the day where possible; shift long sessions to early morning.
- Measure individual sweat rate by weighing before and after (file `05`); do not rely on thirst alone in prolonged work, and do not overdrink.
- Replace sodium in prolonged sessions with high sweat losses.
- Use pre-cooling (cold fluid ingestion, ice slurry, cooling garments) for competition in heat — the evidence for ice slurry ingestion in particular is reasonable.
- Wear light, loose, light-coloured clothing; manage solar load with shade and timing.
- Recognise heat illness: heat exhaustion (weakness, nausea, heavy sweating, headache, tachycardia, core temperature usually <40°C) and **heat stroke** (core temperature ≥40°C with central nervous system dysfunction — confusion, ataxia, collapse).

> ⚠️ **Exertional heat stroke is a medical emergency.** The single most important determinant of survival is time to cooling. Cold water immersion on site, before transport, is the recommended first-line treatment. Do not wait for an ambulance to begin cooling.

**[NA]** Namibian conditions vary sharply by region and season. The interior (Windhoek, ~1 650 m) is hot and very dry in summer with large diurnal ranges; the Namib and Kalahari reach extreme daytime temperatures with negligible humidity; the coast (Walvis Bay, Swakopmund) is cool and foggy under the Benguela influence and presents an entirely different problem. **The low humidity of the interior causes systematic underestimation of sweat losses**, because sweat evaporates before it is perceived. Gulf conditions differ again: comparable heat with *high* humidity, which impairs evaporative cooling and is more dangerous at the same air temperature.

### Altitude

**Physiological challenge.** Reduced barometric pressure lowers the partial pressure of inspired oxygen, reducing arterial oxygen saturation and therefore VO2max. VO2max begins to decline measurably above roughly 1 200–1 500 m and falls progressively — commonly cited as roughly 6–8% per 1 000 m above that threshold, though individual variation is large.

**Acute responses:** hyperventilation, increased heart rate at a given workload, diuresis and haemoconcentration, and reduced sleep quality. **Acute mountain sickness** (headache, nausea, fatigue, dizziness, insomnia) affects a substantial fraction of people ascending rapidly above ~2 500 m.

**Acclimatisation:** ventilatory acclimatisation over days; increased erythropoietin within hours, with meaningful haemoglobin mass increase requiring roughly 3–4 weeks of adequate exposure; increased 2,3-DPG; capillary and mitochondrial changes with longer exposure. Adequate **iron status is essential** for the erythropoietic response, and iron supplementation is standard practice in altitude camps under medical supervision.

**Altitude training models:**
- **Live high–train high (LHTH).** Simple; the drawback is that training intensity is compromised at altitude.
- **Live high–train low (LHTL).** The most supported model: sleep and live at altitude (or in normobaric hypoxia), train near sea level to preserve intensity. A 2026 meta-analysis of cycling studies (6 of 34 identified studies met inclusion criteria) found improvements in red cell mass, haemoglobin concentration and mass, reticulocyte count, VO2max and time-trial performance, with a **significant positive effect on haemoglobin concentration and mass of 0.82% (95% CL 0.62–1.03%)**. However, studies with lower PEDro scores reported non-significant or modest enhancements, and the authors urge caution in interpretation given influence and bias analyses.
- **Intermittent hypoxic training / hypoxic exposure at rest.** Weaker evidence.

**Practical points:** competing at altitude without acclimatisation degrades endurance performance substantially, and the first 1–3 days are typically the worst; sprint and power events are less affected (and throwing and sprinting events are actually aided by reduced air density). Hydration requirements increase (respiratory water loss plus altitude diuresis). Carbohydrate becomes a preferred fuel at altitude.

**[NA]** Windhoek sits at roughly 1 650 m. This is high enough to measurably reduce VO2max relative to sea level and to require a short adjustment period for visiting athletes, but far below the range where altitude training adaptations are meaningfully induced (generally ≥2 000–2 500 m for LHTL protocols). Athletes moving between Windhoek and the coast will notice the difference in both directions.

## 7. The longevity literature

This is where the evidence for exercise produces its largest and most robust effect sizes.

### Cardiorespiratory fitness

**Mandsager and colleagues (2018)** retrospectively studied **122 007 patients** referred for symptom-limited exercise treadmill testing at a tertiary centre (1991–2014), with **13 637 deaths over 1.1 million person-years** and a median 8.4 years of follow-up. Patients were stratified into age- and sex-matched fitness percentile groups.

- **Risk-adjusted all-cause mortality was inversely proportional to cardiorespiratory fitness and lowest in elite performers** (elite vs low: adjusted **HR 0.20**, 95% CI 0.16–0.24, p < 0.001).
- **Elite vs high**: HR 0.77 (0.63–0.95, p = 0.02) — even at the top of the distribution, more was better.
- **Low vs elite: adjusted HR 5.04** (4.10–6.20, p < 0.001).
- Below average vs above average: HR 1.41 (1.34–1.49).
- **For comparison, in the same cohort**: coronary artery disease HR 1.29 (1.24–1.35), smoking HR 1.41 (1.36–1.46), diabetes HR 1.40 (1.34–1.46).
- Benefit of elite over high performance persisted in patients ≥70 years (HR 0.71, 0.52–0.98) and in those with hypertension (HR 0.70, 0.50–0.99).
- **"Cardiorespiratory fitness is inversely associated with long-term mortality with no observed upper limit of benefit."**

The comparison to traditional risk factors is the striking part. Being in the lowest fitness quartile was associated with a larger mortality hazard than having coronary artery disease, smoking, or diabetes. Note the caveats: this is an observational cohort of patients referred for treadmill testing (a clinical population, not the general public), and reverse causation — subclinical disease reducing both fitness and survival — cannot be excluded, though the analysis adjusted extensively.

### Muscular strength and grip

**Leong and colleagues (2015)**, the PURE study: **139 691 participants** across 17 countries of varying income, median follow-up 4.0 years, 3 379 deaths.

- Per **5 kg reduction in grip strength**: all-cause mortality **HR 1.16** (95% CI 1.13–1.20, p < 0.0001); cardiovascular mortality 1.17 (1.11–1.24); non-cardiovascular mortality 1.17 (1.12–1.21); myocardial infarction 1.07 (1.02–1.11); stroke 1.09 (1.05–1.15).
- **Grip strength was a stronger predictor of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality than systolic blood pressure.**
- The associations were consistent across country-income strata (with exceptions for cancer and respiratory admissions).
- No significant association with incident diabetes, pneumonia/COPD admission, fall injury or fracture.

An important honest caveat, which the authors state: **"Further research is needed to identify determinants of muscular strength and to test whether improvement in strength reduces mortality."** Grip strength is a marker of general physiological reserve, and the fact that it predicts death does not establish that training grip prevents death.

**Momma and colleagues (2022)** provide the interventional-exposure counterpart, discussed in file `09`: muscle-strengthening activities associated with **10–17% lower risk** of all-cause mortality, CVD, total cancer, diabetes and lung cancer, independent of aerobic activity, with a **J-shaped curve peaking at roughly 30–60 min/week**.

### The dose-response curve

From **Arem and colleagues (2015)**, detailed in file `09`: relative to no leisure-time activity, hazard ratios of 0.80 below the guideline minimum, 0.69 at 1–2×, 0.63 at 2–3×, 0.61 at 3–5×, and 0.69 at ≥10× — **no evidence of harm at very high volumes.**

The shape of this curve is the single most policy-relevant fact in the domain: **the marginal return on the first hour of weekly activity is enormous, and it diminishes steeply.** Moving a sedentary person to any activity is worth vastly more than moving an active person to more activity.

### Honest treatment of popular longevity claims

The longevity field has attracted substantial commercial interest, and several widely promoted claims outrun their evidence.

| Claim | Honest assessment |
|---|---|
| **"VO2max is the single best predictor of longevity"** | Well supported as an *association* (Mandsager 2018). But causality is not established by observational data, and improving VO2max by training has never been shown in an RCT to reduce mortality — such a trial would be enormous and long |
| **"Zone 2 training is essential for mitochondrial health"** | The physiology (low-intensity work at LT1 developing fat oxidation and mitochondrial density) is sound. The claim that a specific zone is uniquely necessary, and the very precise weekly prescriptions attached to it, are extrapolation. Higher-intensity work also drives mitochondrial biogenesis (file `01`) |
| **"Strength training prevents death"** | Momma 2022 shows a 10–17% association. That is real and useful. It is not the same as prevention, and the J-shaped curve means the maximum benefit occurs at a surprisingly modest 30–60 min/week |
| **"Grip strength is a longevity dial you can turn"** | Leong 2015 explicitly cautions that whether *improving* strength reduces mortality is untested. Grip strength is more plausibly a marker of overall physiological reserve than a lever |
| **Fasting, autophagy and longevity** | Compelling animal data across many species; human evidence for extended lifespan is essentially absent. Human trials show metabolic effects; the TREAT trial (file `05`) showed no body-composition advantage and a possible lean-mass cost |
| **Rapamycin, metformin, NAD+ precursors, senolytics** | Genuinely interesting research programmes with animal data. Human efficacy for lifespan or healthspan is unproven; several (metformin in particular) may **blunt exercise adaptations**. These are drugs with side effects, not supplements |
| **Blue Zones** | The observational methodology has come under serious criticism, including analyses attributing much of the reported longevity clustering to poor birth-record keeping and pension fraud. Treat the specific claims with caution; the general advice (move a lot, eat mostly plants, maintain social connection) is uncontroversial regardless |
| **Cold plunges for longevity** | Extrapolated from hormesis and brown adipose tissue research. There is no human longevity evidence. As file `09` documents, routine post-training cold immersion blunts hypertrophy adaptation |
| **Biological age tests (epigenetic clocks)** | Scientifically interesting; individual-level reliability and actionability are poor. Consumer tests give results that fluctuate substantially and have not been shown to guide useful decisions |

**What the evidence does robustly support for healthspan:** don't smoke; maintain cardiorespiratory fitness; maintain muscle mass and strength; sleep adequately; maintain a healthy body composition; eat a predominantly whole-food, plant-forward diet with adequate protein; limit alcohol; manage blood pressure, lipids and glucose; maintain social connection; and get the age-appropriate medical screening. Everything else in the longevity market is a rounding error by comparison.
