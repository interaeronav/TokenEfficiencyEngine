# Hypertrophy and strength science — what the evidence actually says

**Summary.** Mechanical tension imposed on muscle fibres by high-threshold motor unit recruitment is the primary driver of hypertrophy; metabolic stress and muscle damage, once treated as co-equal mechanisms, have been substantially demoted. Hypertrophy occurs across a wide load range (roughly 30–85% 1RM) provided sets are taken close enough to failure, while maximal strength is far more load-specific. Volume shows a graded dose-response with diminishing and eventually negative returns, but the top of the useful range is genuinely uncertain and highly individual. Frequency matters mainly as a way to distribute volume. Periodisation models produce small and inconsistent differences once volume and intensity are equated. Realistic rates of muscle gain are far slower than the fitness industry implies.

## 1. The mechanisms of hypertrophy

Schoenfeld's influential 2010 review proposed three mechanisms: **mechanical tension**, **metabolic stress** and **muscle damage**. Fifteen years of subsequent work has substantially reordered them.

### Mechanical tension — primary

Force applied to muscle fibres is transduced into biochemical signalling. Mechanosensors — integrins at costameres, focal adhesion kinase, mechanically sensitive phospholipase D producing phosphatidic acid, and stretch-activated channels — converge on **mTORC1**, which upregulates translation initiation (via p70S6K and 4E-BP1) and therefore muscle protein synthesis. Satellite cells are activated, proliferate and donate myonuclei, expanding the transcriptional capacity of the fibre and supporting further growth.

Two properties of tension matter:

1. **It must reach the fibre.** A fibre that is not recruited experiences essentially no active tension. This is why proximity to failure or heavy load — either of which ensures high-threshold recruitment — is necessary.
2. **Tension per fibre falls as shortening velocity rises** (the force–velocity relationship). A fast, explosive rep at a light load produces less tension per recruited fibre than a slow, grinding rep at the same load. This is the mechanistic argument for why the last few reps of a set — slow, maximally recruited, high tension per fibre — are the productive ones.

### Metabolic stress — demoted

Accumulation of lactate, H⁺, Pᵢ and the accompanying cell swelling was proposed to independently stimulate growth. The evidence has weakened. Blood-flow-restriction training does produce hypertrophy at very low loads, which is often cited for this mechanism — but the most parsimonious explanation is that ischaemia accelerates fatigue of low-threshold fibres, forcing high-threshold recruitment at low load. That is a tension mechanism operating through a metabolic route, not an independent pathway. The honest current position: metabolic stress probably contributes mainly by *causing* recruitment, and any additional independent effect is small.

### Muscle damage — largely dismissed

**Damas and colleagues (2016)** is the decisive study. Ten young men were tracked through 10 weeks of resistance training with biopsies at week 1, week 3 and week 10. Muscle damage (Z-band streaming) was highest in week 1, attenuated by week 3, and minimal by week 10. Integrated myofibrillar protein synthesis was *highest* in week 1 — when damage was highest and hypertrophy was zero — and the week-1 response did **not** correlate with subsequent growth. At weeks 3 and 10, once damage had attenuated, the protein synthesis response correlated strongly with hypertrophy (r ≈ 0.9, p < 0.04).

The interpretation: the early protein synthesis response is largely **repair**, not growth. Damage is a cost of unaccustomed training, not a mechanism of adaptation. Programming for soreness is programming for repair expenditure.

## 2. Effective reps and proximity to failure

The **effective reps** (or "stimulating reps") model proposes that only the reps performed under conditions of near-full motor unit recruitment and low shortening velocity — roughly the last five before failure — contribute meaningfully to the hypertrophic stimulus.

The model is a useful heuristic and is consistent with recruitment physiology, but it should not be treated as established quantitative fact. Its predictions have been only partially tested, the exact number of "effective" reps is not measured, and it fails to explain why heavy sets far from failure still build strength effectively.

What is better established is the empirical picture from **Grgic and colleagues (2022)**, a meta-analysis of 15 studies:

- Strength: ES −0.09 (95% CI −0.22 to 0.05) — no significant difference between failure and non-failure training.
- Hypertrophy: ES 0.22 (95% CI −0.11 to 0.55) — no significant difference overall.
- In **volume-non-equated** studies, non-failure training significantly favoured strength (ES −0.32, 95% CI −0.57 to −0.07) — because stopping short allows more total sets.
- In the **resistance-trained subgroup**, failure training significantly favoured hypertrophy, but only slightly (ES 0.15, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.26).

Practical reading: training to momentary failure is neither necessary nor harmful, but it is expensive in fatigue and recovery. Working within roughly **0–3 reps in reserve (RIR)** on most sets captures nearly all of the stimulus at much lower fatigue cost. Failure is more defensible on isolation and machine work (low systemic cost, low technical risk) than on heavy compound lifts, where it degrades technique, spikes recovery demand, and adds injury exposure for little marginal gain.

A caveat worth stating: most trainees are poor at estimating RIR, systematically overestimating how close to failure they are. Novices in particular routinely stop 5+ reps short while reporting 1–2 RIR. Calibrating this — occasionally taking a set to genuine failure to recalibrate the scale — is a real coaching problem, discussed in file `03`.

## 3. Volume and the dose-response relationship

**Schoenfeld, Ogborn and Krieger (2017)** meta-regressed 34 treatment groups from 15 studies:

- Weekly sets as a continuous predictor: significant effect on muscle size (p = 0.002). **Each additional set was associated with ES +0.023, corresponding to +0.37 percentage points of muscle gain.**
- Higher versus lower volume within study: significant (p = 0.03), ES difference 0.241, equating to a **3.9 percentage point** difference in gain.
- Three-level categorisation (<5, 5–9, 10+ sets/muscle/week): trend only (p = 0.074).

The conclusion — "a graded dose-response relationship" — is sound but has been over-extrapolated. Three cautions:

1. **The model is linear over a limited observed range.** Extrapolating "more is better" beyond the volumes actually studied (mostly under ~20 sets/muscle/week) is not licensed by the data.
2. **Subsequent higher-volume work has been inconsistent.** Some trials find benefits up to 30–45 sets/muscle/week in trained lifters; others find plateau or regression. Heterogeneity is large and sample sizes are small.
3. **Set counting is a crude currency.** A set of 5 at 85% and a set of 20 at 40% both count as "one set" but differ enormously in effective reps, fatigue, and time. Junk volume — sets far from failure, with poor technique, or on redundant exercises — inflates the count without inflating the stimulus.

The defensible practical statement: **most trainees see clear benefit moving from very low volume (under ~4 sets/muscle/week) into a moderate range (roughly 10–20 hard sets/muscle/week), with diminishing returns above that and an individual ceiling beyond which recovery, not stimulus, becomes limiting.** Where exactly that ceiling sits for a given person is not predictable from the literature and must be found empirically.

Volume also interacts with everything else. Higher volume with short rest and high proximity to failure is a different proposition from higher volume with long rest and 3 RIR. Total *fatigue* rises faster than total *stimulus* as volume climbs.

## 4. Load and intensity — a wide effective range

**Schoenfeld and colleagues (2017)**, 21 studies, compared low load (≤60% 1RM) with high load (>60% 1RM), all sets to momentary failure:

- **1RM strength**: significantly greater with high load.
- **Isometric strength**: no significant difference.
- **Hypertrophy**: **similar between conditions.**

**Morton and colleagues (2016)** independently confirmed this in trained men: 12 weeks, 30–50% 1RM for 20–25 reps versus 75–90% 1RM for 8–12 reps, all to failure. Lean mass, Type I and Type II fibre CSA increased equally; only bench press 1RM differed (9 ± 1 kg versus 14 ± 1 kg, favouring heavy).

The practical implications:

- **Hypertrophy is load-permissive.** Anywhere from roughly 30% to 85% 1RM works if effort is sufficient. The 8–12 rep "hypertrophy zone" is a convention, not a physiological requirement — though it is a sensible default because very low loads require uncomfortably high rep counts and very high loads accumulate joint and CNS stress.
- **Strength is load-specific.** If the goal is a bigger 1RM, you must train near it. This is a skill and neural specificity effect (section 8), not a hypertrophy effect.
- **Caveats.** Low-load-to-failure training is genuinely unpleasant, and adherence in the real world is worse than in a supervised trial. Very-high-rep sets may also be limited by local metabolic discomfort before true muscular failure, meaning the "to failure" condition is harder to actually achieve.

## 5. Frequency

**Schoenfeld, Ogborn and Krieger (2016)** meta-analysed 10 studies. Training a muscle group **twice weekly produced greater hypertrophy than once weekly** on a volume-equated basis (ES 0.49 ± 0.08 versus 0.30 ± 0.07, p = 0.002). Whether three times weekly beats twice could not be determined from the available data.

The mechanistic rationale is that the elevation in muscle protein synthesis after a training bout is transient — roughly 24–48 h in trained individuals, longer in novices — so distributing volume across more sessions keeps the muscle in a net anabolic state a larger fraction of the time.

However: subsequent volume-equated work has narrowed this. When total weekly volume is genuinely matched, frequency differences shrink considerably. The most defensible reading is that **frequency is primarily a delivery mechanism for volume**. If you need 16 hard sets for a muscle per week, doing them in one session is impractical (fatigue degrades the later sets badly); splitting them into two or three sessions preserves set quality. That, more than any special property of frequency itself, is the argument.

## 6. Exercise selection and regional hypertrophy

Muscles do not grow uniformly. **Regional hypertrophy** — differential growth along the length of a muscle, or between compartments — is well documented:

- Different exercises for the same muscle produce different regional growth patterns (e.g. leg extension versus squat within the quadriceps; incline versus flat pressing within pectoralis major).
- Muscles with distinct architectural or neural compartments (biceps brachii long versus short head, triceps long head, gluteus maximus upper versus lower, hamstrings) can be biased by joint position.
- **Biarticular muscles** are the clearest case. The triceps long head and biceps long head cross the shoulder as well as the elbow; the rectus femoris crosses the hip; the hamstrings cross both hip and knee. Their length — and therefore the tension they experience — depends on the position of *both* joints. A leg curl and a Romanian deadlift both train hamstrings but at very different hip angles and therefore very different muscle lengths.

The practical inference: use **two to four distinct exercises per major muscle group**, chosen to differ in joint angle and resistance profile, rather than many near-duplicates. The gain from adding a genuinely different movement pattern is larger than from adding a fifth variation of the same one.

Exercise selection also carries a **stability and stimulus-to-fatigue** dimension. Free-weight compounds train more muscle per unit time and transfer better to sporting and daily tasks; machines and cables offer better stimulus-to-fatigue ratios for isolated muscles, more consistent resistance profiles, and lower technical and injury exposure. Neither is superior in general; they answer different questions.

## 7. Range of motion, lengthened partials, tempo and rest

### Range of motion

Full ROM has consistently outperformed **shortened** partial ROM for hypertrophy. The more interesting recent question is **lengthened partials** — training only the stretched portion of the movement.

Mechanistically the case is good: tension per fibre is high at long muscle lengths, and stretch-mediated hypertrophy is a well-documented phenomenon in animal models. Several small trials reported advantages for lengthened partials.

But the largest trial to date pushes back. **Gschneidner and colleagues (2025)** ran a pre-registered, 15-site cluster-randomised trial (n = 297; lengthened-partial 163, full ROM 134) over 12 weeks. The condition × time interaction estimate was **−0.032 for arm muscle CSA and 0 for thigh muscle CSA** — practically equivalent. The authors could not formally reject the null at their stringent alpha, but explicitly concluded that between-condition effects are "small and practically equivalent".

Meanwhile, McMahon and colleagues (2026) found lengthened partials at 55% 1RM produced hypertrophy comparable to full ROM at 80% 1RM, with *superior* fascicle-length adaptations — so the architectural claim may hold even where the size claim does not.

**Honest position:** train through a full range of motion as the default, emphasise the lengthened portion, and treat lengthened partials as a legitimate variation for specific exercises rather than as a proven superior method. The field is genuinely unsettled and the largest data set to date is null.

### Tempo

There is no strong evidence that any particular concentric tempo is superior for hypertrophy within a broad range (roughly 0.5–8 s per rep total). Very slow tempos (super-slow protocols) reduce the load that can be used and appear inferior. Intentionally slow eccentrics are popular but the evidence for a hypertrophy advantage is weak; they do increase time under tension and muscle damage, and — per section 1 — damage is not the goal. The defensible advice is to control the eccentric enough to maintain tension and technique, and to move the concentric with intent.

**Time under tension** as a metric is a poor proxy for stimulus, since it does not distinguish tension per fibre. Two sets of equal TUT at 30% and 85% 1RM are not equivalent stimuli.

### Rest intervals

The old bodybuilding convention of 30–60 s rest to "maximise metabolic stress" has not held up. **Schoenfeld and colleagues (2016)** randomised 21 trained men to 1-minute versus 3-minute rest, all other variables held constant, 8 weeks, 3 total-body sessions per week. The 3-minute group had significantly greater 1RM squat and bench press, significantly greater anterior thigh muscle thickness, and a trend for triceps (p = 0.06).

The mechanism is straightforward: short rest reduces the load or reps achievable in subsequent sets, cutting effective volume. Practical guidance: **at least 2–3 min between hard sets of compound lifts; 1–2 min is adequate for isolation work.** Where time is limited, antagonist supersets and non-competing pairings recover the time cost without sacrificing set quality.

## 8. Progressive overload and its practical implementations

Adaptation requires a stimulus that exceeds current capacity. "Progressive overload" names the requirement but not the method. The practical implementations, roughly in order of how commonly they are useful:

1. **Add load** — the cleanest signal, and the only one that directly drives strength. Constrained by how finely you can increment (microloading plates matter for upper-body lifts).
2. **Add reps at the same load** — double progression: work up to the top of a rep range across all sets, then increase load and drop back to the bottom.
3. **Add sets** — increases volume; the most reliable long-run driver of hypertrophy but the fastest to run into recovery limits.
4. **Improve technique / increase ROM** — increases the effective stimulus at the same nominal load. Often the real progression in the first year.
5. **Reduce rest at equal work**, **improve density**, or **improve rep quality/control** — genuine but lower-signal.
6. **Add frequency** — mostly a mechanism for delivering (3).

Progression cannot be linear indefinitely. A novice can add load session-to-session; an intermediate progresses week-to-week or block-to-block; an advanced lifter may progress over months and accept long plateaus. Mismatching the progression rate to training age is the single most common programming error — it produces either stagnation (too slow) or failed sessions and burnout (too fast).

## 9. Periodisation — the honest evidence

Three canonical models:

- **Linear (classic) periodisation.** Volume decreases and intensity increases across a macrocycle: e.g. 4 weeks of 4 × 10, then 4 × 6, then 4 × 4, then 3 × 2. Originating in Matveyev's Soviet-era work.
- **Undulating (non-linear) periodisation.** Load and rep ranges vary within the week (daily undulating, DUP) or within the block (weekly undulating).
- **Block periodisation.** Sequenced blocks each with a concentrated focus — accumulation (volume), transmutation (intensification), realisation (peaking) — exploiting the residual training effects of the preceding block.

The evidence: **periodised programmes generally outperform genuinely non-periodised, non-progressive programmes** — but that comparison is trivial, since the control is a programme that does not change at all. Comparisons *between* periodisation models, once volume and intensity are equated, produce small and inconsistent differences. Meta-analyses in this area have modest sample sizes, high heterogeneity, and frequently confound the model with the total volume actually performed.

The defensible position:

- Periodisation matters most for **peaking** — arriving at a competition with maximal expression of trained capacity. For a powerlifter or weightlifter this is genuinely important.
- Periodisation matters least for **hypertrophy** in a general trainee, where the volume performed and the consistency of progression dominate.
- The main real-world value of any periodisation scheme is **structured variation and planned fatigue management**, which prevents both staleness and unmanaged accumulation. Almost any coherent structure delivers this.

Treat claims that one model is definitively superior with scepticism.

## 10. Strength as a skill versus size

Strength = muscle cross-sectional area × neural and mechanical efficiency of expressing it in a specific task.

Two lifters with identical quadriceps volume can differ substantially in squat 1RM because of:

- **Motor learning** — groove, bracing, bar path, timing.
- **Segment lengths and moment arms** — anthropometry (file `04`).
- **Tendon stiffness** and stretch-shortening cycle utilisation.
- **Neural drive and inhibition** — voluntary activation, antagonist co-contraction.
- **Specificity of practice** — the 1RM is a tested skill; performing it frequently improves it.

Consequences: hypertrophy sets the ceiling over long timescales, but strength can be improved substantially without size change (and vice versa). A trainee who trains only in the 10–15 rep range will underperform their muscle mass on a 1RM test. A trainee who trains only singles will underperform their neural capacity on hypertrophy.

## 11. Specificity

The **SAID principle** (Specific Adaptation to Imposed Demand): adaptation is specific to the movement pattern, contraction type, velocity, load, range of motion, and energy system trained. Transfer decays with dissimilarity.

This constrains almost everything else in this file. It is why:
- Low-load training builds size but not maximal strength.
- Machine strength transfers imperfectly to free-weight strength.
- Isometric training strengthens mainly around the trained joint angle (roughly ±15–20°).
- "Functional training" claims that a novel unstable exercise transfers to sport are usually unsupported — instability training reduces force output and its transfer evidence is weak.

## 12. Individual response variability

The single most under-reported finding in the training literature. In studies with the same standardised programme, the same supervision and the same nutrition:

- Some participants gain substantial muscle; some gain little or none over the study period. The distribution of responses is wide, and heterogeneity of response is real (not purely measurement error) in the better-controlled studies.
- Response is trait-like across variables: high responders for hypertrophy are not automatically high responders for strength or for VO2max.
- Candidate determinants include satellite cell content and proliferative capacity, baseline myonuclear number, fibre-type distribution, ribosome biogenesis capacity, and a long list of genetic polymorphisms none of which is currently useful for prediction. **Commercial genetic tests for training response have no demonstrated predictive validity and should not be purchased.**

The practical consequence: population means are a starting hypothesis for an individual, not a prescription. The correct response to "the meta-analysis says X" is to try X, measure, and adjust.

## 13. Realistic rates of muscle gain

This section carries the largest gap between the literature and popular belief.

The most widely used practitioner model is **Alan Aragon's rate-of-gain heuristic**, which is a synthesis of the training literature and coaching observation rather than a single measured result. It should be labelled as such:

| Training age | Rate of muscle gain per month (drug-free) |
|---|---|
| Beginner (year 1) | ~1.0–1.5% of body mass |
| Intermediate (years 2–3) | ~0.5–1.0% of body mass |
| Advanced (year 4+) | ~0.25–0.5% of body mass |

For an 80 kg man that is roughly **0.8–1.2 kg/month in year one, 0.4–0.8 kg/month as an intermediate, and 0.2–0.4 kg/month thereafter** — and these are optimistic ceilings under good training, adequate protein and a small surplus, not expectations.

Other reference points:

- **Lifetime drug-free ceiling.** The Kouri (1995) fat-free mass index data found that non-users of anabolic steroids clustered up to a normalised FFMI limit of about **25.0**, and pre-steroid-era (1939–1959) Mr America winners averaged an estimated FFMI of 25.4. For a 1.80 m man that corresponds to roughly 81 kg of fat-free mass — around 88–90 kg at 10% body fat. That is a *very* high figure representing decades of training in genetically exceptional people; the paper is explicitly preliminary and FFMI is not a perfect discriminator. Detail in file `08`.
- **Women** gain muscle at a similar *relative* rate to men but a lower absolute rate, largely because of lower starting lean mass. The claim that women cannot gain appreciable muscle is false; the claim that they will "get bulky" from ordinary training is equally false.
- **The first weeks are misleading.** Early scale weight gain includes glycogen, associated water (roughly 3 g water per gram of glycogen), and muscle swelling from damage and inflammation. Genuine contractile protein accretion typically becomes measurable after 3–6 weeks. The Damas (2016) data illustrate this directly: no fibre CSA increase was detectable until week 10.

Anyone promising faster than these rates is selling something, has a drug-assisted reference population, or is measuring water.

## Open questions

- The rate-of-gain table is a **practitioner heuristic** (Aragon), not a meta-analytic result. It is widely used and broadly consistent with trial data but has no single primary citation. Marked `needs-verification` as a quantitative claim.
- The upper end of the volume dose-response (above ~20 sets/muscle/week) is genuinely unresolved; individual trials disagree and sample sizes are small.
- The "effective reps" model is a mechanistically motivated heuristic with only partial empirical support; the specific claim that ~5 reps before failure are the stimulating ones is not directly measured.
- Whether 3× weekly frequency exceeds 2× on a volume-equated basis remains undetermined (explicitly stated by Schoenfeld 2016).
- Lengthened-partials effect size remains contested; the largest trial (Gschneidner 2025) is null while several smaller trials are positive.
