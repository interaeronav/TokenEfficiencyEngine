# Health and fitness — domain map, disclaimer and evidence standard

**Summary.** This domain is an evidence-based reference on exercise physiology, resistance and endurance training, sports nutrition, body composition, supplementation, performance-enhancing drugs (covered factually and from a harm standpoint only), recovery, and the health and longevity literature. It is written the way a sports-science textbook or a rigorous practitioner would write it: mechanisms first, then what the controlled trials and meta-analyses actually show, then the honest statement of where evidence is thin, contested or absent. It is deliberately not a motivational document, a diet plan, or a marketing brochure. Where the popular fitness industry asserts certainty, this domain usually reports a wide confidence interval.

> ⚠️ **Disclaimer.** Everything in this domain is **educational information, not personalised medical, dietary or training advice**. It cannot account for your medical history, medication, injuries, pregnancy status, disordered-eating history, or laboratory results. Consult a physician before starting or substantially changing an exercise or nutrition programme, especially if you have cardiovascular, metabolic, renal or musculoskeletal disease, are pregnant or postpartum, are under 18, are over 65 and sedentary, or have any history of an eating disorder. Nothing here is a recommendation to use any prohibited or prescription-only substance.

## The evidence standard applied here

Six rules govern every claim in this domain.

**1. Named source or nothing.** Every quantitative claim — an effect size, a gram-per-kilogram figure, a hazard ratio, a prevalence percentage — traces to a specific paper or position stand listed in that file's `## Sources`. Where a figure is a practitioner heuristic rather than a measured result (for example "roughly 10–20 sets per muscle per week"), it is labelled as a heuristic and its provenance is stated.

**2. Effect sizes, not p-values.** "Statistically significant" is close to meaningless in a field of 20-person, 8-week trials. Where an effect exists, this domain reports its magnitude and, where available, its confidence interval. A standardised mean difference of 0.15 is reported as small even when *p* < 0.05, because it is small.

**3. The honest statement of weakness.** Sports science has a small-sample, short-duration, mostly-young-men, mostly-untrained-participants problem. Trials rarely exceed 12 weeks; hypertrophy is often measured by ultrasound with measurement error of comparable size to the effect; publication bias favours the novel over the null; and industry funding is common in supplement research. Where this applies, files say so explicitly rather than reporting the headline as fact.

**4. Mechanistic plausibility is not evidence of outcome.** Acute increases in muscle protein synthesis, hormone concentration, or blood flow are hypothesis-generating, not conclusive. The clearest lesson of the last fifteen years of hypertrophy research is that acute surrogate markers repeatedly failed to predict long-term outcomes — most notably the "post-exercise anabolic hormone spike" hypothesis, which the training literature has now largely abandoned.

**5. Contested is labelled contested.** Volume dose-response above ~20 sets/muscle/week, the practical value of periodisation models, the magnitude of the lengthened-partials advantage, deload frequency, and the effect of the menstrual cycle on training are all genuinely unsettled. Where researchers with good methods disagree, the disagreement is presented rather than resolved by fiat.

**6. Never invent.** If a figure could not be verified against a retrievable source, it is marked `needs-verification` in the file's frontmatter or flagged inline, and the file's `## Open questions` section says what remains unchecked.

## What this domain deliberately does not do

- **It does not provide drug protocols.** File `08` covers anabolic-androgenic steroids, SARMs, growth hormone, insulin, peptides, beta-agonists, thyroid hormones, diuretics, EPO and stimulants — their pharmacology, the size of the effects in the controlled literature, the documented adverse effects and mortality data, the legal position, and the anti-doping framework. It contains no cycles, no dosages, no sourcing information, and no harm-reduction usage instructions. That boundary is deliberate and is not negotiable within this knowledge base.
- **It does not promote restriction.** Fat loss is discussed as a physiological process with trade-offs. Contest preparation, aggressive deficits and very low body-fat percentages are covered with their documented costs — bone mineral density loss, endocrine suppression, menstrual dysfunction, mood disturbance, sleep disruption, metabolic adaptation, and elevated risk of clinical eating disorders — stated plainly and not as footnotes.
- **It does not sell supplements.** File `07` reports that most of the supplement market does nothing measurable, names the specific products in that category, and reports contamination rates from the analytical literature.
- **It does not treat "natural" outcomes as self-evident.** File `08` covers the fat-free mass index literature and the systematic misrepresentation of drug-assisted physiques as drug-free, because that misrepresentation directly distorts what readers believe is achievable.

## Cross-cutting facts worth knowing before anything else

Six findings recur across this domain and change how most other questions should be answered.

**Adherence dominates.** Across nutrition and training comparisons, the variable that best predicts a person's twelve-month outcome is whether they kept doing the thing. DIETFITS randomised 609 people to healthy low-fat versus healthy low-carbohydrate for a year and found a between-group weight difference of 0.7 kg (95% CI −0.2 to 1.6). The diets were not the difference. The people were.

**The first increment of activity is worth the most.** In a pooled analysis of 661 137 adults, moving from no leisure-time physical activity to *less than* the recommended guideline minimum was associated with 20% lower all-cause mortality. Meeting the guideline reached 31%. Tripling it reached 39%. The curve is steep at the bottom and flat at the top.

**Sleep is a training and body-composition variable, not a lifestyle preference.** Identical caloric restriction with 5.5 rather than 8.5 hours of sleep cut the proportion of weight lost as fat by 55% and increased fat-free mass loss by 60%.

**Measurement error is usually comparable to the effect being measured.** Ultrasound muscle thickness, DXA lean mass, skinfold-derived body fat and self-reported energy intake all carry errors of a size that swamps eight-to-twelve-week changes. This is the reason so many published findings fail to replicate, and the reason trends over months beat readings on a day.

**Recovery interventions that make you feel better may make you adapt worse.** Post-exercise cold water immersion reduces soreness and improves perceived recovery — and, over twelve weeks of strength training, produced smaller gains in strength, muscle mass, Type II fibre cross-sectional area and myonuclear number than simple active recovery.

**A large fraction of the visible reference standard is pharmacologically assisted.** Global lifetime prevalence of anabolic steroid use is 3.3% overall and 6.4% among males, and is far higher in the physique-focused subpopulations that dominate fitness media. Reasoning about your own rate of progress against those examples is reasoning against a contaminated baseline.

## Recurring failure modes this domain is built to avoid

Fitness information fails in a small number of predictable ways. Naming them makes the rest of the domain easier to read critically.

**The acute-marker fallacy.** A single bout raises muscle protein synthesis, or testosterone, or blood lactate; a product or method is then sold on that basis. Long-term outcome trials repeatedly contradict these extrapolations. Morton and colleagues (2016) found no correlation between post-exercise systemic hormone rises and 12-week gains in muscle size or strength in trained men, which invalidated a whole generation of programme-design advice built on "maximising the hormonal response".

**The trained-versus-untrained conflation.** Untrained participants improve on almost any stimulus. A protocol that produces large gains in novices tells you very little about what a person with five years of training should do. Where a study's participants were untrained, this domain says so, because the transfer is genuinely unreliable.

**The volume-equated trap in reverse.** Many comparisons of "method A versus method B" differ in total work, not only in the variable of interest. A study showing failure training beats non-failure training may simply be showing that more total effective reps beat fewer. Grgic and colleagues (2022) found exactly this: when volume was *not* equated, the analysis favoured non-failure training for strength — because the non-failure groups did more total sets.

**Measurement noise mistaken for effect.** B-mode ultrasound muscle thickness, DXA lean mass and skinfold-derived body fat all carry error that is often as large as the 8–12 week change being measured. A 3% between-group difference in muscle thickness across 20 people is not a finding; it is within the noise floor of the instrument and the biological variability of the sample.

**Survivorship and self-selection in physique examples.** The people visible in fitness media are the extreme right tail of genetic responsiveness, of training history, and — as file `08` documents at length — frequently of undisclosed pharmacology. Reasoning backwards from a visible physique to the method that produced it is unreliable in a way that is difficult to overstate.

**Regression to the mean dressed as intervention effect.** People start programmes when they feel worst — after an injury flare, a bad training block, a period of poor sleep. Improvement afterwards is partly the natural course. Uncontrolled before-and-after reports, including almost all coaching testimonials, cannot separate the two.

**The single-study headline.** Individual trials in this field routinely reverse on replication. The default posture toward any new single result — including a striking one — is to wait for it to be reproduced or meta-analysed, and to note it here as provisional in the meantime.

## Regional notes

Most of the science here is universal — muscle physiology does not vary by jurisdiction. Four things do:

- **Legal status of controlled substances** varies sharply. File `08` covers the US, UK, EU, South Africa and Namibia separately. **[NA]** and **[ZA]** markers appear where a requirement is jurisdiction-specific.
- **Supplement regulation** varies: the US operates a post-market model under DSHEA, the EU and UK a stricter pre-market food-supplement regime, and southern African markets carry a high proportion of imported product with variable enforcement. See file `07`.
- **Environmental training conditions.** Namibia and the Gulf present heat and aridity that materially change hydration, pacing and acclimatisation requirements; file `10` covers heat and altitude explicitly. **[NA]** Coastal Namibian conditions (Swakopmund, Walvis Bay) differ substantially from the interior highveld around Windhoek (~1 650 m elevation) and from the Kalahari and Namib interior in summer.
- **Food availability and cost** shape what dietary advice is actionable. File `05` favours principles over branded products for this reason.

## How to read the confidence flags

Every file carries a `confidence` key in its frontmatter:

- `high` — core claims rest on multiple meta-analyses or position stands with consistent direction of effect.
- `medium` — core claims rest on a small number of trials, or on meta-analyses with substantial heterogeneity, or the field is actively contested.
- `low` — mechanistically plausible and widely believed but poorly tested; treat as a working hypothesis.

Where a single figure inside an otherwise `high`-confidence file is unverified, it is flagged inline and repeated under `## Open questions`.
