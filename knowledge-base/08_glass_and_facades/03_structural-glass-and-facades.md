# Structural glass and facade engineering

**Summary.** Once glass stops being an infill panel in a wall and starts carrying load — its own weight, wind, barrier loads, or the loads of other elements — a different discipline applies. This file covers curtain wall typologies (stick, unitised, structural silicone glazed, point-fixed), the fundamentals of facade engineering, wind load and thickness selection, deflection limits, thermal breakage risk, movement and tolerance, and the specific problems of glass floors, stairs, balustrades, fins and bolted connections.

## 1. Curtain wall typologies

A curtain wall is a non-loadbearing envelope hung from the primary structure, carrying only its own weight and the wind and seismic loads applied to it, and transferring those to the floor slabs or columns.

### Stick system

Mullions and transoms are delivered as lengths ("sticks") and assembled in place, floor by floor, with glazing installed on site from inside or outside depending on access.

- **Advantages:** lowest tooling cost; forgiving of structural tolerance because each piece can be adjusted; repairable and re-glazable in place with ordinary trade skills; the right choice for low-rise, for small quantities, and for remote sites.
- **Disadvantages:** slow; quality depends entirely on site workmanship and site weather; site-applied seals; needs external access (scaffold or cradle).
- **Relevance to southern Africa:** stick systems and their shopfront cousins dominate the local market, because they suit the available labour, the project sizes, and the absence of large unitised fabrication capacity.

### Unitised system

Storey-height (or larger) panels are fabricated and glazed in a factory, delivered complete, and hung from cast-in or bolted brackets. Panel-to-panel joints are made by interlocking male/female mullion halves with pressure-equalised gaskets and drainage chambers.

- **Advantages:** factory quality control in a climate-controlled shop; very fast installation; no external access needed for installation; sealed joints are gasket-based rather than site-applied.
- **Disadvantages:** high tooling and engineering cost; needs accurate setting-out and bracket tolerance; damaged units are replaced whole; economics only work on large projects or where site labour is expensive.
- **Ladder systems** sit between the two: split mullions snapped or screwed together to speed site assembly, at the cost of structural performance and additional visible joint lines.

### Structural silicone glazed (SSG)

The glass is bonded to the frame with a structural silicone sealant rather than captured by a mechanical pressure plate — either two-sided (vertical joints bonded, horizontals capped) or four-sided (fully bonded, giving a flush all-glass appearance).

- The silicone must transfer **wind load** from glass to frame in tension and compression, and — unless a mechanical setting device is provided — nothing else. **Dead load must be carried mechanically**, by setting blocks or a support ledge: ASTM C1401 limits permanent dead-load shear on the sealant to **1 psi (0.007 MPa)**.
- **Bite** (the bonded width) is sized from wind load and the short span of the glass; **glueline thickness** is sized from differential movement between glass and frame. A published worked example uses a **12 mm bite × 6 mm glueline** at a design stress of **0.21 MPa** for a 2.5 × 3.2 m module.
- **Only structural silicone** is acceptable — not polysulphide, not polyurethane, not "hybrid" sealants. It must be a two-part or approved one-part structural grade with the manufacturer's project-specific approval.
- **Durability is genuinely proven:** specimens cut from an SSG facade after 23 years in service passed the ETAG 002-1 residual strength criterion (≥75% of initial strength, ≥90% cohesive failure), and second-generation sealants have passed ageing regimes representing 50 years.
- **Compatibility and adhesion testing is mandatory**, not optional — see `04_glazing-installation-and-detailing.md`.

### Point-fixed / spider glazing

Glass is supported at discrete points by bolts through countersunk or cylindrical holes, connected to articulated "spider" fittings, which are in turn carried by a backing structure — tension rods, cable nets, steel trusses or glass fins.

- Every hole is a stress concentration. Panes must be **toughened** (holes cannot be drilled after toughening, so hole positions are frozen very early), and for anything overhead or above a public route, **heat soak tested**.
- Fittings must be **articulated** (spherical or swivel bearings) so that pane rotation under wind does not induce bending at the bolt.
- Minimum edge distances and hole diameters are set by the fitting manufacturer and by the glass processor's capability; these are governing constraints and must be confirmed before the panel geometry is fixed.
- Bushings (usually acetal, nylon or aluminium) prevent glass-to-metal contact. **Glass must never bear directly on steel.**

## 2. Facade engineering fundamentals

A facade engineer is answering six questions simultaneously:

1. **Structure** — can the glass and framing carry wind, dead load, barrier load and impact within stress and deflection limits?
2. **Water** — where does water that gets past the outer line go, and how does it get out?
3. **Air** — how much air leaks, and how is the pressure-equalised cavity vented?
4. **Thermal** — what is the whole-facade U-value including frame and edge effects, and where is the condensation risk?
5. **Movement** — how does the facade accommodate thermal movement, structural deflection, inter-storey drift and construction tolerance?
6. **Fire and safety** — compartmentation at slab edges, spandrel behaviour, safe breakage and retention.

**Water management** works one of three ways: *face-sealed* (a single perfect outer seal — fragile and now discouraged); *water-managed* (accept some penetration, collect and drain it back out); and *pressure-equalised / rainscreen*, the standard for good curtain walling. In a rainscreen, the glazing rebate between the outer and inner gaskets is ventilated to the exterior so that the pressure on both sides of the outer gasket is the same; with no pressure difference, water is not drawn through gasket imperfections. Testing is by **ASTM E1105** (water penetration under a positive air pressure difference with an exterior spray rack) and **ASTM E783** (air infiltration), with EN equivalents referenced from EN 13830.

## 3. Wind load and thickness selection

Glass thickness in a facade is governed by **lateral load resistance**, not by handling convenience.

The process:

1. **Establish the design wind pressure** at the pane location. **[ZA]** SANS 10400-B and SANS 10160 (loading) govern; **[NA]** the SANS suite is used in practice. Pressures are highest at **corners, parapets and the top of the building**, often 2–3× the pressure on the middle of a face. Zoning the facade and varying the glass thickness by zone is normal engineering, not over-specification.
2. **Determine the pane geometry and support condition** — four-edge supported, two-edge supported, point-fixed. A two-edge supported pane needs far more thickness than a four-edge supported pane of the same area.
3. **Calculate** to **EN 16612:2019** (Determination of the lateral load resistance of glass panes by calculation), or to the equivalent national method, applying:
   - the **design strength** appropriate to the heat state (annealed < heat-strengthened < toughened);
   - a **load duration factor** — glass strength is time-dependent, so a 3-second gust and a permanent load use different values;
   - an **edge condition factor** — as-cut edges are weaker than ground or polished edges;
   - for laminates, an **interlayer shear transfer** assumption at the design temperature (ionoplast transfers shear; standard PVB at 50 °C transfers almost none, so a laminate must sometimes be treated as two independent plies).
4. **Check deflection** as a separate serviceability limit.

**[ZA]** SANS 10400-N carries deemed-to-satisfy thickness tables relating maximum pane area to thickness. Published commentary gives, for clear annealed glass in exterior doors and windows: 3 mm up to 0.75 m²; 4 mm to 1.5 m²; 5 mm to 2.1 m²; 6 mm to 3.2 m²; and for the safety glasses, laminated annealed from 6 to 12 mm covering 2.9–5.7 m² and toughened from 4 to 12 mm covering 1.9–8.0 m². **These are deemed-to-satisfy simplifications for ordinary buildings, not a substitute for calculation** on tall, exposed or unusually shaped facades. Treat them as a sanity check.

## 4. Deflection limits

Deflection controls appearance, glass stress, seal integrity and occupant confidence.

- **Framing members:** **L/175** is the commonly cited curtain-wall mullion limit under wind; **L/240** or an absolute cap of 19 mm is also widely used, and many specifications require the *lesser* of L/175 and 19 mm. Under dead load, mullion and transom deflection is usually limited far more tightly (of the order of L/500 or 3 mm) because it bears directly on the glass edge.
- **Glass panes:** typically limited to the **shorter span / 60**, or an absolute value, to keep the glass within its stress limit and the gaskets in contact.
- **Barriers and balustrades:** the Eleftheria Square project used **L/120 with a 25 mm cap** for barrier loads. Belgian practice is described as the most restrictive of the European national standards.
- **Supporting structure:** slab edge deflection is a facade problem, not just a structural one. A slab that deflects 20 mm between propping and cladding closes the movement joint the facade was designed around. Facade movement allowances must be coordinated with the actual predicted structural movement, including long-term creep.

## 5. Thermal breakage risk

The mechanism: the sunlit centre of a pane heats and tries to expand; the edge, held in a cool frame or in shadow, does not. The resulting tensile stress at the edge exceeds the edge strength of annealed glass and the pane cracks — typically with a crack running perpendicular to the edge from a point on the edge, then wandering.

Cumulative risk factors, from published guidance:

- **Absorbing glass.** Tinted and spectrally selective glasses absorb more solar radiation and are more susceptible than clear glass.
- **Coatings.** Reflective and low-E coatings both reflect and absorb; risk depends on coating type and surface position.
- **Partial external shading.** The worst case is a shadow line across the pane. Guidance recommends avoiding conditions where **less than 50% of the panel is shaded** — i.e. avoid partial shadow patterns.
- **Internal blinds and drapes.** They reflect solar energy back through the glass and raise its temperature. Close-fitting internal blinds behind absorbing glass are a classic failure combination.
- **Heating outlets** directed at the glass.
- **Framing with high heat capacity**, which holds the edge cold; low-heat-capacity framing reduces risk.
- **Edge quality.** As-cut and damaged edges dramatically reduce the tensile capacity available.

Vitro's published guidance sets a design threshold: if thermal stress analysis predicts more than **8 breaks per 1 000 units**, either change the design or specify heat-treated glass.

> ⚠️ In a hot, high-radiation climate with strongly absorbing solar-control glass, **heat-strengthened glass is the default, not the upgrade.** Heat-strengthened glass gives roughly double the thermal capability of annealed at a fraction of the spontaneous-fracture risk of fully toughened, and it breaks into retained fragments when laminated.

## 6. Movement and tolerance

Three movement sources must be accommodated, and they are additive:

1. **Thermal movement of the facade itself.** Aluminium has a coefficient of thermal expansion roughly twice that of steel and glass. A dark-coloured aluminium mullion in a Namibian summer can reach very high surface temperatures; a 3 m mullion moving over a 60 K range needs several millimetres of end clearance. Long horizontal runs need expansion joints.
2. **Structural movement.** Slab deflection, column shortening, creep and shrinkage in concrete, and inter-storey drift under wind or seismic action. Standard curtain wall systems are described as accommodating up to **3 in (≈76 mm)** of relative floor movement without glass breakage or leakage — but only if the joints were designed for it.
3. **Construction tolerance.** The facade bracket must have three-dimensional adjustability, because the concrete frame will not be where the drawings say it is. A ±25 mm adjustment range in all three axes is a normal minimum requirement and should be a stated performance requirement in the specification.

The corollary: **a facade must never be rigidly restrained at more than one point per direction.** Slotted holes, sliding brackets and split mullions exist for a reason.

## 7. Glass floors, stairs and treads

Walking on glass is a special engineering case governed by post-breakage behaviour.

- **Always laminated, always multi-ply, always with a sacrificial top ply.** The canonical build-up is three or more plies: an upper sacrificial ply (which may be annealed or heat-strengthened and is expected to be replaced if damaged), and two or more structural plies below it that alone must carry the full design load with the top ply broken.
- **Heat-strengthened, not fully toughened**, for the structural plies wherever possible: post-breakage fragment retention is what keeps a person out of the void.
- **Ionoplast (SGP) interlayers** are effectively mandatory for the structural laminate, because PVB has almost no shear stiffness at service temperature and, once the glass breaks, produces only a "blanket effect".
- **Slip resistance** on the walking surface — ceramic frit, etched pattern, or applied anti-slip — must be specified, and the pattern chosen so that it does not disappear when wet.
- The Grand Canyon Skywalk illustrates the principle at an extreme: the deck is **four layers of low-iron glass with SentryGlas interlayers**; the glass sidings use two layers of the same material.

## 8. Balustrades

The design problem is that a balustrade must both resist the barrier load *and* remain safe when the glass is broken.

**Loads.** Barrier line loads from European national standards: residential **1.0 kN/m**; offices **1.5 kN/m**; areas where people congregate **2.0–3.0 kN/m**. BS 6180:2011 is cited with distributed loads in the 0.5–1.5 kN/m² range. **[ZA]** SANS 10400-D requires a balustrade or parapet **not less than 1 m high** at the edge of any balcony, bridge, flat roof or similar place **more than 1 m above** the adjacent ground or floor level.

**Post-breakage.** The most demanding published criterion is Singapore's BCA requirement that a free-standing balustrade **resist 1.5 kN/m with all glass layers broken, within a maximum deflection of 150 mm**. Testing shows ionomer laminates with heat-strengthened glass meet this, while tempered-glass laminates initially failed it — because fully toughened glass, once broken, has no residual bending capacity at all.

**Typical build-ups** from published projects:
- 8 mm HS + 3.04 mm ionomer + 8 mm HS + 3.04 mm ionomer + 8 mm HS (triple laminate, cantilevered).
- 2 × 12 mm toughened + 2.28 mm ionomer.
- 10 mm + 10 mm fully toughened and heat-soak-tested, with a 1.54 mm SentryGlas interlayer, bottom-supported in a continuous AISI 316 stainless channel on nylon bearing blocks and top-restrained by stainless patch plates (Eleftheria Square, Nicosia).

**Impact testing** to EN 12600 or EN 950 regimes: a **50 kg** soft body (sandbag) dropped from **1.5 m**, and a **4.5 kg** hard body (steel ball), with post-breakage stability assessed afterwards.

**[ZA]** A frequently repeated practitioner rule is a **10 mm minimum glass thickness in a post-and-infill balustrade**. Treat that as a floor, not a design.

> ⚠️ Never use monolithic toughened glass in a free-standing (cantilevered) balustrade. When it breaks — and nickel sulphide guarantees that some will — there is nothing left. Use laminated heat-strengthened glass with a stiff interlayer, or provide a continuous top rail that would remain in place if a panel failed.

## 9. Glass fins and beams

Glass fins stabilise large glazed walls out of plane and let the facade read as pure glass.

- Fins are **always laminated**, minimum three plies for anything of consequence, with heat-strengthened or toughened glass and a stiff interlayer. Redundancy is the design principle: any single ply must be able to fail without collapse.
- **Lateral torsional buckling** governs long slender fins, not bending stress. Restraint at the connection points to the facade glass is part of the structural system.
- **Connections** are either bolted (with bushings and articulated fittings) or bonded (structural silicone, or increasingly transparent structural silicone adhesive, TSSA). Bolted connections in fins are stress concentrations in a member already working hard.
- **Splices** between fin segments transfer load through steel or titanium plates bonded or bolted across the joint; the splice, not the glass, usually governs.

## 10. Bolted connections — practical rules

- **No glass-to-metal contact anywhere.** Bushings, gaskets, or an injected resin between fitting and glass.
- **Countersunk fittings** give a flush exterior but demand thicker glass and tighter tolerance; **through-bolt / disc fittings** are more forgiving.
- **Articulation** at the fitting so the pane can rotate freely under load.
- **Hole positions are frozen before toughening.** A change to a bracket position after the glass has been toughened means new glass.
- **Fixings must be adjustable** in three axes and must accommodate the movement calculated in section 6.
- **Corrosion:** stainless steel grade must suit the exposure. **[NA]** Coastal Namibia — Walvis Bay, Swakopmund, Lüderitz — is a severe marine environment; specify AISI 316 as a minimum there, and be aware that even 316 will show tea-staining. Inland Namibia is very much less aggressive.

## Open questions

- **Deflection limits of L/240, L/500 and 19 mm** are stated as widely used industry practice; only **L/175** was verified from a cited source. Confirm against EN 13830, the system supplier's technical manual and the project specification.
- SANS 10160 wind loading clause references have not been verified from the standard.
- The SANS 10400-N deemed-to-satisfy thickness tables reproduced in section 3 come from published commentary, not from the standard itself.
- No southern African facade-engineering practice guide was located in this research pass; the wind zoning and pressure-multiplier statements are general engineering practice, not cited.
- Minimum edge distances and hole diameters for point fixings are manufacturer-specific and were not verified.
