# Landmark glass projects

**Summary.** Nine projects, each teaching a different lesson about what glass can and cannot do: the Louvre Pyramid on low-iron glass and geometric discipline; the Grand Canyon Skywalk on redundancy in a glass floor; the Shard on scale and facade area; the Willis Tower Ledge on the difference between glass failure and coating failure; the Steve Jobs Theater on glass as primary structure; Fondation Louis Vuitton on digitally fabricated curved glass; Apple Fifth Avenue on the economics of ever-larger panes; and the Leonardo and Portside Tower on what large-scale glazed construction looks like in South Africa. Facts here are limited to what could be verified; unverified attributions are flagged rather than guessed.

## 1. Louvre Pyramid, Paris (1989)

- **Architect:** I. M. Pei, selected 1983, concept public in early 1984, completed 1989.
- **Engineers:** Nicolet Chartrand Knoll Ltd. of Montreal (pyramid structure/design consultant); Rice Francis Ritchie of Paris (pyramid structure, construction phase). Vinci built the base and underground lobby.
- **Glass:** Saint-Gobain.
- **Geometry:** height **21.6 m**; square base **34 m** per side, about **1 000 m²**; side angle **51.52°** to the base. **673 panes** in total — **603 rhombus-shaped** and **70 triangular**. Three sides carry 171 panes each; the entrance side carries 160.

**Technical challenge.** Pei wanted the glass to disappear. Ordinary float glass, seen at an angle and doubled up in a laminate, reads distinctly green — which over a 21.6 m pyramid would have looked like a greenhouse over the Cour Napoléon rather than a transparent void.

**Solution.** Low-iron glass, produced to a specification with the iron content reduced far below ordinary float, so that the transmitted and reflected colour is neutral. The pyramid is, in effect, the project that made low-iron glass an architectural product rather than a laboratory curiosity. The second half of the solution is the space-frame of stainless steel rods and cables behind the glass: it carries the load with members thin enough that the eye reads the glass, not the structure.

**Lesson.** In a fully glazed geometric form, **the colour of the glass is a primary design decision**, and it is set by the iron content of the batch — a decision made at the float plant, not on site.

## 2. Grand Canyon Skywalk, Arizona (2007)

- **Designer:** Mark Ross Johnson (architect). General contractor Executive Construction Management, Las Vegas.
- **Glass:** deck of **four layers of Saint-Gobain Diamant low-iron glass with DuPont SentryGlas interlayers**; the glass sidings use **two** layers of the same.
- **Geometry:** cantilevers **70 ft (21 m)** from the posts nearest the canyon wall, over a vertical drop of **500 to 800 ft (150–240 m)**.
- **Capacity:** engineered to carry **822 people of 200 lb (91 kg) each without overstress**; maximum occupancy is administratively limited to **120** at a time.
- **Opened:** 28 March 2007. **Cost:** approximately **US$30 million**.

**Technical challenge.** A glass walking surface over a 240 m void, on a remote site, exposed to extreme temperature swings, dust, wind and a very large public.

**Solution.** Redundancy, executed twice over. First, in the laminate: four plies rather than the two that would satisfy a bending check, so that the loss of one or two plies does not compromise the structure. Second, in the interlayer: **SentryGlas ionoplast** rather than PVB, because ionoplast retains shear stiffness at service temperature and, critically, holds broken glass in a load-carrying configuration rather than as a limp blanket. Third, in the glass itself: **low-iron**, because four plies of ordinary float would have made the canyon floor look green.

**Lesson.** A glass floor is designed by its **post-breakage** case, not its intact case. The intact structure is easy; the question is what happens when the top ply is dropped on.

## 3. The Shard, London (2012)

- **Architect:** Renzo Piano Building Workshop with Adamson Associates Architects.
- **Structural engineer:** WSP. **Services engineer:** Arup.
- **Facade:** **11 000 panes** of glass, total surface area **602 779 sq ft** (approximately **56 000 m²**), described as equivalent to about eight football pitches.
- **Building:** **309.6 m**, 95 storeys with 72 habitable floors. Construction started 16 March 2009; completed 5 July 2012; opened to the public 1 February 2013.

**Technical challenge.** Piano's brief to himself was "a sophisticated use of glazing, with expressive façades" whose appearance changes with the weather and the season. The building is a tapering, faceted form in which the eight facade planes do not meet — they slide past one another — so almost every level has a different plan dimension and no two facade zones are identical.

**Solution.** Angled glass panes, set out so that each reflects a different part of the sky, and a facade rationalised into a manageable number of unitised panel types despite the taper. The commercial lesson is in the numbers: 11 000 panes over 72 habitable floors is roughly 150 panes per floor, and the only way to build that is factory-glazed unitised panels on a repeating bracket grid.

**Lesson.** Facade area drives everything on a tall building — cost, programme, thermal performance, cleaning. 56 000 m² of glass is the project.

## 4. Willis Tower Skydeck, "The Ledge", Chicago (2009)

- **Opened:** 2 July 2009, on the 103rd floor at approximately **1 353 ft (412 m)** above street level.
- **Geometry:** the glass boxes extend approximately **4 ft (1.2 m)** from the facade.
- **Glass:** laminated glass flooring. **Capacity:** 5 short tons (4.5 t).
- **Incidents:** on **29 May 2014** the laminated glass flooring of one box was damaged while visitors were present; no injuries. The same box's flooring cracked again on **12 June 2019**.

**Technical challenge and what actually happened.** The Ledge is instructive precisely because of the incidents. In the 2014 event the damage was reported as being to the protective coating over the laminated glass rather than to the structural glass itself. The structure held; the visible cracking was in a sacrificial layer.

**Solution — and the design principle.** A public glass floor should be built with a **sacrificial top layer** that takes the scratching, the impacts and the abuse, and can be replaced without touching the structural laminate below. That is the same three-ply principle as the Grand Canyon Skywalk, applied to a much smaller and much more heavily trafficked surface.

**Lesson.** In a glass floor, **plan for damage as a maintenance event, not a structural one**. And note the public-perception risk: a crack in a sacrificial layer over the Chicago Loop makes global news whether or not it was ever a safety issue.

## 5. Steve Jobs Theater, Apple Park, Cupertino (2017)

- **Auditorium:** underground, **1 000 seats**, entered through a large above-ground cylindrical glass lobby with stairs down.
- **Roof:** carbon fibre, **80 short tons (73 t)**, **44 identical panels**, each **70 ft (21 m) long × 11 ft (3.4 m) wide**, supplied by Premier Composite Technologies (Dubai). Described as the largest carbon-fibre roof and **"the largest glass-supported structure in the world."**
- **Facade glass:** produced by the Bavarian companies **Josef Gartner** and **sedak** (sedak is part of the seele group).
- **Also:** a **42 ft (13 m)** glass elevator that rotates **171°** from the lower floor to the upper lobby — described as the tallest free-standing glass elevator in the world.

**Technical challenge.** A 73-tonne roof carried on nothing but a ring of glass, with no columns, no mullions and no visible structure — and it had to be earthquake-country compliant.

**Solution.** The glass cylinder is the structure. That demands: laminated glass panels of extreme size and flatness (sedak's specialism is oversize laminating and toughening); tolerances of a fabricated-steel order applied to glass; a roof stiff and light enough that the glass can carry it (hence carbon fibre rather than steel); and connections that transfer load into the glass edge without concentrating stress. The roof was made in 44 identical panels precisely so that the load path into the glass ring would be uniform.

**Lesson.** Glass can be the primary structure, but only when everything else in the building is subordinated to that decision — the roof material, the panel repetition, the tolerance regime, the fabricator selection. It is not a detail; it is the design.

## 6. Fondation Louis Vuitton, Paris (2014)

- **Architect:** Frank Gehry, with STUDIOS architecture as local architect.
- **Glass:** **3 600 glass panels** and 19 000 concrete panels overall; the signature "sails" comprise **3 584 laminated glass panels**, each individually curved to the geometry the architect drew.
- **Process:** more than 400 people contributed to a shared, web-hosted 3D digital model, using **Digital Project** — software adapted from the aviation industry — and industrial robots moulded the panels from that common model.
- **Dates:** design meeting 2001; project presented 2006; construction began March 2008; building permit April 2011; glass sails installed 2012; opened 20 October 2014.

**Technical challenge.** 3 584 curved laminated panels, essentially none of which are the same. Under conventional practice that means 3 584 sets of shop drawings, 3 584 moulds, and a schedule measured in decades.

**Solution.** A single shared parametric model as the sole source of truth, from which mould geometry was driven directly to robotic forming — eliminating the drawing-and-re-drawing loop that would otherwise have made the project impossible. Each panel was hot-bent to its own curvature and then laminated.

**Lesson.** **Free-form glass is a data problem before it is a glass problem.** The enabling technology is the model-to-fabrication pipeline, not the glass. Where that pipeline does not exist — which is most projects, and certainly most projects in southern Africa — free-form glass geometry should be resisted, or replaced by cold-bending flat panels within their elastic limit, which is a far cheaper way to approximate curvature.

## 7. Apple Fifth Avenue glass cube, New York (2006, rebuilt 2011)

- **Geometry:** a cube **32 ft (9.8 m)** on each side.
- **Panel count:** **90** glass panels as originally built in 2006; **15** panels after the 2011 renovation, unchanged through the 2017–19 reconstruction.
- **Structure:** multi-ply glass for nearly the entire structure, with only small stainless steel bolts tying the panels together.
- **Dates:** opened 19 May 2006; 2011 renovation took five months and cost **US$6.7 million**; closed 2017–2019, reopened 20 September 2019.
- **Design lineage:** explicitly inspired by the Louvre Pyramid; the architects consulted I. M. Pei. Peter Bohlin was involved in the design; a design patent was granted in 2014.

**Technical challenge.** The 2006 cube was already a structural glass tour de force, but at 90 panels it read as a grid of glass — the mullion lines, even as thin silicone joints, were legible.

**Solution.** By 2011 the glass industry could laminate and toughen panels large enough that each cube face could be built from far fewer, far larger pieces. Reducing 90 panels to 15 removed most of the joint lines and made the cube read as an object rather than an assembly.

**Lesson.** **The maximum available pane size is a moving constraint, and it moves fast.** A detail that was impossible in 2006 was routine by 2011. Conversely — and this is the point for a southern African project — **the constraint is your processor's furnace and autoclave, not the world's.** Design to the largest size your actual supplier can toughen, laminate and transport, which in this region means the stock sheet, not the jumbo sheet.

## 8. The Leonardo, Sandton, Johannesburg (2019)

- **Height:** **234 m** to roof level. **Floors:** 55, with 200 apartments and 11 floors of commercial office, plus street-level retail and podium facilities.
- **Architect:** Co-Arc International Architects. **Developer/owner:** Legacy Group. **Main contractor:** Aveng Grinaker-LTA.
- **Completed:** 2019. Tallest building in South Africa and in sub-Saharan Africa since 2019; briefly Africa's tallest in April 2019.

**Technical challenge.** A 234 m residential-led tower with a heavily articulated, stepped, terraced form — many balconies, many setbacks, and consequently an enormous perimeter-to-floor-area ratio and a facade with very little repetition. Add Johannesburg's high altitude (about 1 750 m, so lower air density but very high UV), a summer thunderstorm regime with driving rain, and a large diurnal temperature swing.

**Solution and status.** The facade and glazing package, the facade engineer and the glass supplier for the Leonardo **were not verified in this research pass** — see Open questions. What the project demonstrates unambiguously is that South Africa has the contracting capacity to deliver a 234 m tower with a complex glazed envelope using regional supply, which is the relevant data point for a southern African specifier.

## 9. Portside Tower, Cape Town (2014)

- **Location:** 5 Buitengracht Street, Cape Town CBD.
- **Height:** **139 m**, **32 storeys**, over **52 000 m²** of floor area, roughly 1 382 parking bays.
- **Architects:** dhk Architects and Louis Karol Architects. **Civil engineer:** WSP Structures Nadeson.
- **Sustainability:** **five-star Green Star rating** from the Green Building Council of South Africa; described as the tallest green building in the country.
- **Cost:** approximately **R1.6 billion**. Construction began 12 August 2011, temporarily halted March 2013 due to severe weather; completed 2014.

**Technical challenge.** Cape Town's south-easter is one of the more demanding urban wind environments in the southern hemisphere, and the site sits at the foot of Signal Hill where the wind accelerates. A 139 m tower there faces high design wind pressures, severe corner suction, and driving rain — a combination that punishes any facade whose drainage is an afterthought.

**Solution and status.** The building is described as designed for low energy use throughout, and as the first large building in South Africa to use almost exclusively LED lighting. **The specific glazing make-up, curtain wall system and facade contractor were not verified.** Its five-star Green Star rating implies a glass selection with controlled SHGC and a fenestration strategy that satisfies SANS 204, but the actual figures were not obtained.

**Lesson.** The instructive point for this knowledge base is that **South African Green Star practice already forces the SHGC/VLT trade-off** described in `02_performance-and-coatings.md`. A five-star building is not achievable with clear float glass on a fully glazed envelope.

## Cross-cutting lessons

1. **Low-iron glass is the enabler of transparency** at scale, at angle and in multi-ply build-ups (Louvre, Skywalk).
2. **Glass floors are designed by their broken state** and need a sacrificial layer plus a stiff interlayer (Skywalk, Willis).
3. **Ionoplast interlayers are what make structural laminated glass possible**; PVB is a safety interlayer, not a structural one.
4. **Facade area is the project** on a tall building (Shard).
5. **Glass as primary structure demands total subordination of everything else** to the tolerance and load-path regime (Steve Jobs Theater).
6. **Free-form glass is a data pipeline problem** (Fondation Louis Vuitton) — and where the pipeline is absent, cold-bending flat glass is the pragmatic substitute.
7. **Maximum pane size is set by your processor, not by the state of the art** (Apple Fifth Avenue).
8. **South African supply can deliver a 234 m tower and a five-star Green Star envelope** (Leonardo, Portside).
