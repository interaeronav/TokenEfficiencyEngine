# Glazing installation and detailing

**Summary.** Most glazing failures are not glass failures. They are installation failures: glass bearing on metal, no drainage from the rebate, an incompatible sealant, a setting block in the wrong place, an edge clearance too tight for thermal movement. This file covers the practical trade — setting and location blocks, edge clearance and bite, tapes and gaskets, wet versus dry glazing, silicone selection and compatibility, frame materials and thermal breaks, sill and head detailing, water management, sealant joints, and safe handling and site glazing method. **[ZA]** The governing installation standard is **SANS 10137, Installation of glazing in buildings**, referenced from SANS 10400-N.

## 1. First principles

Four rules govern all glazing detailing. Everything else is an elaboration of them.

1. **Glass must never touch a hard material.** Not steel, not aluminium, not concrete, not another pane. Every contact is through a resilient block, gasket or sealant.
2. **The rebate will get wet. Drain it.** Assume water passes the outer seal. The rebate must have a fall, weep holes, and a clear path out that does not run back inside.
3. **Everything moves.** Glass, frame, structure and building all move at different rates. Clearances exist to absorb that movement, not to make assembly easy.
4. **Chemistry matters.** Sealants, gaskets, setting blocks, spacers, coatings, interlayers and cleaning solvents can all attack one another. Compatibility is tested, not assumed.

## 2. Setting, location and distance blocks

Three different components, routinely confused on site.

**Setting blocks** carry the weight of the glass and sit on the bottom rebate.
- Material: typically EPDM, neoprene or silicone of specified hardness (commonly 80–90 Shore A); **nylon or acetal** where a harder bearing is needed, as in a balustrade shoe channel.
- **Position:** conventionally at the **quarter points** of the sill — roughly one-quarter of the pane width in from each vertical edge — so that the sill deflects symmetrically and the glass is not loaded at a corner. On very wide panes, and where the frame is stiff, other positions may be specified by the system supplier; follow the system manual, because the mullion and transom design assumes a particular load position.
- **Length and width:** long enough that the bearing pressure is within the block's capacity, and **at least as wide as the full glass thickness** — including, for an IGU, both lites and the spacer, so that the unit is supported across its full edge and the edge seal is not put into shear.
- Blocks must be **compatible with the IGU secondary seal and with any structural silicone**.

**Location (or centring) blocks** hold the glass centred in the rebate side to side and stop it sliding. They go on the vertical edges near the top and bottom.

**Distance pieces** maintain the face clearance between glass and rebate upstands so the sealant or gasket is not squeezed out and the drainage path is not blocked.

> ⚠️ **In structural silicone glazing, the setting blocks are what carry the glass.** The silicone carries wind load only. A missing or mis-sized setting block puts permanent dead-load shear into the sealant, which ASTM C1401 limits to 1 psi (0.007 MPa) — an order of magnitude below what a hanging pane would apply.

## 3. Edge clearance, edge cover and bite

Four dimensions define a glazing rebate and all four must be stated in a specification.

| Term | What it is | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Edge clearance** | gap between the glass edge and the back of the rebate | absorbs thermal and structural movement and manufacturing tolerance; too tight and the glass is compressed by a moving frame |
| **Edge cover** (bite, in framed glazing) | depth the frame overlaps the glass face | resists wind suction pull-out; too shallow and the glass can be sucked out of the frame |
| **Face clearance** | gap between the glass face and the rebate upstand | space for the sealant, gasket or tape |
| **Structural bite** (SSG) | bonded width of structural silicone | transfers wind load; sized by calculation, not by rule of thumb |

Practical guidance:
- Edge clearance grows with pane size and with the thermal movement expected. Large dark-framed panes in a Namibian summer need generous clearance.
- Edge cover must be maintained **all round** and must be checked after the frame has been squared — a racked frame loses cover at the corners.
- In SSG, bite and glueline are calculated from wind pressure and short span, and from differential movement respectively. A published worked example is a **12 mm bite with a 6 mm glueline** at a design stress of **0.21 MPa** on a 2.5 × 3.2 m module. **Never scale these to another project without recalculating.**
- For laminated glass, and especially for laminates with exposed edges, the edge must be protected from standing water — long-term water contact at a laminate edge causes delamination.

## 4. Wet, dry and hybrid glazing

**Dry glazing** uses preformed gaskets — an outer wedge or bubble gasket and an inner gasket, usually EPDM or santoprene, held in a groove. Advantages: fast, consistent, no cure time, easily re-glazed, and it naturally forms a ventilated rebate. Disadvantages: gasket corners are the weak point (corners should be **vulcanised or moulded**, not simply mitred and butted), and gaskets take a compression set over time.

**Wet glazing** beds the glass in gunned sealant or in traditional putty. Advantages: tolerant of irregular openings, good on refurbishment. Disadvantages: slow, weather-dependent, messy, needs skilled application, and re-glazing means cutting the glass out.

**Hybrid** (tape plus heel bead, or gasket plus cap bead) is common: a preformed **glazing tape** — butyl or polyisobutylene, sometimes with a shim — on the inside face for a consistent face clearance and an air seal, with a gunned weather seal outside.

**Putty** remains in use in southern Africa on small timber and steel domestic windows, and has historically been carried as a product line by PFG. It is not appropriate for coated glass, laminates, IGUs, or anything with a movement requirement.

## 5. Silicone selection and compatibility

Three functionally different sealants get called "silicone" on site and they are not interchangeable:

| Type | Purpose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Structural silicone** | transfers wind load from glass to frame | two-part or approved one-part; project-specific approval from the manufacturer; ETAG 002 / EN 13022 family |
| **Weather-seal silicone** | seals movement joints against water and air | high movement capability; neutral cure for most substrates |
| **IGU secondary-seal silicone** | structural edge seal of the sealed unit | mandatory in place of polysulphide where the unit edge is exposed to UV or is structurally glazed |

**Cure chemistry matters.** *Acetoxy* (acetic acid) cure silicones are cheap, cure fast, and corrode metals, attack cementitious substrates, and can damage mirror backing and some coatings. *Neutral cure* (oxime or alkoxy) silicones are the correct choice for facade work, coated glass, mirrors and masonry.

**Compatibility and adhesion testing is a deliverable, not a courtesy.** Before any structural or weather sealant is used, the sealant manufacturer must be sent actual samples of every material the sealant will touch — the glass with its actual coating, the frame with its actual finish, the gaskets, setting blocks, spacers, interlayer, backer rod and any tapes — and must issue a written **compatibility and adhesion report** for the project. This is where projects fail. A plasticiser migrating out of a gasket into a structural silicone will destroy the bond years later.

**Joint design.** A movement joint sealant works in a 2:1 width-to-depth ratio with a **backer rod** to give a bond-breaker and the correct hourglass profile. Three-sided adhesion — where the sealant bonds to the back of the joint as well as the two faces — makes the joint fail. Backer rod must be closed-cell or bond-breaker-taped.

**Cleaning and priming.** Substrate preparation is a two-cloth solvent wipe (apply, then wipe off before it evaporates), followed by the specified primer where the manufacturer requires one. Do this in the shade; a substrate at 50 °C will flash off solvent before it has cleaned anything.

## 6. Frames

### Aluminium

The dominant architectural frame material in southern Africa, extruded locally by **Wispeco** (Alrode, Alberton — an aluminium casting facility and nine extrusion presses) and fabricated into systems by systems houses, of which **Crealco** is the largest local example, with ranges organised by market segment: **Home**, **Mansion**, **Mall** and **Tower**, and named systems including **Swift** (28 / 30.5 / 34 / 38 mm), **Skyline** 41 mm high-performance, **Serene** 52 mm tilt-and-turn, **Elite** sliding, **Clip 44** shopfront, **Vistafold** sliding-folding, and **Façade 60** curtain wall in pressure-plate and flush-glaze versions. **The system depth governs the glass make-up it can take** — a 28 mm Swift window will not accept a 24 mm IGU; check the system's glazing pocket against the scheduled make-up before the glass is ordered. International systems (Schüco, AluK and others) are available through licensed local fabricators.

Practical points:
- **Finish:** powder coating or anodising. **[NA]** For coastal Namibia specify a marine-grade powder coat or a heavier anodic film; inland the exposure is mild.
- **Dark colours run hot** and move more; check the system's maximum recommended panel size for dark finishes.
- **Thermal breaks** are a barrier between the exterior and interior metal — polyamide strips or poured-and-debridged polyurethane in modern systems, PVC in older ones. They cut the U-value substantially but **reduce the moment of inertia** of the mullion, so a thermally broken profile is structurally weaker than the same profile solid. Do not substitute one for the other without checking spans.
- **Galvanic corrosion:** aluminium in contact with steel, or with wet concrete, corrodes. Isolate with a separating tape or a non-conductive washer.

### Timber

Good thermal performance intrinsically, needs maintenance. In this climate the enemies are UV degradation of the coating and, in the north, termites and borer. Requires: a stable, kiln-dried, treated species; a drained and ventilated rebate with a proper sub-sill; and a maintainable coating system. Sealing timber against moisture on both faces is essential to prevent cupping.

### uPVC

Excellent thermally, poor structurally at large sizes, and the reason it is uncommon in southern Africa is UV and heat: unreinforced white uPVC in high-altitude, high-UV sunlight degrades and dark uPVC distorts. If used, it must be steel-reinforced in the chambers and specified with a high-UV-stability formulation.

### Steel

Slender sightlines, very high strength, and historically the domestic window material in southern Africa. Thermally poor unless thermally broken (few steel systems are). Requires a robust corrosion system: hot-dip galvanising plus a coating for external use. Steel and glass have similar coefficients of thermal expansion, which helps.

## 7. Sill, head and jamb detailing

**Sill.**
- Provide a **fall to the outside** — 1:10 minimum on an exposed sill.
- A **drip** or throating on the underside of the projecting sill so water does not track back to the wall.
- **Stop ends and upstands** at the jambs so water cannot run sideways into the reveal.
- **Weep holes** from the glazing rebate to the outside, sized and positioned so they are not blocked by sealant or by the outer gasket. Two per pane minimum, near the setting-block positions but not under them.
- A **sub-sill or flashing** under the frame, lapped over the DPC and turned up at the ends, is what actually keeps water out of the wall.

**Head.**
- A DPC/flashing over the head, turned up behind the wall face and stopped up at the ends, discharging over the frame.
- Allow for **structural deflection of the lintel or slab above.** A soft joint of adequate depth at the head is essential; a window jammed hard under a deflecting slab will break its own glass.

**Jambs.**
- Vertical DPC returning into the reveal.
- Fixing brackets adjustable in all three axes.
- A perimeter movement joint that is sealed, not packed with mortar.

## 8. Water management in a facade

The rainscreen / pressure-equalisation principle is the standard for good curtain walling: the glazing rebate between the inner and outer gaskets is **ventilated to the exterior**, so the air pressure on both sides of the outer gasket is the same and there is no pressure gradient to drive water through gasket imperfections. Any water that does enter is drained through the horizontal transom chamber and out through weep slots at the next horizontal below.

For this to work in practice:
- The **inner seal must be the airtight line**, and it must be continuous. Most water leaks are actually air-leak paths.
- **Horizontal-to-vertical joints** are where systems leak; the interlocking gaskets or the "wet corner" splice must be executed exactly as the system manual shows, including the small silicone dabs at the corner intersections that everyone skips.
- **Drainage must be tested** — a hose test on a mock-up before the first bay is closed up is worth its cost many times over.

## 9. Handling, lifting and site glazing method

Glass is heavy, fragile in bending, and lethally sharp. A 6 mm pane weighs about 15 kg/m²; a 12 mm pane about 30 kg/m²; a 12.38 mm laminate about 31 kg/m². A 3 × 2 m sheet of 12 mm glass is therefore around 180 kg — a two-crane-and-frame proposition, not a four-man lift.

**Handling rules:**
- **Carry glass on edge, never flat.** Flat, it will break under its own weight.
- **Store on A-frames or racks at a slight lean (about 5–7° off vertical), on timber or rubber bearers, out of direct sun, and dry.** Glass stored flat, wet, in sun, or with paper between the sheets in humid conditions will stain permanently ("weathering" or "bloom") and is then unusable for glazing.
- **PPE:** cut-resistant gloves, forearm protection, safety boots, eye protection.
- **Never walk under a suspended pane.** Never stand in the plane of a pane being tilted.

**Suction lifting:**
- Use a **rated vacuum lifter** with the correct number and size of pads for the pane weight and the pad's rated capacity — with a stated safety factor, conventionally 2:1 for manual lifters and higher for powered lifting.
- **Powered lifters must have a vacuum reservoir and an audible low-vacuum alarm**, and a means of holding vacuum on power failure.
- **The pads must sit on clean, sound, flat glass.** Never on a curved pane not matched to the pad, never over a hole, never on a fritted or textured face without checking that the pad seals.
- **Check the vacuum gauge before every lift and watch it throughout.**
- **Coated glass:** lift on the uncoated face where possible; some soft coats mark under pads.

**Site glazing sequence (framed, dry-glazed):**
1. Check the opening is square, plumb and within tolerance; check the frame is drained and the weeps are clear.
2. Fit the inner gasket, then the setting blocks at the correct positions, then the location blocks.
3. Offer the glass in on suction cups; land it on the setting blocks; centre it; confirm edge clearances all round.
4. Fit the outer gasket or beads, working from the centre of each side out to the corners so the gasket is not stretched.
5. Where a cap bead is specified, tool it against a backer or bond-breaker.
6. Clean off, mark the pane where required, and protect it.

**[ZA]** Mark and manifest the glazing where the standard requires it — large areas of clear glass in circulation routes must be made "obvious to anyone who approaches it", which in practice means manifestation at the two heights people look at.

## 10. Common failures and their causes

| Symptom | Usual cause |
|---|---|
| Water on the internal sill | blocked or absent weep holes; discontinuous inner air seal; missing corner seals |
| Cracks running perpendicular from an edge | thermal stress: absorbing glass, partial shading, internal blinds, poor edge quality |
| Cracks from a corner or a bearing point | glass on metal, missing or displaced setting block, frame racking |
| IGU fogging internally | edge seal failure — often from standing water in the rebate, UV on a polysulphide seal, or incompatible setting blocks |
| Laminate edge cloudiness | prolonged water contact at an exposed laminate edge |
| Structural silicone adhesion failure | no compatibility/adhesion testing; contaminated substrate; wrong primer; applied in heat or dust |
| Black spots around mirror edges | moisture ingress at cut edge, or solvent attack from an incompatible adhesive |
| Gasket shrinkage at corners | mitred and butted gasket corners instead of vulcanised corners |
| Permanent staining on stored glass | flat, wet, sunlit storage; interleaving powder left in contact with moisture |
