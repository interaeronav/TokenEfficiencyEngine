# Glass types and manufacture

**Summary.** Almost all architectural glass begins life as soda-lime-silica float glass, and every subsequent product — toughened, laminated, coated, mirrored — is a secondary process applied to that substrate. This file covers the float process itself, the three heat states (annealed, heat-strengthened, fully toughened), lamination and interlayer chemistry, the special glasses (wired, patterned, low-iron, mirror), the standard thicknesses and sheet sizes actually available in southern Africa, cutting and edgework, and the nickel-sulphide spontaneous-breakage problem with its only mitigation, heat soak testing.

## 1. The float process

Pilkington's float process (commercialised 1959) replaced grinding and polishing with a physical trick: pour molten glass onto a bath of molten tin and let gravity and surface tension produce two parallel, fire-polished faces.

The sequence at PFG's Springs plant:

1. **Batching.** Silica sand, soda ash (Na₂CO₃, the flux that lowers melting point), dolomite and limestone (the stabilisers supplying MgO and CaO), sodium sulphate (a refining agent) and **cullet** — recycled glass, which reduces energy demand — are mixed.
2. **Melting.** The batch is melted at around **1 500 °C** in a continuous regenerative furnace. Furnaces run without interruption for years; PFG runs its two lines 365 days a year.
3. **Float bath.** A continuous ribbon of molten glass floats on molten tin in a reducing (nitrogen/hydrogen) atmosphere so the tin does not oxidise. Glass enters at about **1 100 °C**. Irregularities melt out; the ribbon reaches an equilibrium thickness of about 6–7 mm if left alone. **Thickness is controlled by pulling or restraining the ribbon** — top rollers stretch it thinner or restrain it thicker — which is why standard thicknesses cluster around discrete manufactured values rather than being continuously available.
4. **Annealing lehr.** The ribbon leaves the tin at about **600 °C** and passes through a lehr roughly **100 m** long, cooling in a controlled gradient to about **200 °C** so that residual stress is relieved. Skipping this would leave the sheet uncuttable.
5. **Cold end.** Washing, on-line inspection, automatic cutting to stock sizes, stacking.

Two practical consequences of the float process are worth carrying forward:

- **Every float sheet has a tin side and an air side.** The face that lay on the tin bath carries a microscopic tin diffusion layer, detectable with a UV lamp. It matters for coating (pyrolytic coatings are applied on-line, sputtered coatings off-line), for mirror silvering, and occasionally for laminating adhesion.
- **Standard clear float is not colourless.** Iron oxide in the sand gives it a green cast, visible on the edge and cumulative through thickness. Low-iron glass exists specifically to remove it.

## 2. The three heat states

### Annealed glass

The as-manufactured state. Cuttable, drillable, edge-workable. Breaks into large sharp shards and is **not** a safety glass. Modest strength; low resistance to thermal stress. Use where the glass will not be impacted, is not in a critical location, and thermal gradients are low.

### Heat-strengthened glass (EN 1863)

Reheated to near the softening point and then cooled more slowly than toughened glass, producing an intermediate level of surface compression. Roughly twice the strength of annealed. Critically, **heat-strengthened glass breaks like annealed glass — into large fragments that stay in the frame or in the laminate**. That makes it the preferred substrate for laminated overhead glazing, balustrades and structural applications, where post-breakage retention matters more than fragment size. It is also far less susceptible to nickel-sulphide spontaneous fracture than fully toughened glass, because the residual tensile stress in the core is lower. Heat-strengthened glass is **not** a safety glass in its own right; it becomes one when laminated.

### Fully toughened / tempered glass (EN 12150)

Heated to around 620–650 °C then quenched rapidly with air jets. The surfaces set first and go into compression; the core, cooling later, goes into tension. Any crack must overcome the surface compression before it can propagate, giving roughly **four times** the strength of annealed glass.

- ASTM C1048-18 requires, for 6 mm fully tempered glass, a minimum surface compression of **69 MPa** or an edge compression of not less than **67 MPa**. For classification as a safety glass the surface compressive stress should exceed **100 MPa**.
- **Fragmentation** is the defining safety property: on breakage the stored energy dices the pane into small blunt granules. EN 12150-1 verifies this by impacting an **1 100 × 360 mm** specimen with a pointed tool at the mid-point of the longest edge and counting fragments in the **50 × 50 mm** area of minimum fragment density, **3 to 5 minutes** after breakage.
- **Toughened glass cannot be cut, drilled or edge-worked after toughening.** All cutting, notching, drilling and edge finishing must be complete before the glass enters the furnace. This is the single most common cause of site rework in glazing.
- **Roller wave and bow.** Horizontal toughening lines leave a slight periodic distortion from the roller contact. It is visible in reflection on large facade panes and must be anticipated on a mirrored or highly reflective specification.

## 3. Laminated glass

Two or more sheets bonded by a polymer interlayer under heat and pressure. PFG's process: clean the float glass, assemble glass/interlayer/glass, pass through nip rollers to expel air and form a pre-bond, then autoclave under heat and pressure to complete the lamination.

### Nomenclature

A laminate is named by its total nominal thickness including the interlayer: **3 mm + 0.38 mm PVB + 3 mm = 6.38 mm**. The southern African range is therefore 4.38, 5.38, 6.38, 8.38, 10.38, 12.38 mm for single-interlayer PVB laminates, with 6.76, 7.52, 10.76, 12.76 and 13.52 mm appearing where thicker interlayers are used.

### Interlayers

| Interlayer | Character | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **PVB** (polyvinyl butyral) | Standard. Tough, high elongation, low shear modulus, adheres strongly to glass, blocks essentially all UV | General safety and security glazing, shopfronts, laminated facades |
| **Acoustic PVB** | Softer, damping-modified core, usually 0.50 mm | Acoustic laminates (PFG "Soundprufe") |
| **Ionoplast / SGP (SentryGlas)** | Very much stiffer — reported shear modulus roughly **100×** that of standard PVB — and far stronger in post-breakage retention | Balustrades, glass floors, structural glass, hurricane and blast glazing |
| **Stiff PVB** | Intermediate; shear modulus comparable to ionomer **up to about 30 °C** | Balustrades where cost rules out ionoplast |
| **EVA** | Thermoset; can block up to **99.9%** of UV; tolerant of encapsulated inserts | Decorative laminates, fabric/mesh inclusions, some solar |
| **Cast-in-place resin** | Poured and cured between panes | Curved or awkward assemblies, retrofit |

The temperature dependence of interlayer stiffness is not a footnote in this climate. Ionoplast retains stiffness at facade temperatures where PVB has effectively none. For any laminate carrying load — a balustrade, a fin, a floor — the design must be done at the actual service temperature, not at 20 °C.

## 4. The special glasses

**Wired glass.** Annealed glass with a welded steel mesh cast into the ribbon (EN 572-3 polished wired, EN 572-6 wired patterned). Its historical role was fire integrity — the mesh holds the pane in place after the glass cracks. It is **weaker than unwired glass of the same thickness** and, in impact, the exposed wire ends can aggravate injury. The US International Building Code effectively banned wired glass in impact locations in 2006. Treat wired glass as a legacy product: for fire-rated glazing specify a modern fire-resistant glass (Pilkington Pyrostop, AGC Pyrobel and equivalents); for impact resistance specify a safety glass.

**Patterned / obscured glass.** Rolled rather than floated, with a texture impressed by a patterned roller (EN 572-5). Supplies privacy and light diffusion in bathrooms, screens and partitions. It cannot be assumed to be a safety glass; **patterned annealed glass is explicitly a separate category from laminated and toughened safety glass** in SANS 10400-N. Where it is used in a critical location it must be toughened or laminated.

**Low-iron ("extra-clear") glass.** Made from sand with very low Fe₂O₃, removing the green cast and lifting visible transmittance by several percentage points over standard float. Sold as Pilkington Optiwhite, Saint-Gobain Diamant, Guardian UltraClear, AGC Clearsight. Specify it where the edge of the glass is visible (frameless balustrades, glass fins, display cases, glass stairs), where colour fidelity matters (retail, galleries, museum vitrines), or where multiple laminated plies would otherwise compound the green. The Grand Canyon Skywalk deck is four plies of Saint-Gobain Diamant low-iron glass with SentryGlas interlayers precisely because a four-ply standard float deck would look like pond water.

**Mirror.** Float glass silvered on one face, protected by copper (traditional) or copper-free layers plus backing paint. PFG operates an ISO 14001 mirror plant and publishes a copper-free mirror range with its own storage, handling and installation guidance and warranty. Mirror is vulnerable at the edges — moisture attacks the silver from a cut edge — so edge sealing and ventilated fixing matter more than for plain glass. Never bond mirror with an incompatible adhesive; solvent attack on the backing paint produces black blooms that appear months later.

## 5. Thicknesses, sizes and tolerances

- **Manufactured thicknesses** in common architectural use: 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 15, 19 mm (PFG's float line covers 1.8–12 mm; thicker sheet is imported).
- **Jumbo sheet** off a float line is typically 6 000 × 3 210 mm internationally; PFG's published stock sheet is **3 660 × 2 440 mm**.
- **The stock sheet is the real design module.** Sizing a facade panel so that two panels cut cleanly from a stock sheet with normal edge trim is worth more to a budget than almost any other decision.
- Thickness, dimensional, flatness and edge tolerances are given in **EN 572-8** (supplied and final cut sizes) and the product standards for the processed products (EN 12150-1, EN 1863-1, EN ISO 12543-2). Always quote a tolerance standard in the specification rather than a bare dimension.

## 6. Cutting and edgework

Annealed glass is scored with a carbide or diamond wheel and run (snapped) along the score. Everything else — shape cutting, notches, holes, cut-outs — happens at this stage, **before** any heat treatment.

Edge finishes, in ascending cost:

| Finish | Description | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Cut / as-cut** | Raw, sharp, contains micro-flaws | Hidden in frame only; never on toughened work |
| **Arrised / seamed** | Sharp arris removed with a belt | Minimum acceptable for framed glazing and handling safety |
| **Ground / flat ground** | Uniform matt edge | Exposed edges in frames; pre-toughening standard |
| **Smoothed** | Ground then lightly polished | Semi-exposed |
| **Polished** | Fully transparent bright edge | Frameless glazing, balustrades, fins, shelves, mirrors |
| **Mitred / bevelled** | Angled edge | Mirrors, glass-to-glass corners |

Edge quality is a **structural** matter, not a cosmetic one. Glass fails from surface and edge flaws in tension. An as-cut edge has far lower strength than a ground and polished edge, which is why thermal-stress calculations and lateral-load calculations to EN 16612 distinguish edge condition explicitly. Any pane that will see thermal gradients or edge tension should be specified ground or polished as a minimum.

## 7. Nickel sulphide and heat soak testing

The most important failure mode in toughened glass is not impact — it is **spontaneous fracture from nickel sulphide inclusions**, years or decades after installation.

**Mechanism.** Trace nickel (from contamination in the batch or furnace hardware) combines with sulphur to form NiSₓ inclusions, with x reported between about **0.9 and 1.06** depending on residence time and temperature history. The α-phase is stable above **379 °C**. Rapid quenching in the toughening furnace freezes the inclusion in the α-phase. Over time — accelerated by facade temperatures in a hot climate — it converts to the β-phase with a **2–4% volume increase**. If the inclusion sits in the tensile core of a toughened pane and is large enough, that expansion initiates fracture. The signature is the **"figure eight"** or butterfly break origin, each loop about **30 mm** across.

**Statistics that matter.** Only about **15%** of the original NiS population survives into the finished glass, because larger particles settle out in the furnace; observable inclusions top out around **500 µm**. Critical size is roughly **50 µm** for detection under heat soak conditions and about **75 µm** for the in-service building situation.

**Heat soak testing (HST) to EN 14179-1** is the only industrial mitigation. Toughened panes are stacked in a calibrated furnace and held at **(280 ± 10) °C** for a **2-hour** dwell, with controlled heating and cooling phases. Critical inclusions convert and fracture the pane in the furnace rather than in the facade. The standard's design target was a residual risk of no more than **one breakage in 400 tonnes** of heat-soaked glass; Saint-Gobain field data from 2011–2017 suggests the real figure is closer to **one in 6 000 tonnes**, roughly fifteen times better than the standard assumes.

> ⚠️ **Specify heat soak testing to EN 14179-1 for every fully toughened pane that is overhead, in a balustrade, in a frameless assembly, at height above a public route, or where replacement would be disproportionately difficult.** HST is not free and it is not universal in southern African supply — it must be called for explicitly, and the test certificates must be required as a deliverable. Note also that HST is not a treatment applied to already-installed glass, and it degrades nothing: it is a destructive screening test on a batch.

Where the consequence of spontaneous fracture is unacceptable and HST capacity is not available, the correct answer is **heat-strengthened laminated glass**, which has both a lower residual core tension and, being laminated, retains its fragments.

## Open questions

- PFG publishes iron oxide content as **0.075%** on its "All about glass" page; a second rendering of the same table gives **0.75%**. The lower figure is consistent with ordinary clear float; treat 0.075% as the likely correct value but verify with PFG technical services.
- **EN 12150-1 minimum fragment counts per 50 × 50 mm by thickness were not obtained from a primary source** and are deliberately not stated here. Read the standard before quoting them.
- Toughening furnace temperatures (620–650 °C) are given as the industry range and were not verified from a manufacturer document in this research pass.
- PFG's current thickness and size availability may have changed since the 2016 brochure; confirm with PFG or PG Building Glass before specifying.
