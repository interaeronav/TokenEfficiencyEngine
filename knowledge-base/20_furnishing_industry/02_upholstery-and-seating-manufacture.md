# Upholstery and seating manufacture — frames, suspension, foam, fill and quality judgement

**Summary.** A sofa is four systems stacked: a **frame** that carries load into the floor, a **suspension** that spans the frame and provides the first stage of give, a **foam and fill package** that provides comfort and shape, and a **cover** that takes the wear. Price differences between a R15,000 and a R150,000 sofa are almost entirely in the first three; the cover is the part the buyer looks at and the part that can be replaced. Flexible polyurethane foam accounts for 67 % of all polyurethane produced, and its density and load-deflection behaviour are the single most predictive numbers for how a seat will feel in year five.

## Key facts

- Foam is specified by **two independent numbers**: density (kg/m³ — how much polymer per unit volume, predicts durability) and hardness/ILD-IFD (N or lbf at 25 % or 40 % compression — predicts feel). **Density is durability; ILD is firmness. They are not the same thing.**
- Trade guideline seat-foam densities: **≥ 30 kg/m³** for occasional use, **35–45 kg/m³** for daily domestic, **45–55 kg/m³ HR** for contract/hospitality (`needs-verification` — trade practice, not a published standard).
- Eight-way hand-tied springs are the traditional premium suspension; sinuous (serpentine, "no-sag") springs are the volume standard; rubber/elastic webbing (Pirelli-type) is the modern European standard used by most Italian and Scandinavian makers.
- **CAL TB 117-2013** replaced the open-flame test with a **smoulder resistance** regime (cigarette on fabric and foam) and no longer effectively requires chemical flame retardants in furniture foam.
- **[UK/EU]** BS 5852 defines ignition sources (0 = cigarette, 1 = match equivalent, 2–7 = increasing wood-crib severity); EN 1021-1 (cigarette) and EN 1021-2 (match) are the European upholstery ignitability tests.
- Martindale thresholds in general use: **10,000–15,000 domestic**, **25,000–35,000 office/commercial**, **200,000–500,000** for extreme-duty applications.

## 1. Frame construction

| Frame type | Description | Typical tier | Expected life |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kiln-dried hardwood, doweled and corner-blocked** | Beech, ash, maple or (locally) saligna/kiaat, dried to ~8–12 % MC, joints doweled or mortised, glued, screwed and corner-blocked | Premium / bespoke | 25+ years |
| **Engineered hardwood plywood (11–18 ply)** | Laminated ply rails, CNC-cut, glued and screwed; very stable, does not split at fixings | Premium contract (much US and Italian production) | 20+ years |
| **Hardwood/ply hybrid** | Ply panels with solid rails at load points | Good mid-market | 12–20 years |
| **Steel frame** | Welded tube or box section, often with rubber webbing | Contract, some Italian designer (also for very thin profiles) | 20+ years; watch weld quality |
| **Softwood, stapled** | Pine or SA pine, butt joints, staples, no corner blocks | Budget | 3–7 years |
| **Particleboard / MDF elements** | Panels for arms and plinths | Budget to mid | Fails at fixings under racking loads |

**What to check physically:** lift one front corner 150 mm off the floor — the opposite front leg should lift with it. If the frame twists, it has no diagonal stiffness. Rock the arms: any creak or lateral play means loose or stapled joints. Look under the base cloth: you should see corner blocks, glue squeeze-out and screws, not staples alone.

**Joinery and reinforcement.** Good practice: dowelled or mortise-and-tenon rail-to-post joints, glued corner blocks screwed in two directions, gussets at arm/back junctions, and a continuous front rail. Legs should be integral to the frame or bolted through with a threaded insert — never screwed into end grain or into board.

## 2. Suspension systems

| System | How it works | Feel | Cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Eight-way hand-tied coil springs** | Individual coil springs stapled to a webbed base, tied in eight directions with Italian twine | Deep, progressive, "sinking" | Highest labour cost | Traditional English/American premium. Repairable. Adds mass and depth — requires a deep frame |
| **Sinuous / serpentine (no-sag)** | S-shaped steel wire clipped across the frame, cross-tied with helical wire | Firmer, shallower, consistent | Low | The volume-production standard worldwide. Quality depends on wire gauge (8–9 gauge good; 11–12 gauge budget), spacing (≤ 100 mm centres) and edge clips |
| **Rubber/elastic webbing (Pirelli-type)** | Rubber-and-fabric elastic straps interwoven or parallel, clipped or stapled into rebates | Even, taut, modern; less deep | Low–medium | Standard on Italian and Scandinavian designer seating. Elastomer ages: expect softening over 10–15 years, faster in heat |
| **Non-elastic webbing (jute/polypropylene)** | Woven strapping as a base under springs or foam | Firm, minimal give | Very low | As a base for coil springs (correct) or as the whole suspension (budget) |
| **Pocket springs in the seat** | Individual springs in fabric pockets, as in a mattress | Very even support | Medium | Increasingly used in mid-market "spring cushions" |
| **Solid platform (plywood or board)** | No suspension at all; foam directly on board | Hard, dead | Lowest | Common in budget "boxy" sofas and daybeds |

## 3. Foam grades and cushion construction

### Foam
Flexible polyurethane foam is made by reacting a diisocyanate with a polyol; water is used as a blowing agent, reacting with isocyanate to release CO₂ and produce an open-cell structure. Open-cell foams are soft and air-permeable, which is what makes them suitable for seating.

| Grade | Density (kg/m³) | Typical use | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low-density conventional | 16–22 | Packaging, throwaway cushions, cheap scatter | Collapses within 1–2 years of daily use |
| Standard conventional | 22–30 | Budget seating, back cushions | Acceptable in backs, poor in seats |
| High-density conventional | 30–38 | Domestic seat cushions | The realistic mid-market minimum |
| **High resilience (HR)** | 35–55 | Quality domestic and contract seats | Different chemistry (higher support factor); recovers better and lasts longer at the same density |
| Viscoelastic (memory) | 45–85 | Mattress comfort layers, some arm pads | Temperature-sensitive — **behaves noticeably firmer in cold and softer in heat**; poor choice for a room that swings 15 °C in a day |
| Latex (natural/blended) | 60–95 | Premium seat and mattress cores | Durable, breathable, heavy, expensive; can be sensitive to UV and ozone |
| Reconstituted / chip foam | 80–140 | Bases, gym mats, cheap platform layers | Very firm, very durable, no comfort |

**The two-number rule.** Ask any supplier for *both* the density and the hardness. "High density" alone means nothing; some marketing "high density" foams are 25 kg/m³. A 35 kg/m³ / medium ILD foam will still be supporting in year 10; a 20 kg/m³ / same-ILD foam will feel identical on the showroom floor and be dead in year two.

### Fill and wrap
- **Dacron / polyester fibre wrap** — 100–500 g/m² polyester batting bonded or loose-wrapped around the foam core. It softens the arris of the foam, fills the cover, and gives the "crown". It compacts over time — the commonest cause of a cushion looking flat while the foam is fine.
- **Feather and down.** Down is the plumule from waterfowl (no quill); feather has a quill. Ratios are quoted as down:feather, e.g. **10/90 (mostly feather, supportive, cheap)**, **50/50**, **90/10 (mostly down, soft, luxurious, needs daily plumping)**. Fill power measures loft. Down alone in a seat cushion collapses; the standard premium construction is a **foam core wrapped in a feather/down chamber**.
- **Chambered/baffled construction** — the cushion cover is divided into chambers so the fill cannot migrate. A serious quality marker on a feather-wrapped cushion.
- **Hollow-fibre / ball fibre** — polyester substitute for down; cheaper, non-allergenic, compacts faster.

### Cushion constructions, ranked
1. **Foam core + down/feather chamber wrap + baffled ticking** — premium, needs plumping.
2. **HR foam core + bonded dacron wrap** — the sensible durable default.
3. **Pocket springs + foam + fibre wrap** — even support, heavier.
4. **Solid foam, single density, no wrap** — budget; edges wear first.
5. **All-fibre cushions** — cheapest; flattens fast.

**Back cushions** are usually softer: fibre, feather, or low-density foam plus fibre. Loose scatter backs are more comfortable but require constant plumping; attached backs look neater longer.

## 4. The upholstery process, step by step

1. **Frame assembly** — cut, machine, dowel/tenon, glue, clamp, screw, corner-block, sand.
2. **Suspension** — webbing stretched and tacked/stapled; springs clipped or tied; edge wire fitted.
3. **Base/undercover** — hessian or spring cover over the suspension.
4. **Edge roll / crown** — a firm edge (traditionally stitched edge roll in horsehair or fibre; modern: a firmer foam strip) that defines the profile and stops the front edge collapsing.
5. **Foam cutting and shaping** — CNC or hot-wire cut; contour-cut for shaped arms; multi-density laminations glued up.
6. **Fibre wrap and stockinette** — polyester wrap adhered, then a stretchy stockinette layer to let the cover slide.
7. **Cutting and sewing the cover** — pattern nesting, cutting (die or CNC knife), sewing with matched thread, piping/welting or top-stitch detailing, zips fitted.
8. **Upholstering** — cover pulled, aligned to the grain/pattern and stapled; tension is the craft.
9. **Trimming** — piping, studs, skirts, base cloth (bottom dust cover), legs fitted.
10. **QA and packing** — dimension check, function check, cover alignment, packing with corner protection.

**Fixed vs. loose covers.** Loose (removable) covers cost roughly 15–30 % more and require more fabric and more sewing, but transform the sofa's service life: they can be cleaned, replaced or remade. In a dusty, sunlit climate this is a genuinely good investment. Specify **pre-shrunk fabric** for loose covers or expect a fight after the first wash.

## 5. Judging quality at a price point

| Price tier (indicative, 3-seat sofa) | Frame | Suspension | Foam | Cover | Realistic life |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Budget (R8,000–20,000) | Stapled softwood + board | Serpentine, wide spacing, or platform | 20–28 kg/m³ | PU "leather" or low-rub polyester | 3–6 years |
| Mid (R20,000–60,000) | Hardwood/ply hybrid, some blocks | Serpentine, good gauge, or elastic webbing | 30–38 kg/m³ + dacron | 25,000+ Martindale poly/blend | 8–15 years |
| Upper-mid (R60,000–140,000) | Kiln-dried hardwood or 11+ ply, doweled | Elastic webbing or 8-way tied | HR 35–45 kg/m³, layered, some feather | 40,000+ Martindale, wool/linen blends, semi-aniline leather | 15–25 years |
| Designer/luxury (R140,000+) | Engineered, often with steel; cold-cure moulded foam (B&B Italia invested €4 m in an in-house foaming department in 2010) | Engineered per model | Multi-density moulded, feather chambers | Designer houses, full-grain aniline, exchangeable covers | 25+ years, re-upholsterable |

**Field test in a showroom (5 minutes):**
- Sit and slide to the front edge — does the edge collapse or hold?
- Press the seat corners — is the foam supported to the very edge, or only in the middle?
- Lift the sofa at one corner — does it twist?
- Unzip a cushion — is the foam wrapped, is the wrap bonded, is the ticking chambered?
- Look at pattern match across seams, and at the alignment of piping.
- Ask for the **foam density and hardness figures in writing**. A maker who cannot supply them is telling you something.

## 7. Fire regulations for upholstery

| Regime | Jurisdiction | Test basis | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **EN 1021-1 / -2** | EU | Ignition source 0 (smouldering cigarette) and source 1 (match flame equivalent) | The baseline for European contract and domestic upholstery |
| **BS 5852** | UK | Ignition sources 0–7 (cigarette, match, then wood cribs 2–7) | Crib 5 is the standard for UK contract/hospitality |
| **UK Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations 1988** | UK | Requires filling and cover ignitability performance and a permanent label | The strictest domestic regime in the world; under long-running review |
| **CAL TB 117-2013** | California / de facto US | 45-minute smoulder test with cigarette on cover and foam; open-flame requirement removed | Ended the effective mandate for chemical flame retardants; SB 1019 introduced FR-content labelling |
| **CAL TB 133** | California public occupancies | Full-scale burn test | For assembly occupancies |
| **[ZA] SANS** | South Africa | SANS standards for upholstered furniture flammability exist but the specific number and its scope were **not verified** — `needs-verification` | Hospitality projects in SA generally specify to BS 5852 crib 5 or EN 1021 |

> ⚠️ **[NA] [ZA]** For any guest house, lodge, hotel or public building, do not accept "domestic" upholstery. Specify EN 1021-1 and -2 as a minimum and BS 5852 crib 5 where the local fire officer or insurer requires it, and require **test certificates for the exact cover/filling combination** — a fabric certified over one foam is not certified over another. Fire retardant interliners (FR barrier cloth) are the usual route to compliance without treating the show fabric.
