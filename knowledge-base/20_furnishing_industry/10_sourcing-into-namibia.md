# Sourcing furnishings into Namibia — availability, imports, lead times and the decision framework

**Summary.** Furnishing a house in Namibia is a three-bucket problem: **what you can buy in Windhoek today, what must come up from South Africa, and what must be imported from outside SACU.** The single structural advantage is that Namibia and South Africa sit inside the same customs union, so South African goods cross the border without customs duty — which makes South Africa, not Europe or China, the natural first source for almost everything. The single structural disadvantage is that Namibia is a small, distant market, so specialist items carry long lead times, thin stockholding and no local spares.

## Key facts

- **SACU** (Botswana, Eswatini, Lesotho, Namibia, South Africa; established **1910**; secretariat in **Windhoek**) maintains a **common external tariff** and the **free interchange of goods between member countries**. Duties collected go into South Africa's National Revenue Fund and are redistributed by formula.
- **[NA]** Customs and tax are administered by the **Namibia Revenue Agency (NamRA)**, established under the Namibia Revenue Agency Act, which assesses and collects taxes and duties and administers customs and excise.
- **No IKEA anywhere in sub-Saharan Africa** (504 stores in 60 countries as of April 2025) — the budget furniture tier is filled by South African retail.
- **[NA] Import VAT still applies** to goods entering Namibia from South Africa even though customs duty does not — the movement is a cross-border supply for VAT purposes. **VAT rate and the exact mechanism must be confirmed with NamRA or a clearing agent — not stated on the NamRA page fetched.** `needs-verification`.
- Sea freight Europe/Asia → **Walvis Bay** typically runs **4–8 weeks port-to-port** plus loading and clearing; road freight Johannesburg/Cape Town → Windhoek typically **2–5 days**. `needs-verification` — see domain 11.

## 1. The three buckets

### Bucket A — available in Namibia
| Category | Reality |
|---|---|
| Bespoke joinery (kitchens, wardrobes, doors, built-ins) | Windhoek, Swakopmund, Okahandja workshops. The default and usually the right answer |
| Upholstery: new build and re-covering | Local upholsterers work to order |
| Steel and aluminium fabrication (bases, frames, screens, balustrades) | Good local capability |
| Stone: granite and quartz worktop fabrication | Local fabricators, working from imported slabs |
| Curtains and blinds — making-up and installation | Local workrooms buying SA fabric |
| Paint, plaster, tile adhesive, general building finishes | Full local supply |
| Standard tile, sanitaryware, taps, geysers | Local retail (SA-sourced ranges) |
| Standard appliances (Defy, Samsung, LG, Bosch, Hisense) | Local retail with service support |
| Standard lighting (Eurolux/Radiant/K.Light-type ranges) | Local electrical wholesalers |
| Hardware (Blum/Hettich/Häfele stock items) | Through SA agents, held by local joinery suppliers |

### Bucket B — must come from South Africa
| Category | Reality |
|---|---|

### Bucket C — must be imported from outside SACU
| Category | Reality |
|---|---|
| Italian/Nordic designer furniture | Direct or via an SA agent; 14–20 weeks + freight |
| German/Italian system kitchens and wardrobes | Via SA dealer; flat-packed for containerisation |
| Specialist architectural lighting not agented locally | Direct order; allow spares |
| Sintered stone and specialist slabs not held in SA | Container quantities |
| Specialist ironmongery (FSB, d line, Olivari) | Long lead, order early |
| Rugs (hand-knotted) | Direct from source or via a dealer |

## 2. How importing into Namibia works

**Between SACU members (SA → NA):**
1. **No customs duty** — SACU free interchange of goods.
2. A **customs declaration is still required** at the border; a clearing agent or the transporter handles it.
3. **Import VAT is payable in Namibia** on entry (mechanism and rate to confirm with NamRA). A registered Namibian VAT vendor can usually claim it as input tax; a private individual cannot.
4. Documentation: commercial invoice, transport document, and the declaration. Some goods (e.g. certain electrical items) may need additional certification.

**From outside SACU (EU, China, US → NA):**
1. **Customs duty** at the SACU common external tariff rate for the tariff heading, calculated on the customs value (generally CIF).
2. **Import VAT** on the duty-inclusive value.
3. **Clearing**: an agent lodges the declaration (NamRA operates the customs system); documents are the commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading or air waybill, and certificate of origin where a preference is claimed.
4. **Port and terminal charges** at Walvis Bay, plus transport inland.
5. **Possible additional requirements** for specific goods: electrical safety, chemicals, plant/animal material (rattan, untreated timber and leather can attract inspection).

> ⚠️ **Duty rates on furniture, lighting and appliances vary by tariff heading and change.** Do not budget from a remembered percentage. Get the tariff heading and the current rate from a Namibian clearing agent **before** committing to an import — this is a five-minute question that can move a budget by 20 %.

| Appliances — Gaggenau/Miele specials | EU via SA | 8–20 weeks |
| Architectural lighting — European | EU | 8–16 weeks + freight |

**Programme rule:** for an imported item, the honest date is **manufacturing lead time + freight + clearing + inland + a 3-week contingency**. Most furnishing programmes fail because someone quoted only the first number.

## 4. Trade accounts and the designer-discount system

| Mechanism | How it works | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Trade account with a fabric house** | Registered designer/decorator opens an account; buys at trade price and sells at retail | Trade price is commonly **retail less 30–50 %** | The core economics of the SA decorating trade. `needs-verification` on ranges |
| **Trade discount at a furniture retailer** | Registered trade buyer gets a discount off retail | **10–25 %** typical | Often tiered by annual spend |
| **Specifier/architect discount** | Given by lighting and sanitaryware agents to specifiers | **10–30 %** | Frequently contingent on the agent being named on the schedule |
| **Ex-works / direct-from-factory** | Buying direct from a manufacturer, bypassing the local agent | Can save 30–50 % on the local landed price | **You take on freight, clearing, warranty and spares risk.** Often a false economy for anything mechanical |
| **Project pricing** | Negotiated for a whole-house or multi-unit order | 10–30 % beyond standard trade | Ask; the list price is rarely the real price on a whole-house order |
| **Designer fee models** | (a) fee-only with discounts passed to the client; (b) discount-retained as the fee; (c) cost + % | — | **Agree which model applies in writing.** Ambiguity here is the commonest source of client-designer conflict |

**[NA] Practical note:** a Namibian buyer without a trade account is usually better off appointing a designer or buying through a Namibian retailer with SA accounts than trying to open trade accounts directly with SA houses, which generally require registration, references and minimum annual spend.

## 5. Container consolidation for a whole-house furnish

When the imported content of a house exceeds roughly **8–12 m³**, consolidation into a single container usually beats LCL (less-than-container-load) shipments.

| Container | Internal volume (approx.) | Payload (approx.) | Typical furnishing content |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20' standard | ~33 m³ | ~28 t | A kitchen + wardrobes + some loose furniture |
| 40' standard | ~67 m³ | ~26 t | A whole house's imported furniture, or kitchen + wardrobes + tile |
| 40' high cube | ~76 m³ | ~26 t | As above with tall/bulky items |

`needs-verification` — nominal container capacities; confirm with the forwarder.

**How consolidation works in practice:**
1. Appoint a **freight forwarder with a European consolidation warehouse** (typically Italy, Germany or the Netherlands for furniture).
2. Every supplier delivers **ex-works to the consolidation warehouse** by an agreed cut-off date.
3. The forwarder checks, photographs, re-packs where necessary, and loads.
4. One bill of lading, one clearing event, one inland delivery.

**Rules that make it work:**
- **Set the cut-off date and hold it.** One late supplier delays the whole container. Build in a 2–3 week float and be prepared to leave a late item behind for air freight or the next shipment.
- **Order the longest-lead items first** (Italian upholstery, system kitchens, made-to-order lighting), even before the design is fully signed off.
- **Specify export packing** — crated, not just cartoned. Sea freight plus a gravel road is not kind to a flat-packed wardrobe.
- **Insure at replacement value**, all-risks, including inland legs. Standard carrier liability is trivially low.
- **Keep a complete, priced packing list per carton** — clearing depends on it, and so does the insurance claim.
- **Plan the receiving end**: a 40' container needs turning space and a hard standing; if the site cannot take it, break bulk at a Windhoek warehouse.
- **Do not ship into an unfinished house.** Budget for 4–8 weeks of secure, dry, dust-controlled storage.

## 6. The case for local manufacture

| Argument | Detail |
|---|---|
| **No freight, no duty, no clearing** | Removes 15–40 % of landed cost and 6–10 weeks of programme |
| **Fits the actual building** | Namibian houses have thick walls, deep reveals and idiosyncratic dimensions; a made-to-measure unit has no filler panels |
| **Serviceable** | The maker is 20 minutes away when a door drops |
| **Material appropriateness** | A local maker knows what happens to timber in Windhoek in September |
| **Economic** | Keeps the money and the skills in the country |
| **Against: consistency** | Quality varies far more between local workshops than between European factories |
| **Against: hardware** | Local makers still depend on imported hardware — the lead-time risk moves rather than disappears |
| **Against: capacity** | A single workshop cannot deliver a whole house in 8 weeks |
| **Against: design content** | Buying an authored design means buying it from the author |

**Where local manufacture clearly wins:** kitchens, wardrobes, built-in storage, doors, tables, beds, bases and frames, upholstery re-covering, curtains, steel work, stone worktops.
**Where importing clearly wins:** designer seating, engineered mechanisms (office chairs, recliners), lighting, appliances, sanitaryware, brassware, hardware, tile, engineered flooring.

## 7. A decision framework

Ask these questions in order for each line of the schedule:

1. **Can it be made locally to an acceptable standard?** If yes and it is fitted or dimension-specific → make locally. Stop.
2. **Is it available in Namibia off the shelf?** If yes and the specification is met → buy locally. Stop.
3. **Is it available in South Africa?** If yes → buy from SA. No duty, short freight, warranty enforceable in the region. Stop.
4. **Does it have moving parts, electricity or water in it?** If yes and there is no southern African agent → **think very hard before importing.** No agent means no spares, no warranty and no technician.
5. **Is the design content the point?** If yes (an authored chair, a specific luminaire) → import, and order early.
6. **Is the imported volume ≥ ~8–12 m³?** If yes → consolidate into a container. If no → LCL or air freight for small high-value items.
7. **Have you priced the true landed cost?** Ex-works price + export packing + inland to port + sea freight + insurance + port charges + duty + import VAT + clearing fee + inland transport + storage + installation. **Then** compare with the local price.
8. **Have you added the programme?** Manufacture + freight + clearing + inland + 3 weeks.

### Worked comparison (illustrative structure, not real prices)

| Line item | Import from EU | Buy from SA | Make in NA |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ex-works / ex-factory | Base | Base + local margin | Base |
| Customs duty | Applies (SACU CET) | **None (SACU)** | None |
| Import VAT | Applies | Applies | N/A (local VAT) |
| Freight | Sea + inland, 4–8 weeks | Road, 2–5 days | None |
| Clearing/agent | Yes | Minimal | None |
| Insurance | Essential | Advisable | N/A |
| Warranty enforceability | Weak without an agent | Good | Good |
| Spares | Poor | Good | Good |
| Lead time | 18–28 weeks | 2–12 weeks | 4–10 weeks |

> ⚠️ The most expensive furnishing decision in Namibia is not choosing an expensive brand — it is **importing something mechanical with no local agent**. A €4,000 imported oven with no service agent becomes scrap the day the control board fails.
