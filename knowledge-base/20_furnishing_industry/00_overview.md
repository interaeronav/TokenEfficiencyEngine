# The furnishing industry — domain map and supply chain from raw material to installed interior

**Summary.** "Furnishing" is not one industry but roughly nine, each with its own factories, standards, price ladders and lead times: case goods and upholstery, fitted furniture (kitchens and wardrobes), textiles, lighting, floor coverings, sanitaryware and brassware, appliances, hardware, and decorative accessories. They meet only at the point of specification — the schedule an interior designer or architect issues — and again at the point of installation. Understanding the domain means understanding five parallel tiers (luxury/design, contract/hospitality, mid-market retail, budget, bespoke/local maker), each of which has a different value chain, a different margin structure and a radically different lead time. For a house in Namibia, almost every tier except "bespoke/local maker" ends in a container.

## Key facts

- The domain divides into **9 product families** (below), which map onto the files `01`–`09` of this folder.
- Five commercial tiers, with typical **retail multiples on ex-factory cost of 2.0× (contract direct) to 4.0–5.0× (luxury retail)** — trade practice, `needs-verification`.
- Lead times run from **stock (0–2 weeks)** for mass retail to **14–24 weeks** for made-to-order Italian upholstery and German fitted furniture, plus **4–8 weeks sea freight** to Walvis Bay.
- **[NA] [ZA]** Namibia and South Africa are inside the same customs union (SACU, established 1910, headquartered in Windhoek), so goods move between them without customs duty — the single most important commercial fact in this domain.
- IKEA operated **504 stores in 60 countries** as of April 2025 with **€45.1 bn of goods sold in FY2024** — and **no store anywhere in Africa south of the Sahara**, which shapes the whole southern African budget tier.
- A designer-tier sofa maker such as B&B Italia has produced **more than 1,000 designs** since 1966 and reinvests **>3 % of annual sales in R&D**; a village joiner reinvests none. That gap is the tiering.

## 2. The supply chain, stage by stage

**Stage 1 — Raw material.** Logs, hides, quartz sand and polyester resin, bauxite/aluminium billet, steel coil, polyol and isocyanate, cotton and wool bales, polymer chip, clay bodies, LED wafer. Prices here are commodity-driven and move with oil (polymer, foam, polyester), currency and freight.

**Stage 2 — Primary conversion.** Sawmill and board plant (see `06_joinery_and_woodwork/03_sheet-goods-and-boards.md`), tannery (wet blue), foam block plant, spinning and weaving mill, tile press and kiln, LED packaging house, extruder. This stage is capital-intensive, consolidated, and almost entirely outside southern Africa apart from board, some steel, some tile and some foam.

**Stage 3 — Component manufacture.** Drawer boxes and runners, hinges, springs, castors, cut-and-sewn covers, drivers and heat sinks, taps and cartridges. The dominant component suppliers are invisible to the end client but set the quality ceiling: a sofa is only as good as its foam and its suspension; a wardrobe only as good as its hinge and runner.

**Stage 4 — Assembly / final manufacture.** Where a "brand" actually exists. Note that many prestigious brands assemble rather than manufacture: they buy frames, foam, hardware and cloth and add design, engineering, QA and marketing.

**Stage 5 — Distribution.** Three models coexist:
- **Direct-to-trade**: contract manufacturers sell to specifiers with no retail markup.
- **Agent/distributor**: the standard model for southern Africa. A Johannesburg or Cape Town agent holds the franchise for a European brand, imports on their own account, and sells at a landed multiple.
- **Retail**: showroom stock, immediate availability, highest markup.

**Stage 6 — Freight and clearing.** For Namibia: sea freight to Walvis Bay or road freight from Gauteng/Cape Town. See `10_sourcing-into-namibia.md` and cross-reference `11_logistics_remote_areas/02_transport-and-freight-southern-africa.md`.

**Stage 7 — Site delivery, installation, snagging.** The stage most often under-budgeted. Fitted furniture and lighting need trades; loose furniture needs access, protection and a clean, finished building. Delivery into an unfinished house is the commonest cause of damage claims.

## 3. The five tiers

| Tier | Typical brands | Value proposition | Lead time | Typical sofa price band (indicative, `needs-verification`) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Luxury / design** | B&B Italia, Cassina, Minotti, Poliform, Bulthaup, Flos | Authored design, R&D, materials, long product life, resale value | 12–24 weeks + freight | €8,000–25,000+ |
| **Contract / hospitality** | Vitra, Andreu World, Pedrali, Vincent Sheppard, local contract houses | Standards compliance (EN/BIFMA), repairability, batch pricing, FF&E documentation | 8–16 weeks | €1,500–5,000 |
| **Mid-market retail** | Hay, Muuto, &Tradition, West Elm, Article, Weylandts (ZA), Coricraft (ZA) | Design-led but volume-produced, showroom stock | Stock–10 weeks | €900–3,000 |
| **Budget** | IKEA, JYSK, Mr Price Home, Furniture City (ZA) | Price, availability, flat-pack logistics | Stock | €200–900 |
| **Bespoke / local maker** | Houtlander, Pedersen + Lennard, Meyer von Wielligh, David Krynauw (ZA); local joiners (NA) | Fit to the exact space, local timber, repairable, no freight | 6–12 weeks | Highly variable |

Houtlander, for example, works exclusively in FSC-certified American white oak, quotes **6–8 week** made-to-order lead times, and offers a **lifetime structural guarantee** — a good benchmark for what the credible local-maker tier looks like.

## 4. How the tiers differ technically (not just in price)

The honest technical differentiators, in descending order of importance:

1. **Structure and substrate.** Kiln-dried hardwood frames vs. softwood and staples; 18–19 mm moisture-resistant board vs. 16 mm standard MDF/chipboard; brass cartridges vs. plastic.
2. **Hardware.** Blum/Hettich/Grass vs. unbranded. This alone separates a 5-year kitchen from a 25-year kitchen.
3. **Comfort engineering.** Foam density and layering, spring type, seat depth and back angle. This is R&D, and it does not survive copying.
4. **Finish durability.** Two-pack polyurethane vs. single-pack lacquer; UV-cured melamine vs. paper foil.
5. **Tolerances and fit.** Panel and reveal consistency of 1 mm vs. 4 mm.
6. **Documentation and spares.** Whether you can still get a replacement gas lift, hinge or cover in year 12.

## 5. Who does what in a project

| Role | Responsibility | Typical fee/margin basis |
|---|---|---|
| Architect | Fitted elements shown on drawings; coordination of services | % of works |
| Interior designer / decorator | FF&E specification, schedules, sourcing, procurement management | % fee, or trade discount retained, or both |
| Main contractor | Builder's work, first fix, fitted joinery if in contract | Preliminaries + margin |
| Specialist subcontractor | Kitchens, wardrobes, lighting installation, flooring | Supply-and-fit price |
| Supplier / agent | Import, warehousing, delivery | Landed cost multiple |
| Installer / fitter | Assembly, levelling, fixing, snagging | Day rate or % of supply |

> ⚠️ The single most common project failure in furnishing is a **specification/procurement gap**: the designer specifies at luxury tier, the client budgets at mid-market tier, and the substitution happens at site level without re-checking dimensions, fire performance or lead time. Fix it with a priced FF&E schedule signed before ordering.

## 6. The southern African particulars

- **No IKEA.** The budget tier is filled by SA retail chains and by Chinese direct import. This raises the entry price of a furnished house relative to Europe.
- **A real local upholstery industry.** South Africa manufactures sofas at volume; frames, foam and even some fabric are locally available.
- **A strong design-maker scene.** Johannesburg and Cape Town studios (Houtlander, Pedersen + Lennard, Dokter and Misses, Meyer von Wielligh) export internationally and are the most cost-effective route to "designer" furniture in the region because there is no import freight or duty into Namibia.
- **Everything electrical and electronic is imported.** Lighting, appliances and control gear come from Europe or Asia; local content is limited to assembly and distribution.
- **Namibia is a distribution market, not a manufacturing market**, with the exception of joinery, upholstery re-covering, steelwork and stone. See `10_sourcing-into-namibia.md`.
