# Practice models and the business of a small award-winning office

**Summary.** Almost every practice in this domain is small — one to twenty people — and stayed small on purpose. The evidence contradicts the assumption that prizes follow growth: Murcutt has no staff at all, Zumthor keeps about fifteen in a Swiss village, Kéré's Berlin office lists around eight core people, Amateur Architecture Studio is two principals, Radić keeps his Santiago office deliberately intimate. What these practices do instead of growing is choose work ruthlessly, build a second institution alongside the office (a foundation, a school, a research arm), collaborate deeply with engineers, publish, and teach. This file sets out those structural decisions and what each one costs.

## 1. Office size is a design decision, not an outcome

Three stable models appear:

**The sole practitioner (n = 1).** Murcutt works alone, from a Sydney office, without staff, designing each project himself from brief through to site supervision and deliberately avoiding projects large enough to require an office. The economics are brutal and clean: no salaries, therefore no obligation to accept work, therefore total selection control, therefore a very long waiting list and a very high hit rate on quality. The cost is scale — he will never build a hospital — and succession: the practice ends with him. He mitigates that by teaching: he chairs Architecture Foundation Australia and runs the annual Glenn Murcutt International Master Class, which transmits method without transmitting an office.

**The atelier (n ≈ 5–20).** Zumthor's ~15 people in Haldenstein since 1979, Kéré's ~8 in Berlin, Radić's small Santiago studio. This is the size at which a principal can still see and correct every drawing, and at which one bad year is survivable. It requires refusing growth actively — the demand exists, and every project won pulls toward hiring.

**The studio-plus-institution (n ≈ 20–60 with a second body).** Doshi's Vastushilpa Consultants grew to five partners and about sixty staff — but the more important structure is what he built around it: a school of architecture (1962), a research foundation (Vastu-Shilpa Foundation, 1978), and CEPT (1966; university status 2002). Aravena's ELEMENTAL is the sharpest version: a "Do Tank" constituted in 2001 with corporate and university shareholders precisely so that social housing, which cannot pay a normal fee, can be done at professional standard.

**Rule of thumb.** Decide your maximum headcount before you need it, write it down, and treat exceeding it as a failure requiring a decision, not as success.

## 2. Selecting work

The single strongest common feature of these practices is that they **decline more than they accept**.

- Murcutt refuses large and institutional work outright.
- Zumthor takes very few commissions and accepts them personally.
- Grafton named itself after a street to signal that the work — universities, schools, housing — is the identity, and has stayed in a narrow programmatic band for four decades.
- ELEMENTAL made an explicit decision to work on public-interest problems, and structured its ownership so that decision was affordable.

For a young practice with no leverage, the practical version of selection is a **written intake filter** — four or five questions applied to every enquiry before quoting: Is there a real budget? Is the decision-maker in the room? Does the project let us do something we want to be known for? Can we get to site within a day's drive? Would we put it in the portfolio? Two "no"s and you decline, politely, with a referral. Declining work is how a portfolio acquires a shape, and a shape is what gets you commissioned.

## 3. Fee models

> ⚠️ Do not take fee percentages from this file. Statutory and recommended fee tariffs are jurisdictional and change annually — see `01_architecture/07_professional-practice.md` for the **[ZA]** SACAP/professional fee guideline framework and the **[NA]** position under the Architects' and Quantity Surveyors' Act 13 of 1979.

The structural points, which do not change:

1. **Percentage-of-cost fees punish efficiency and cheap building.** A practice specialising in low-cost, low-material work is paid least for exactly the work that takes longest. Kéré, Aravena, Lacaton & Vassal and Lari all operate in this trap, and all solve it the same way: **non-fee income streams** — foundations, grants, teaching, prizes, publishing, and corporate/university partners.
2. **Time-based fees require you to actually record time.** Most small practices lose money because they do not know which project types are unprofitable. A one-line daily timesheet per person per project is the minimum viable financial instrument.
3. **Stage-based payment with a mobilisation payment up front** is what keeps a small office solvent. Work not paid for by stage is work financed by the architect.
4. **Separate the paid work from the positioning work and budget for both.** Counterspace was a research practice from 2015 and was commissioned for the Serpentine Pavilion in 2020 on the strength of a research position, not a building portfolio. That research was, in accounting terms, marketing — and it worked.

## 4. Competitions as a business channel

Competitions are the main route by which unknown practices get large commissions, and the main way small practices lose money. Both are true.

The tradition is deep — Brunelleschi won the Florence Cathedral dome by competition in 1419; open competition emerged in the late eighteenth century; nineteenth-century England ran over 2 500 competitions in five decades; RIBA issued rules in 1839 and formal regulations in 1872, and the UIA now provides the international framework. Formats matter commercially:

- **Open, anonymous, single-stage** — cheapest to enter, lowest odds, best for building a position.
- **Two-stage with paid shortlist** — the only format a small practice should enter at scale, because stage two is funded by an honorarium.
- **Invited** — usually requires a track record and often a fee, and is the format that follows a prize or a publication.
- **Ideas competitions** — no building, but a deadline, a defined problem and published comparison. Use these as training (see `04`).

Piano and Rogers won the Centre Pompidou by open competition and it defined both careers. The discipline for a small office: enter no more than one large competition a year unless it is paid, and treat the entry cost as a marketing budget line with a decided ceiling.

## 5. Collaboration with engineers

The best small practices do not merely appoint engineers; they co-invent with one.

Renzo Piano's Building Workshop is built around it — the practice's defining relationship was with the engineers of Ove Arup, and each project produced a *piece* invented jointly: the Pompidou's gerberettes, the Menil Collection's light leaves (1987), Kansai Airport's 1.7 km roof (1988–94), the ventilated timber *cases* of the Tjibaou Cultural Centre in Nouméa (1998). Shigeru Ban collaborated with **Frei Otto** on the Japan Pavilion at Expo 2000 in Hanover, and pursued regulatory approval of paper-tube structure until Japan's Minister of Construction certified it in 1995 — that is an engineering campaign, not a consultancy appointment.

Practical form for a small office: pick **one** structural engineer and **one** services engineer and work with them repeatedly, at a loss on the first two jobs if necessary. Bring them in at concept, not at tender. Ask them what they have always wanted to try. In a market like Namibia, where the pool of consultants is small, this is easier to do than in London — and it is the cheapest available source of technical differentiation.

## 6. Publishing, teaching and reputation

Reputation in this field is manufactured deliberately, and the mechanisms are cheap:

- **Writing.** Liu Jiakun was a **novelist** before he was a known architect, and published *Narrative Discourse and Low-Tech Strategy* (1997), *Now and Here* (2002), *I Built in West China?* (2009) and *The Conception of Brightmoon* (2014); his low-tech position was argued in print for two decades before the 2025 Pritzker. Moneo co-founded the magazine *Arquitectura Bis* in the late 1970s. Zumthor's two short books do more reputational work than most monographs.
- **Teaching.** Farrell and McNamara taught at UCD from 1976 to 2006 and became adjunct professors in 2015; they held the Kenzo Tange Chair at Harvard GSD (2010) and the Louis Kahn Chair at Yale (2011), and describe teaching as "a parallel reality… a way of trying to distill our experience". Moneo chaired the Harvard GSD architecture department 1984–90. Siza taught at Porto from 1966 and became professor in 1976. Doshi founded a university. Aravena has held the ELEMENTAL Copec Chair at Universidad Católica since 2006. Kéré holds a chair at TU Munich (from 2017) and has taught at Harvard GSD. Teaching is income, recruitment, research and reputation simultaneously.
- **Curating and exhibiting.** Aravena directed the 2016 Venice Biennale; Grafton curated the 2018 one; Vally was assistant curator of the South African Pavilion in 2014 and artistic director of the first Islamic Arts Biennale in Jeddah in 2023.
- **Giving the work away.** ELEMENTAL published the construction drawings for several incremental housing types as free downloads. This maximises influence at the direct cost of fee — and it is why "half a good house" is now built by people who have never met Aravena.

For a practice in a small market, the local versions are exact: write for the professional journal, teach a studio at NUST, curate one exhibition, publish one measured-drawing survey, and put a real technical document (a detail set, a specification, a climate analysis) into public circulation each year.

## 7. The second institution

The recurring structural move worth copying above all others is that these practices build a **non-commercial twin**:

| Practice | Twin | Founded |
|---|---|---|
| Kéré Architecture | Schulbausteine für Gando e.V. → Kéré Foundation | 1998 (before the practice) |
| Shigeru Ban Architects | Voluntary Architects' Network | 1995 |
| Vastushilpa Consultants | Vastu-Shilpa Foundation for Studies and Research in Environmental Design | 1978 |
| David Chipperfield Architects | Fundación RIA, Galicia | 2017 |
| Counterspace | *Support Structures for Support Structures* grant programme | 2021 |
| Heritage Foundation of Pakistan (Lari) | the foundation *is* the practice after retirement in 2000 | 1980 |

The twin does three things a firm cannot: it accepts grant and donor money, it holds the research and the advocacy, and it survives the principal. Kéré's is the instructive sequence — **association (1998) → demonstration building (2001) → Aga Khan Award (2004) → practice (2005) → Pritzker (2022)**. The non-profit came first.

## 8. From sole practitioner to studio

The transition breaks most practices. What the record suggests:

1. **Do not hire until you have twelve months of committed fee income**, not twelve months of promising conversations.
2. **The first hire is a technologist, not a designer.** The bottleneck in a one-person practice is production and site administration, not ideas.
3. **The second hire is administrative.** Most small practices are killed by unbilled invoices and lapsed insurance, not by bad design.
4. **Partner rather than employ, where you can.** Grafton began as five partners and settled at two; Amateur Architecture Studio is two; Lacaton & Vassal is two; Farrell/McNamara, Lacaton/Vassal, Wang Shu/Lu Wenyu and Herzog/de Meuron all show the two-principal firm to be unusually stable. A partner shares risk; an employee is a fixed cost.
5. **Name the practice for its place or its idea, not for yourself, if you intend it to outlive you.** Grafton named itself after a street; Amateur Architecture Studio after a stance; ELEMENTAL after a method. Compare the exposure of a single-name practice: Adjaye Associates lost the Liverpool International Slavery Museum commission, an Africa Institute campus and its role on the UK Holocaust Memorial following allegations against its principal in July 2023, with significant redundancies. Whatever one concludes about the allegations, the *structural* lesson is that a firm named for one person carries that person's entire risk.
6. **Keep a stated ceiling.** Zumthor at ~15 for four decades is the demonstration that a ceiling is enforceable.

## 9. The builder-side analogue

The same logic runs on the contracting side, at different scale (see `03`): the FMB represents over 6 500 UK construction SMEs and around 42 000 builders, and its award winners are typically small firms with a **specialism** (the 2025 Master Builder of the Year, R & M Williams Limited, won for converting a derelict Grade II listed theatre in Swansea), a **directly employed core team**, systematic **photographic records**, and **managed client testimonials**. Replace "publish a monograph" with "photograph everything before it is covered up" and the reputation machine is identical.
