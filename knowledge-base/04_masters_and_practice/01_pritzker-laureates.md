# Pritzker Architecture Prize laureates and what they studied

**Summary.** The Pritzker Architecture Prize has been awarded annually since 1979 by the Hyatt Foundation; through 2026 there have been 48 award years and, counting co-laureates, more than fifty individual recipients. This file gives the complete register by year with country and a one-line statement of significance, then profiles fourteen laureates whose formation and method are directly instructive for hot-climate, low-budget, craft-based building. The selection is biased on purpose: it favours architects who were trained through a trade, who worked in or for the global South, who built with earth, timber, salvage or unreinforced masonry, and who ran small offices.

## Key facts

- **Established:** 1979, by Jay A. and Cindy Pritzker through the Hyatt Foundation.
- **Award years covered:** 1979–2026 (48).
- **Co-laureate years:** 1988 (Bunshaft & Niemeyer), 2001 (Herzog & de Meuron), 2010 (Sejima & Nishizawa), 2017 (Aranda, Pigem & Vilalta), 2020 (Farrell & McNamara), 2021 (Lacaton & Vassal).
- **Most-represented country:** Japan (Tange 1987, Maki 1993, Ando 1995, SANAA 2010, Ito 2013, Ban 2014, Isozaki 2019, Yamamoto 2024).
- **First African-born laureate:** Diébédo Francis Kéré, 2022.
- **Laureates who trained in a building trade first:** Zumthor (cabinetmaking apprenticeship 1958–62), Kéré (carpentry, then a German vocational scholarship in 1985), Ando (learned woodworking from a carpenter; never took an architecture degree), Piano (raised in and worked in the family construction firm).

## Deep profiles

### Diébédo Francis Kéré (2022) — Burkina Faso / Germany

Born 10 April 1965 in Gando, Burkina Faso, the village chief's eldest son and the first person from the village to go to school. He was sent at seven to Tenkodogo, where he sat in an unventilated cement-block classroom with about a hundred other children — the direct origin of his later thermal obsession. He trained and worked as a carpenter, and in 1985 took a Carl Duisberg Society vocational scholarship to Berlin, learning roofing and furniture-making by day and attending night classes to complete a school-leaving qualification. He then read architecture at the Technische Universität Berlin, graduating in 2004 with a diploma project that was the Gando Primary School itself.

The method is inseparable from the funding: in 1998, still a student, he founded Schulbausteine für Gando e.V. ("Building Blocks for Gando", now the Kéré Foundation) to raise money and organise the village. Gando Primary School was completed in 2001 and won the Aga Khan Award for Architecture in 2004 — before he had a practice. Kéré Architecture followed in Berlin in 2005, with an affiliate office in Burkina Faso to supervise African construction; the Berlin office is small, of the order of a dozen people.

The technical core is climatic and low-tech: compressed earth/clay blocks made locally, a heavy mass wall, a wide detached corrugated-steel roof floating above a perforated ceiling so that hot air escapes by stack effect, and deep overhangs for shade and for rain protection of the earth. Labour is the village's; women carried water, men rammed floors, and techniques were taught rather than imported. His formulation is that "architecture is not about the object but the objective; not the product, but the process", and — in the participation argument — that "only those who are involved in the development process can appreciate the results achieved, develop them further, and protect them". For a Namibian reader this is the single most directly transferable body of work in the register: same latitude band, same material palette, same skills base.

Selected works: Gando Primary School (2001), National Park of Mali (2010), Serpentine Pavilion, London (2017), Burkina Institute of Technology (2020), Startup Lions Campus, Kenya (2021). He has held professorships at Harvard GSD and, since 2017, the chair for Architectural Design and Participation at TU Munich.

### Balkrishna Vithaldas Doshi (2018) — India

Born 26 August 1927 in Pune into a family in the furniture trade; died 24 January 2023 in Ahmedabad. He began architecture at the Sir J. J. School in Mumbai in 1947 (encyclopaedic sources give the Sir J. J. School of Art, 1947–1950; the Pritzker biography names the Sir J. J. School of Architecture — **flagged**). Without a completed degree he went to Paris and worked in Le Corbusier's atelier from 1951 to 1954, then returned to India in 1954 to supervise Le Corbusier's Ahmedabad and Chandigarh work, including the Mill Owners' Association Building and Villa Shodhan. From 1962 he was Louis Kahn's local associate on the Indian Institute of Management, Ahmedabad, for over a decade — a rare case of one architect apprenticing, at full scale and in sequence, to the two dominant figures of the mid-century.

He founded his own studio, Vastu-Shilpa, in Ahmedabad in 1955 (the Pritzker biography says 1956 — **flagged**); it became Vastushilpa Consultants, five partners and about sixty staff, with more than a hundred completed projects. In 1978 he established the Vastu-Shilpa Foundation for Studies and Research in Environmental Design, a research arm attached to a practice — a model worth copying at small scale. He founded the School of Architecture in Ahmedabad in 1962 and the Centre for Environmental Planning and Technology in 1966, which became CEPT University in 2002; he directed it until 2012.

Method: Doshi's is the clearest case in the register of *climate plus culture plus economics* being treated as a single problem. Aranya Low Cost Housing at Indore (1989, Aga Khan Award 1993–95 cycle) laid out some 6 500 sites with a service core and a demonstration house, and let residents build the rest — the incremental logic Aravena would later formalise. Sangath, his own studio (1980), is a half-buried complex of vaults finished in broken china mosaic, cool without machinery. His summary line: "Design converts shelters into homes, housing into communities, and cities into magnets of opportunities."

### Peter Zumthor (2009) — Switzerland

Born 26 April 1943 in Basel, son of the cabinetmaker Oscar Zumthor, and himself apprenticed as a cabinetmaker from 1958 to 1962. He then took the Vorkurs and Fachklasse at the Kunstgewerbeschule in Basel (1963–67) and studied design at Pratt Institute in New York. From 1967 he worked for the Canton of Graubünden's Department for Monument Preservation as a building consultant and analyst of historic villages, carrying out restorations — that is, he spent roughly a decade *taking old buildings apart and understanding how they were made* before designing one of his own. He opened his practice in 1979 in Haldenstein, a village in the Grisons, and has kept it at roughly fifteen people ever since.

Method: extremely few commissions, accepted personally; long gestation; 1:1 material mock-ups; a refusal to separate the idea from its construction. The Therme Vals (1996) is 60 000 slabs of local Valser quartzite laid in a specific coursing rhythm; the Bruder Klaus Field Chapel (2007) was built by farmers using a wigwam of 112 tree trunks as formwork, rammed concrete poured in daily layers, and the timber then burnt out. He argues that architecture must "answer questions that emerge from specific use, place, and society" rather than illustrate an external theory. His writing — *Thinking Architecture* and *Atmospheres* — is the most useful short primer on sensory judgement available to a self-directed student.

Selected works: Protective Housing for Roman Excavations, Chur (1986); Saint Benedict Chapel, Sumvitg (1988); Therme Vals (1996); Kunsthaus Bregenz (1997); Bruder Klaus Field Chapel (2007); Kolumba Museum, Cologne.

### Glenn Murcutt (2002) — Australia

Born 25 July 1936 in London to Australian parents and raised in the Morobe district of New Guinea, where the light, simple, raised timber buildings became his first reference. He took his architecture degree in 1961 — the Pritzker biography credits the University of New South Wales, encyclopaedic sources credit Sydney Technical College (the two institutions are historically entangled; **flagged**). He then travelled for two years and worked at Ancher, Mortlock, Murray and Woolley in Sydney from 1964 for five years before going independent.

Formation: his father put Mies van der Rohe and Thoreau's *Walden* in front of him early; the Scandinavians — Aalto, Asplund, Lewerentz — and the Australian rural vernacular of the corrugated-iron woolshed did the rest. Method: he practises alone, without staff, designing every project himself from brief to site supervision, and refuses commissions that would require an office. Work is filtered ruthlessly — he has a multi-year waiting list and declines almost all institutional work. The buildings are long, thin, single-room-deep pavilions oriented to the sun path and the prevailing wind, lifted on piers, wrapped in adjustable louvres, blinds and flaps so the occupant tunes the building daily; rainwater is collected, and site disturbance is minimal. The Aboriginal maxim he repeats — "touch the earth lightly" — is a construction instruction, not a slogan.

He chairs Architecture Foundation Australia and runs the annual Glenn Murcutt International Master Class, an intensive residential studio: a deliberate mechanism for transmitting method without building an office. Awards include the Alvar Aalto Medal (1992), the Pritzker (2002), the AIA Gold Medal (2009) and the Praemium Imperiale (2021). For a semi-arid, high-solar-gain country the Murcutt house is the most studiable single-family type in the register.

### Álvaro Siza Vieira (1992) — Portugal

Born 25 June 1933 in Matosinhos, a fishing town north of Porto. He studied at the University of Porto School of Architecture from 1949 to 1955 and had built his first four houses in Matosinhos before graduating; he opened his own practice in Porto in 1954, while still a student. He worked with and was shaped by Fernando Távora, the figure who reconciled modernism with Portuguese vernacular survey work (the Pritzker biography does not mention Távora — **flagged as sourced elsewhere**).

Method: Siza is the register's great argument for drawing as thinking. He works in small sketchbooks, in continuous ink line, drawing the same corner dozens of times from imagined eye level until the sequence of movement through the building resolves. Plans are distorted deliberately to absorb the site's geometry — the Leça swimming pools (1966) are cast into the tidal rock so that it is hard to say where the rock stops and the concrete begins. His own account is anti-heroic: architects "do not invent anything, they transform reality", and "every design is a rigorous attempt to capture a concrete moment of a transitory image in all its nuances".

He built the Malagueira social housing quarter at Évora from 1977 — around 1 200 low-cost patio houses with a raised service aqueduct, done for a post-revolutionary cooperative client with almost no money. That project, more than the museums, is the one to study for southern Africa: hot dry climate, courtyard type, incremental delivery, minimal palette of rendered blockwork. He taught at Porto from 1966, became professor in 1976, and has held visiting chairs at Harvard GSD, Pennsylvania, Los Andes (Bogotá) and EPFL Lausanne.

### Alejandro Aravena (2016) — Chile

Born 22 June 1967 in Santiago; graduated as an architect from the Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile in 1992, studied further in Venice, and set up independently in 1994. In 2001 he founded ELEMENTAL, which he describes not as a think tank but a **"Do Tank"** — a practice constituted to work on public-interest problems, part-owned by the Chilean oil company COPEC and the university, which gave it the balance sheet to take on unprofitable social housing.

The signature invention is **incremental housing**, or "half of a good house". Chile's housing subsidy at Quinta Monroy in Iquique (2004) could pay for about 30 m² per family on expensive central land — a figure that produces a slum. ELEMENTAL instead built the *hard half* of a 72 m² middle-class house: the structural frame, party walls, roof, stair, kitchen and bathroom, with a void beside it. Families then filled the void themselves over years, adding value rather than depreciating. The same logic produced Monterrey Housing in Mexico (2010) and Villa Verde at Constitución (2013), and ELEMENTAL has since designed over 2 500 social units. Crucially, ELEMENTAL **published the construction drawings for several incremental housing types as free downloads**, treating the design as public infrastructure rather than intellectual property.

Method: participatory design workshops with residents to settle trade-offs explicitly (which is bigger — the bathroom or the living room?); a "Do Tank" attitude that the constraint is the design brief; and disaster reconstruction planning after the 2010 Chilean tsunami at Constitución, where the answer included a forest as a wave-energy dissipator rather than a sea wall. He directed the 2016 Venice Architecture Biennale, "Reporting from the Front", which put exactly this kind of practice at the centre of the discipline. He has taught at Harvard GSD and the LSE, and held the ELEMENTAL Copec Chair at Universidad Católica since 2006.

### Anne Lacaton & Jean-Philippe Vassal (2021) — France

Anne Lacaton was born in 1955 in Saint-Pardoux, France; Jean-Philippe Vassal in 1954 in Casablanca, Morocco. They trained together at the École Nationale Supérieure d'Architecture et de Paysage de Bordeaux in the late 1970s; Lacaton added a master's in urban planning from Bordeaux Montaigne in 1984. Vassal then moved to Niger to practise urban planning, and Lacaton visited repeatedly. **Their first built work together was a straw hut in Niamey, made of bush branches gathered locally.** It lasted two years. That West African apprenticeship in scarcity — where a building is what you can gather and assemble, and where shade and air movement are the whole design — is the acknowledged origin of the practice. They founded Lacaton & Vassal in Paris in 1987.

Method: get the maximum usable, tempered, generous space for the available money, using the cheapest industrial technology available — typically agricultural/horticultural greenhouse systems, polycarbonate, galvanised steel and simple frames — and *never demolish*. The Latapie House (1993) and the Cap Ferret House (1998, built around existing pines) established the vocabulary. The transformation of the Tour Bois-le-Prêtre (2011, with Frédéric Druot) and of 530 dwellings at Grand Parc, Bordeaux (2017, with Druot and Christophe Hutin) added winter gardens and balconies to occupied 1960s social housing blocks, roughly doubling the living area of each flat at a fraction of the cost of demolition and rebuild, without rehousing the tenants.

The doctrine is quotable and portable: Lacaton — "Transformation is the opportunity of doing more and better with what is already existing"; Vassal — "We never see the existing as a problem." In a country with a large stock of poorly performing existing housing and no money to replace it, this is a more relevant model than any new-build masterpiece.

### Shigeru Ban (2014) — Japan

Born 5 August 1957 in Tokyo. He transferred to the Southern California Institute of Architecture (SCI-Arc) in 1977 and then to Cooper Union in New York in 1980, graduating with a B.Arch. in 1984 — taught by Ricardo Scofidio, Tod Williams, Diana Agrest, Bernard Tschumi, Peter Eisenman and John Hejduk. He worked briefly for Arata Isozaki in Tokyo and founded his own practice there in 1985 (later adding Paris in 2004 and New York).

Method: material-first invention with a structural-engineering partnership. From 1985–86 he developed **paper tube structures** — recycled cardboard tubes, cheap, locally manufacturable, low-embodied-energy — and pursued certification until Japan's Minister of Construction approved them as permanent structure in 1995. He collaborated with Frei Otto on the Japan Pavilion at Expo 2000 in Hanover. In 1994 he proposed paper-tube shelters for Rwandan refugees; in 1995, after the Kobe earthquake, he built the Paper Log House (beer-crate-and-sandbag foundations, paper-tube walls) and the Paper Church, and founded the **Voluntary Architects' Network**. Disaster work followed in Turkey (1999), India (2001), Sri Lanka (2004), China (2008), Haiti (2010), Italy, New Zealand (2011) and the Philippines.

The transferable lessons are procedural, not formal: (1) pick an ordinary industrial product and take responsibility for proving it structurally; (2) build a permanent parallel non-commercial arm so that relief work is a standing capacity rather than an occasional gesture; (3) keep listening — "I must continue to listen to the people I work for, in my private residential commissions and in my disaster relief work."

### Wang Shu (2012) — China

Born in 1963 in Urumqi, Xinjiang. First degree in architecture from the Nanjing Institute of Technology in 1985, master's from the same institution in 1988. He then worked at the Zhejiang Academy of Fine Arts on environment and architecture, and — the decisive step — spent close to a decade from around 1990 **working on building sites with craftsmen, with no design responsibility at all**, learning how Chinese masons, carpenters and tilers actually work. Only in 1997 did he found Amateur Architecture Studio in Hangzhou with his wife and partner Lu Wenyu; the name is a deliberate rejection of the professionalised Chinese design institute.

Method: salvage as structure and as argument. China's demolition boom produces mountains of old brick, tile and stone; Wang Shu's *wa pan* technique — a traditional Ningbo method of laying mixed reclaimed material in a wall — turns that waste into load-bearing and cladding fabric with an irreproducible texture. For the Xiangshan campus of the China Academy of Art he collected over two million recycled tiles for the roofs. Craft labour is used at a scale that machine-led construction cannot match, and the mason's judgement is designed *into* the tolerance of the detail rather than out of it.

Selected works: Library of Wenzheng College, Suzhou (1999–2000); Xiangshan Campus Phases I and II (2004; 2004–07); Five Scattered Houses, Ningbo (2003–06); Ceramic House, Jinhua (2003–06); Vertical Courtyard Apartments, Hangzhou (2002–07); Ningbo History Museum (2003–08). His own framing: "to me architecture is spontaneous for the simple reason that architecture is a matter of everyday life."

### Tadao Ando (1995) — Japan

Born in Osaka in 1941 (the Pritzker biography gives his age as 53 at the 1995 award). He is the register's one **wholly self-taught** major figure: no architecture degree, no pupillage under a master. He learned woodworking from a neighbourhood carpenter as a boy, boxed professionally for a short period, then educated himself — apprenticeships with designers and city planners, systematic visits to the temples and teahouses of Kyoto and Nara, obsessive reading, and study journeys through Europe, Africa, Asia and the United States in the 1960s. He famously found a book of Le Corbusier's drawings in an Osaka second-hand shop, traced every drawing in it, and later travelled to Marseille to stand in the Unité d'Habitation. He kept detailed sketchbooks throughout, and founded Tadao Ando Architect & Associates in Osaka on his return.

Method: a deliberately narrow palette — smooth in-situ concrete, glass, water, and light — worked through an equally narrow set of geometric operations, in order to make the *phenomenon* rather than the form the subject. The concrete's finish comes from obsessive plywood formwork carpentry and pour discipline, not from an exotic mix. Light is treated as material: "In all my works, light is an important controlling factor." His early Azuma House (Row House in Sumiyoshi, 1977) inserted an open courtyard into a 3.6 m-wide terrace house, forcing the occupant outside to cross between rooms — a deliberate discomfort in exchange for sky, air and rain.

Selected works: Azuma House (1977); Church on the Water (1988); Church of the Light (1989); Rokko Housing I–III (1983–1999); Naoshima Contemporary Art Museum (1992–95). Ando is the proof case for the self-directed curriculum in file `04`: travel, measure, trace, build small, repeat.

### Rafael Moneo (1996) — Spain

Born in May 1937 in Tudela, Navarra. He took his degree from the Madrid University School of Architecture in 1961, working while still a student for Francisco Javier Sáenz de Oiza. He then went to Denmark to work for **Jørn Utzon** during the Sydney Opera House years, travelled through Scandinavia and met Alvar Aalto in Helsinki. He won a fellowship to the Spanish Academy in Rome from 1962 to 1965 — a period he called "fundamental to my career" — spent reading, measuring, travelling and building an intellectual network. He returned to Madrid in 1965.

Method: Moneo is the scholar's model. He treats each commission as a fresh problem reasoned from the specific city, programme and history, drawing on a private reservoir of types and concepts rather than a signature style, which is why his buildings do not resemble each other. The Museum of Roman Art at Mérida (1986) is the exemplar: brick piers laid in Roman coursing and proportions, built directly over the excavated Roman city, so the museum's structure and its contents share a construction logic. He co-founded the magazine *Arquitectura Bis* in the late 1970s, taught at Madrid from 1966, was visiting professor at Princeton, Harvard and Lausanne, and chaired the Harvard GSD architecture department from 1984 to 1990. His maxim — that architecture "only reaches its true status when it is realized, when it acquires its being as an object" — is a corrective to paper practice.

### Renzo Piano (1998) — Italy

Born in Genoa in 1937 into a family of builders: father, grandfather and uncles all contractors. He graduated from the Milan Polytechnic school of architecture in 1964, then worked in his father's construction company — a fact worth dwelling on, because it explains the practice's entire orientation — before associating with Louis Kahn's office in Philadelphia and with the space-frame engineer Z. S. Makowski in London. With Richard Rogers he won the Centre Georges Pompidou competition, completed in 1978.

He founded the **Renzo Piano Building Workshop** in 1980 (Paris, Genoa, and later Berlin). The name is the method: the office is organised as a workshop with model shops and material laboratories, and its defining relationship is with engineers — above all Peter Rice of Ove Arup — with whom the practice invents a *piece* for each project: the Pompidou's cast-steel gerberettes, the Menil Collection's ferro-cement light leaves (1987), Kansai Airport's 1.7 km curved roof (1988–94), the timber-and-steel *cases* of the Tjibaou Cultural Centre in Nouméa (1998), which were wind-tunnel developed to behave like Kanak vernacular huts in the trade winds.

The Tjibaou centre is the one to study from Namibia: a Pacific vernacular type, abstracted, engineered for passive ventilation in a hot humid climate, built by a European office with local timber and local meaning. The Pritzker jury's chairman described Piano as "deeply imbued with a sense of materials and a craftsman's intuitive feel for what they can do". That intuition was earned on his father's sites, not in a studio.

### Yvonne Farrell & Shelley McNamara — Grafton Architects (2020) — Ireland

Farrell was born in 1951, McNamara in 1952. They met studying architecture at University College Dublin and graduated in 1976; both were immediately offered teaching posts at UCD, which they held until 2006, becoming adjunct professors in 2015. In 1978 they co-founded Grafton Architects in Dublin with three other partners — naming the practice after a street rather than after themselves, a deliberate statement that the work is collective and situated.

Method: teaching and practice as a single activity — "Teaching for us has always been a parallel reality… a way of trying to distill our experience" (Farrell). They think in section and in mass: the Università Luigi Bocconi in Milan (2008) and the UTEC campus in Lima (2015) are stacked, cantilevered concrete landscapes whose structure creates shaded, ventilated public space in the section rather than decorating a plan. UTEC in particular is a hot-climate lesson — a "vertical campus" on a cliff edge in Lima, with open circulation, deep shading and no air-conditioning through much of the building. They held the Kenzo Tange Chair at Harvard GSD (2010) and the Louis Kahn Chair at Yale (2011), taught at EPFL and Mendrisio, and curated the 2018 Venice Biennale under the title *Freespace*. McNamara's summary: "Architecture is a framework for human life. It anchors us and connects us to the world."

Selected works: North King Street Housing, Dublin (2000); Urban Institute of Ireland (2002); Bocconi, Milan (2008); Medical School, University of Limerick (2012); UTEC, Lima (2015); Institut Mines Télécom, Paris (2019); Université Toulouse 1 Capitole (2019).

### Liu Jiakun (2025) — China

Born 1956 in Chengdu; graduated with a Bachelor of Engineering in Architecture in 1982 from the Institute of Architecture and Engineering in Chongqing (now Chongqing University). For years afterwards he was primarily a **novelist**, publishing fiction and later essays including *Narrative Discourse and Low-Tech Strategy* (1997), *Now and Here* (2002), *I Built in West China?* (2009) and *The Conception of Brightmoon* (2014). He founded Jiakun Architects in Chengdu in 1999.

Method: a declared "low-tech strategy" — accept the actual skills, materials and tolerances available in western China, and design so that ordinary local labour can execute the building well rather than execute a sophisticated building badly. After the 2008 Wenchuan earthquake he developed **"rebirth bricks"**: rubble from the collapsed buildings crushed, mixed with local wheat straw fibre and cement, and pressed into blocks, then used structurally in later projects including the Novartis Shanghai block, the Shuijingfang Museum and West Village. West Village in Chengdu (2015) is a single city block turned into a continuous public ramp and sports/leisure landscape wrapped around a courtyard — described by the jury as at once "a historical record, a piece of infrastructure, a landscape and a remarkable public space". His own statement of intent: "Architecture should reveal something — it should abstract, distill and make visible the inherent qualities of local people."

### Smiljan Radić Clarke (2026) — Chile

Born in Santiago to a family of Croatian and British descent. He studied architecture at the Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile, graduating in 1989 — having failed his final examination at the first attempt — and then studied history at the Istituto Universitario di Architettura di Venezia. He regards extensive travel as an essential part of his education, and founded his practice in Santiago in 1995, keeping it deliberately small.

Method: buildings that read as provisional, fragile or unfinished, built from stone, concrete, timber and glass used for their weight and their sound as much as their look. The Serpentine Gallery Pavilion of 2014 was a translucent fibreglass shell resting on large loose quarry stones. The jury citation praises a choice of "fragility over any unwarranted claim to certainty" and buildings that "provide a structured, optimistic and quietly joyful shelter, embracing vulnerability". His most portable remark for anyone practising far from a metropolitan centre: **"Sometimes, you have to produce your own roots. That gives you freedom."**

Selected works: Casa Chica, Vilches (1997); Carbonero House (1998); CR House, Santiago (2003); Pite House, Papudo (2005); Restaurant Mestizo, Santiago (2006); House for the Poem of the Right Angle (2013); Serpentine Pavilion, London (2014); NAVE, Santiago (2015); Teatro Regional del Biobío, Concepción (2018).

## What the fourteen have in common

- **A trade or a site before a studio.** Zumthor, Kéré, Ando, Piano and Wang Shu all handled materials professionally before designing with them.
- **A long, low-status middle period.** Almost none of them had a significant commission before their mid-thirties.
- **A small office, permanently.** Zumthor ~15; Murcutt 1; Radić deliberately small; Amateur Architecture Studio two principals; Kéré around a dozen in Berlin.
- **One climate-driven technical idea, pursued for decades** — Ban's tubes, Kéré's ventilated double roof, Lacaton & Vassal's greenhouse skins, Liu's rebirth bricks, Aravena's incremental half-house.
- **Teaching or writing as part of the method**, not as a sideline: Doshi founded a university, Moneo chaired Harvard, Grafton taught for thirty years, Liu wrote novels, Zumthor wrote two short books that do more work than most monographs.
