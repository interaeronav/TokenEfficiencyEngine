# Patterns of thinking among master practitioners

**Summary.** Reading the profiled practitioners' own statements alongside their built work, a small number of cognitive habits recur with unusual consistency: reasoning about material from first principles rather than from catalogue; treating the tightest constraint as the generator rather than the obstacle; iterating in cycles rather than converging in one pass; thinking in section; alternating deliberately between the detail and the whole; using the diagram to compress an argument; treating the building as one element in a system that includes labour, maintenance and money; managing the client as a design problem; and knowing when to stop. This file states each habit, evidences it with attributed quotations, and gives the practical move that follows from it.

## 1. First-principles material reasoning

The habit: ask what this material *is* and what it can do here, rather than what the specification usually says.

Evidence. Shigeru Ban took the cardboard tube — an industrial packaging by-product — and pursued it as **structure** until Japan's Minister of Construction certified paper-tube structures as permanent building in 1995, a decade after he began. Liu Jiakun took earthquake rubble, added local wheat straw fibre and cement, and pressed **"rebirth bricks"** that were then used structurally in the Novartis Shanghai block, the Shuijingfang Museum and West Village. Wang Shu took the demolition waste of Chinese cities — over two million salvaged tiles for the Xiangshan campus roofs alone — and made it into wall and roof fabric using the traditional *wa pan* laying method. Kéré took the clay under his village and made a mass wall, then floated a corrugated steel roof above a perforated ceiling so heat leaves by stack effect. Ando's concrete is "smooth as silk" not because of an exotic mix but because of obsessive plywood formwork carpentry and pour discipline.

The move: for each project, name the three materials that exist within 50 km and design as if the imported ones do not. Then take one of them and test it to failure — literally, physically, with a sample. **[NA]** In Namibia this points at soil-cement block, local stone, and steel sheet, and away from imported timber and fired brick.

## 2. Constraint as generator

The habit: identify the single hardest constraint and make it the design's engine, not its excuse.

Evidence. Aravena's Quinta Monroy problem was that the available Chilean subsidy bought roughly 30 m² on expensive central land. Instead of shrinking the house, ELEMENTAL built the *hard half* of a 72 m² house — frame, party walls, roof, stair, wet cores — and left a void the family would fill. The constraint produced the type. Lacaton & Vassal's constraint was that occupied 1960s social housing could not be emptied and could not be afforded new; the answer at Tour Bois-le-Prêtre (2011) and Grand Parc, Bordeaux (530 dwellings, 2017) was to bolt cheap greenhouse-technology winter gardens onto the existing structure, roughly doubling floor area at a fraction of demolition-and-rebuild cost, with tenants in place. Vassal: "We never see the existing as a problem." Kéré's constraint was that his village had no skilled trades and no money; the answer was a construction system simple enough for villagers to build and to repair. Lari's constraint is disaster and poverty at scale; her Heritage Foundation of Pakistan, co-founded 1980, has built on the order of 50 000 homes since 2010 using bamboo, lime and mud with local labour.

The move: write the brief as a single sentence beginning "The only real constraint is…" and refuse to design until that sentence is true and agreed with the client.

## 3. Iteration and the design spiral

The habit: converge by looping through the whole problem repeatedly at increasing resolution, rather than resolving one part at a time.

Evidence. Siza redraws the same corner dozens of times; his own account is that "every design is a rigorous attempt to capture a concrete moment of a transitory image", and that architects "transform in response to the problems they encounter" rather than invent. Adeyemi's Makoko Floating School **collapsed in June 2016** and was iterated into MFS II and MFS III rather than abandoned — failure treated as a design cycle. Radić failed his final examination at university before graduating in 1989, and his work embraces the provisional: the 2026 jury praised his choice of "fragility over any unwarranted claim to certainty".

The move: fix a cycle length — a week — and at the end of each one produce a complete, if crude, version of the *entire* project: plan, section, structure, cost, programme. Never let one part get three cycles ahead of the others.

## 4. Sectional thinking

The habit: design in section first, because in a hot climate the section is where the building's physics happens — solar gain, stack ventilation, thermal mass, daylight depth, rainwater.

Evidence. Kéré's whole signature is a section: mass wall, perforated ceiling, air gap, elevated detached roof, deep overhang. Grafton Architects' UTEC campus in Lima (2015) is a stacked concrete section on a cliff — open circulation, deep shade, ventilation in the section rather than air conditioning — as is the Bocconi in Milan (2008). Doshi's Sangath studio (1980) is half-buried vaults with china-mosaic skins: a section that stays cool without machinery. Ando's Azuma House (1977) inserts an open courtyard into a 3.6 m-wide terrace, forcing occupants outdoors to move between rooms; the plan is trivial, the section and the sky are the project. Yamamoto's entire fifty-year argument — derived from travels with Hiroshi Hara in 1972 and 1974 and from growing up in a Yokohama *machiya* with his mother's pharmacy in front of the family rooms — is about the **threshold**, which is a sectional and spatial device, not a plan graphic.

The move: for every project, draw the section before the plan, at 1:50, through the most difficult point, and put the sun angles and the air path on it.

## 5. Detail outward vs whole inward

The habit: know which direction you are working in, and switch deliberately.

Two documented poles. **Detail outward:** Zumthor, trained as a cabinetmaker (1958–62) and then employed from 1967 to analyse and restore historic villages in Graubünden, builds from the joint and the material sample outward; the Bruder Klaus Field Chapel (2007) is generated by a construction procedure — 112 tree trunks as a wigwam of formwork, rammed concrete in daily layers, the timber then burnt out. Piano, raised in a builders' family and trained in his father's firm, works the same way: each Building Workshop project invents a *piece* — the Pompidou gerberette, the Menil Collection's light leaves (1987), Tjibaou's ventilated timber *cases* (1998) — and the building grows around it.

**Whole inward:** Moneo reasons from city, history and type down to the detail, which is why his buildings do not resemble one another; the Museum of Roman Art at Mérida (1986) starts from Roman brick coursing as an urban and historical argument and ends at the wall. Rossi and Chipperfield sit on the same side — the 2023 jury praised Chipperfield's "consistency with fundamental design principles and flexibility towards local cultures", and work that "will be able to stand the test of time".

The move: state at the start of each project which pole you are working from, and then force yourself through one full pass from the opposite direction before you commit. If you began with a wall detail, draw the town. If you began with the town, build the wall.

## 6. The role of the diagram

The habit: compress the argument into one drawing that a non-architect can read, and then defend it.

Evidence. Aravena's "half of a good house" is a diagram before it is a building: the filled half, the void half, the arrow of future occupation. It is the reason the idea travelled to Mexico (Monterrey, 2010) and back to Chile (Villa Verde, 2013) and why ELEMENTAL could **publish the drawings for free download** and expect others to build them. Kéré's ventilated-roof section is a diagram. Yamamoto's threshold, Lacaton & Vassal's before/after floor-area comparison, and Ban's paper-tube joint are all one-drawing arguments.

The move: every project gets one diagram, on one A4, that states the idea without any architecture in it. If you cannot draw it, you do not yet have an idea — you have a shape.

## 7. Systems thinking — the building is not the unit

The habit: design the labour, the money, the maintenance and the institution alongside the building.

Evidence. Kéré founded a fundraising association, Schulbausteine für Gando e.V., in **1998** — three years before the school was finished and seven before the practice existed. Ban founded the **Voluntary Architects' Network** in 1995 so that relief work would be a standing capacity rather than an occasional gesture. Doshi founded a school (1962), a research foundation (Vastu-Shilpa Foundation, 1978) and a university (CEPT, 1966; university status 2002) around his practice. Aravena constituted ELEMENTAL in 2001 as a "Do Tank" with corporate and university shareholders so that unprofitable social housing could be done at all. Vally's Serpentine commission came with *Support Structures for Support Structures*, a grant programme for community artists. Kéré's participation doctrine is explicitly about maintenance: "only those who are involved in the development process can appreciate the results achieved, develop them further, and **protect them**."

> ⚠️ The counter-example is essential. Jo Noero's Red Location Museum (opened 10 November 2006) won the World Leadership Award, a RIBA International Award and the RIBA Lubetkin Prize — and was closed in October 2013 after residents protested that they had a museum and no houses. It has never reopened; repair costs were put at R12 million. A building that is excellent and that is not part of a system the community wants will be closed by that community. Design the institution or do not build.

## 8. Dealing with clients

The habit: treat the client relationship as designed, with explicit trade-offs made visible.

Evidence. Ban: "I must continue to listen to the people I work for, in my private residential commissions and in my disaster relief work" — note that he puts both kinds of client in one sentence. Aravena's participatory workshops put trade-offs to residents explicitly (bathroom or living room?), which converts the architect from a taste authority into a facilitator of choices, and makes the resulting design defensible. Murcutt controls the relationship by controlling intake: sole practitioner, no staff, no institutional work, long waiting list — he selects clients who accept his method rather than negotiating his method with clients. Zumthor does the same at ~15 staff.

The move: at the start, write down the three trade-offs the project cannot avoid, and get the client to choose in writing. At the end, ask for written feedback. (Both of those, as `03` notes, are also exactly what wins builder-side awards.)

## 9. Knowing when to stop

The habit: recognise sufficiency; resist adding.

Evidence. Murcutt: "Most of us spend our lives doing ordinary tasks; the most important thing is to carry them out extraordinarily well" — a sufficiency doctrine, and the reason his houses are one-room-deep pavilions with adjustable louvres rather than complex objects. Moneo insists that architecture "only reaches its true status when it is realized… transformed into material reality" — the drawing is not the work, so stop drawing and build. The 2023 jury's phrase for Chipperfield, **"sustainability as pertinence"**, is a stopping rule: the sustainable move is the pertinent one, not the additional one. Radić's deliberately unfinished buildings and Lacaton & Vassal's refusal to demolish are both refusals to do more work than the problem requires.

The move: define, before starting, what "done" looks like in one sentence and one number (a cost, an area, a date). When you hit it, stop and build.
