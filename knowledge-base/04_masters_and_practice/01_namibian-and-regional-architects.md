# African and southern African architects worth studying

**Summary.** This file records the African practitioners whose formation and method are documented well enough to learn from, with the strongest coverage on West Africa and South Africa and, honestly, the thinnest on Namibia — where no consolidated public record of practices, awards or authorship of significant buildings could be retrieved. It is written to answer three questions for each figure: what did they study and where, what did they build, and what is the transferable method. Where a fact could not be verified against a source that was actually fetched, it is marked **unverified** rather than filled in.

## West Africa

### Diébédo Francis Kéré (Burkina Faso / Germany)

Fully profiled in `01_pritzker-laureates.md`. Summary of what matters here: village carpenter → German vocational carpentry scholarship (1985) → TU Berlin architecture diploma (2004) → Aga Khan Award (2004) for a school he had built as a student → Pritzker (2022). His office is small — the practice website lists roughly eight core team members in Berlin plus an affiliate office in Burkina Faso — and its stated aim is "building with the smallest energy footprint and reaching the utmost benefit for its user". His participation doctrine — "People are the basis of every piece of work" — is operational, not rhetorical: the Gando school was funded by an association he founded in 1998, and built by villagers who were trained on the job.

**Transferable to Namibia:** compressed earth block on a raised plinth with a wide, detached, elevated metal roof and a perforated ceiling for stack ventilation is a directly applicable school and clinic type for northern Namibia. So is the funding sequence: association first, demonstration building second, practice third.

### Kunlé Adeyemi / NLÉ (Nigeria / Netherlands)

Born 7 April 1976 in Kaduna, Nigeria. He took his first degree at the University of Lagos, graduating as best graduate, and a post-professional degree at Princeton University School of Architecture in 2005. He joined the Office for Metropolitan Architecture (OMA) in 2002 and spent about nine years there as a senior associate under Rem Koolhaas, running large projects including the Shenzhen Stock Exchange (2006, opened 2007), the Rothschild Bank in London (2006), the Qatar Foundation work in Doha, the Nelson Mandela African Institute masterplan in Abuja (2006) and the Fourth Mainland Bridge masterplan for Lagos. He founded NLÉ ("at home", in Yoruba) in 2010; it now operates from Amsterdam with Lagos work.

The best-known project is the **Makoko Floating School** in the Lagos lagoon — a triangular A-frame timber structure on a raft of empty plastic barrels, built with local boatbuilders for a water-borne settlement that has no legal land tenure. The original structure **collapsed in June 2016** after heavy rain, and NLÉ has iterated the design as MFS II and MFS III, MFS II being exhibited at the Venice Biennale.

**Method to take:** big-office training in complex delivery, then deliberately redirecting it at an African problem no one else is working on; a prototype that fails publicly and is iterated rather than abandoned; construction with an existing local trade (boatbuilders) rather than an imported one. **Unverified:** the widely reported Silver Lion at the 2016 Venice Biennale for MFS II could not be confirmed from a fetched source.

## Southern Africa — South Africa

### David Adjaye (Ghana / UK)

Born 22 September 1966 in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania, son of a Ghanaian diplomat; lived in Tanzania, Egypt, Yemen and Lebanon before moving to Britain aged nine. BA in Architecture, London South Bank University, 1990, winning the RIBA Bronze Medal for the best undergraduate design; MA, Royal College of Art, 1993. Founded Adjaye Associates in 2000; offices in Accra, London and New York.

Key works: Idea Store, Whitechapel (2005); Nobel Peace Center, Oslo (2005); Museum of Contemporary Art Denver (2007); Moscow School of Management Skolkovo (2010); two Washington D.C. libraries (2012); the **National Museum of African American History and Culture** on the Mall in Washington (selected 2009, opened 2016); Ruby City, San Antonio (2019); Abrahamic Family House, Abu Dhabi (2023); Princeton University Art Museum (opened October 2025). Honours: OBE 2007, knighthood 2017, **RIBA Royal Gold Medal 2021** (the first African recipient), Order of Merit 2022.

> ⚠️ In July 2023 three women publicly accused Adjaye of sexual assault and harassment. He denied the allegations; no charges were filed, and he acknowledged "mistakes" in blurring professional and personal boundaries. He resigned as architectural adviser to the Mayor of London, stepped back from the UK Holocaust Memorial project, and the practice lost the Liverpool International Slavery Museum commission and an Africa Institute campus project, followed by significant redundancies. This is recorded because the file is a reference on practitioners, and because reputational risk is a real feature of the sole-name practice model discussed in `06`.

**Method to take (separable from the person):** his early research output — a systematic photographic survey of the built form of a large number of African capital cities, published as *Adjaye • Africa • Architecture* — is a model of precedent study at continental scale, and a template for the kind of survey a Namibian practitioner could do for southern African towns.

### Sumayya Vally / Counterspace (South Africa)

Born February 1990 in Laudium, a township outside Pretoria. Undergraduate architecture at the University of Pretoria; master's at the University of the Witwatersrand, completed 2014. In 2014 she was assistant curator and film producer for the South African Pavilion at the Venice Biennale. She co-founded **Counterspace** in Johannesburg in 2015, initially as a research practice, and from 2015 to 2020 led Unit 12, "An African Almanac", at the University of Johannesburg's Graduate School of Architecture.

In 2020, aged 30, she was appointed to design the 20th **Serpentine Pavilion**, built in 2021 — the youngest architect ever commissioned. The pavilion abstracted the forms of gathering places of London's migrant and Black communities — the Fazl Mosque, the East London Mosque, Centerprise bookshop, The Four Aces Club, The Mangrove restaurant, Notting Hill Carnival — in reclaimed steel, cork and timber with a micro-cement coating, in pink and brown tones. Fragments were sited at four partner organisations across the city, and an accompanying grant programme, *Support Structures for Support Structures*, funded artists doing community work. She was the only architect on the 2021 TIME100 Next list, is a World Economic Forum Young Global Leader, delivered the 18th Geoffrey Bawa Memorial Lecture in Colombo (2022), was artistic director of the inaugural Islamic Arts Biennale in Jeddah (2023), and is Honorary Professor of Practice at the Bartlett, UCL (2023).

**Method to take:** research *is* the practice. Counterspace's route to work was archival and ethnographic study of erased spaces, published and exhibited before any building existed. Her own formulation — a practice "centred around amplifying and collaborating with multiple and diverse voices from many different histories" — describes a business model as much as an ethic: the studio was commissioned on the strength of a position, not a portfolio of buildings.

### Jo Noero (South Africa)

Noero Architects is based in Cape Town. Verified career markers: lecturer at the School of Architecture, University of the Witwatersrand from 1982; Ruth and Norman Moore Professor of Architecture and Director of Graduate Studies at the Sam Fox School, Washington University in St. Louis, from 1995; Director of the School of Architecture and Planning at the University of Cape Town 2000–2005; Professor of Architecture at UCT 2000–2014; Emeritus Professor 2015. Honorary Doctorate of Science, University of Brighton (1995); Alexander Petrie Prize (1998); **SAIA Gold Medal for Architecture (2010)**; RIBA International Award; World Leadership Award for Public Architecture; International Fellow of the RIBA and Honorary Fellow of the AIA.

The defining building is the **Red Location Museum** in New Brighton, Port Elizabeth (Gqeberha), opened 10 November 2006 — twelve corrugated-iron "memory boxes", each 6 × 6 × 12 m, in a top-lit shed with no prescribed route, so that a visitor must choose their own path through the anti-apartheid narrative. It won the World Leadership Award for Public Architecture (2005), a RIBA International Award and the Dedalo Minosse Presidents Award (2006), the **RIBA Lubetkin Prize** for the most outstanding building outside the EU (recorded as 2010 in one source and 2006 in others — **flagged**), and the Icon Building of the Year Award (2012).

> ⚠️ The Red Location Museum, library and gallery were closed in October 2013 after protests by Red Location residents over unresolved housing conditions, and have remained closed. The complex was vandalised and looted; repair costs were estimated at R12 million and the work was never started. This is the single most instructive cautionary tale in southern African architecture: an internationally garlanded cultural building placed inside a community whose primary demand was houses. Any Namibian practitioner doing public work should be able to narrate it.

Other works: Red Location Gallery; the Denis Goldberg House of Hope, Hout Bay; Christ Church, Somerset West; the West Coast Fossil Park interpretive centre, Langebaan.

### Peter Rich (South Africa)

Peter Rich Architects is Johannesburg-based. The practice's own account states that it won **World Building of the Year 2009** at the World Architecture Festival for the **Mapungubwe Interpretation Centre** in Limpopo, and that it exhibited hand-drawn work at the 2018 Venice Biennale; its stated position is "designing with, not for, communities, championing African architecture and home-grown ideas".

Mapungubwe is the most technically important African building of the last twenty years for a resource-constrained context. It is a set of **tile (timbrel/Catalan) vaults** — thin unreinforced shells built up in layers of small tiles laid in mortar without formwork — using pressed soil-cement tiles made on site from local earth, erected by local workers trained specifically for the project as an employment programme. Vaulting removes the need for imported steel and heavy plant; the mass and the earth finish handle the Limpopo heat.

**Unverified:** Rich's birth year, degrees and dates, his teaching career at the University of the Witwatersrand, his long-running documentation of Ndebele and other African settlement patterns, and the identity of the vault engineers (the technique is associated in the literature with John Ochsendorf and Michael Ramage, and with the Guastavino tradition) could not be confirmed from sources fetched during compilation. All should be checked before citation.

For context on the tradition Rich draws from: Ndebele homestead building and painting is women's work, passed mother to daughter, applied to mud-walled houses that replaced grass huts from the mid-eighteenth century, with beadwork-derived geometry outlined in black on white since the late 1960s. Any African-settlement study starts there and at Great Zimbabwe.

### Practitioners recorded but not verified

The following are named here because a Namibian or South African reader should study them, and because leaving them out would misrepresent the field — but nothing below was confirmed from a fetched source, so **treat every claim as needing verification**:

- **Mphethi Morojele — MMA Design Studio, Johannesburg.** Associated in the literature with the South African embassies in Berlin and Addis Ababa, with Freedom Park in Pretoria, and with the Wits Art Museum. Reported to have studied partly outside South Africa during exile. **All unverified.**
- **Luyanda Mpahlwa.** Associated with the Design Indaba **10x10 Housing Project** at Freedom Park, Mitchells Plain, Cape Town — low-cost houses using a timber-frame-and-sandbag ("EcoBeam") wall system that unskilled residents could fill themselves. Reported to have studied in Berlin. **All unverified**, including the build date, unit cost and awards.
- **Mokena Makeka — Makeka Design Lab, Cape Town.** Associated with the pre-2010 refurbishment of Cape Town railway station and with public-transport and civic work in the Western Cape. The Cape Town station article consulted names PRASA-Metrorail and Intersite as the client initiative but **does not name a design architect**. **All unverified.**
- **Heinrich Wolff and Ilze Wolff — Wolff Architects, Cape Town**, and the earlier partnership Noero Wolff Architects with Jo Noero. **All unverified.**

The South African Institute of Architects (SAIA, formed 1996 from the Institute of South African Architects, founded 1927, and six regional institutes) is the authority to check these against: it awards **Awards of Excellence** biennially since 1990, **Awards of Merit** since 1999, a **Gold Medal for Architecture**, a Medal of Distinction and an Architectural Writers and Critics Award. As a scale reference, the 2018 round gave six Awards of Excellence, twenty-four Awards of Merit and five commendations.

## Namibia

### The institutional frame

- **Regulator:** the Namibia Council for Architects and Quantity Surveyors (NCAQS), a statutory council under the Architects' and Quantity Surveyors' Act 13 of 1979, at 8 Koch Street, Klein Windhoek. Its stated purpose is to "protect the public from persons performing duties reserved for Architects and Quantity Surveyors". It does not publish registration statistics online.
- **Voluntary body:** the Namibia Institute of Architects (NIA). **Its website could not be retrieved during compilation; membership numbers, member practices and any awards programme are unverified.**
- **School:** the Namibia University of Science and Technology, Windhoek, carries a Department of Architecture. NUST descends from the Academy for Tertiary Education (1980), became the Polytechnic of Namibia by Act 33 of 1994, and was renamed NUST by Act of Parliament in 2015. Degree levels for architecture and spatial planning are covered in `01_architecture/02_accredited-schools-and-curricula.md`.

### The design climate

Windhoek is hot semi-arid, Köppen **BSh**: coldest month July at about 13.1 °C mean, hottest month December at about 23.5 °C mean, roughly **367 mm** of rainfall a year, and over 300 sunny days. That is the design envelope — enormous diurnal swing, very high direct solar radiation, scarce water, and rainfall that arrives hard and briefly. It rewards exactly the strategies that Kéré, Doshi and Siza use: thermal mass with night purge, a detached and ventilated roof, deep shade, courtyards, small and shaded east/west glazing, and rainwater capture.

### The historic building stock and who made it

- **Gottlieb Wilhelm Eduard Redecker** (b. 30 April 1871, Otjimbingwe; d. 21 January 1945, Gütersloh) — Namibia's first architect. Born in the country, educated at the Augustineum training college, sent to Germany by missionaries after his mother's death when he was eleven, completing secondary education and engineering studies there. Appointed director of engineering by the Imperial Government of German South West Africa in 1901. He designed **Christ Church (Christuskirche), Windhoek**, completed 15 October 1910 after about three years of construction, in neo-Romanesque style and at the third attempt at a design; and the **Tintenpalast** ("Ink Palace"), built 1912–13 and opened 12 April 1913, with a neoclassical front façade, built from regional materials by the firm Sander & Kock, and now the seat of both houses of the Parliament of Namibia.
- **Wilhelm (Willi) Sander** — designed the three Windhoek castles, Heinitzburg, Sanderburg and Schwerinsburg, from about 1907.
- **Alte Feste** — built from 1890 when Major Curt von François established the colonial garrison; now the National Museum.
- **Independence Memorial Museum, Windhoek** — a post-independence museum of anti-colonial resistance. **The designer and builder, opening date and cost could not be verified from any source fetched during compilation.** It is widely reported to have been designed and built by a North Korean state design and construction enterprise; do not repeat that without checking it.

### What a Namibian practitioner should do with this

1. **Assume the record does not exist and make it.** There is no accessible consolidated history of twentieth- and twenty-first-century Namibian practice. A measured-drawing survey of twenty Windhoek and Swakopmund buildings, with dates and attributions checked at the National Archives of Namibia and NCAQS, would be an original contribution and, on the Counterspace and Adjaye precedents, a credible route to commissions.
2. **Study the vernacular before the colonial.** Owambo homestead planning, Herero and Himba settlement, Nama matjieshuis mat-house construction and Damara stonework are the local equivalents of the material Peter Rich mined among the Ndebele. Kéré's whole career rests on his having taken his own village's building culture seriously.
3. **Take the tile vault and the earth block seriously as a Namibian technology.** Mapungubwe proves a soil-cement tile vault can be built by trained-on-site local labour, with almost no steel and no cranes, in a hot southern African setting.
4. **Register, then join.** NCAQS registration is the statutory requirement; NIA membership is the professional network. See `01_architecture/01_pathway-to-registration.md`.
