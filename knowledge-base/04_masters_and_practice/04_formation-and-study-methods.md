# Formation and study methods — a personal curriculum

**Summary.** Architectural and construction skill is transmitted by a small number of well-documented practices: drawing by hand every day, making physical models, analysing existing buildings by redrawing them, visiting sites and reading them out loud, being criticised in public by people who are better than you, apprenticing to someone who builds, entering competitions you will not win, travelling with a measuring tape, and keeping a written record. None of these requires money, a school, or a licence. This file describes each practice, gives the historical source of it, and then assembles it into a self-directed curriculum with weekly, monthly and annual commitments that a person in Windhoek could actually start on Monday.

## Key facts

| Practice | Origin / precedent | Minimum viable version |
|---|---|---|
| Atelier + patron critique | École des Beaux-Arts, Paris, 19th c. | A monthly pin-up with three peers and one senior practitioner |
| The *esquisse* (sketch problem) | Beaux-Arts: the concept fixed in a short timed session, then developed without departing from it | 2 hours, one A3 sheet, no revisions to the parti afterwards |
| The **charrette** | Beaux-Arts Paris: the cart (*charrette*) wheeled round to collect drawings at the deadline; described in Zola's *L'Œuvre*, 1886 | A 3-day intensive; the National Charrette Institute standard is **at least 3–4 consecutive days** with feedback loops |
| Prix de Rome | Beaux-Arts competition; three trials over nearly three months; winner got a funded residency in Rome | Any open ideas competition, entered to a deadline |
| Vorkurs (preliminary course) | Bauhaus, founded **1 April 1919** by Walter Gropius in Weimar; Vorkurs run by Johannes Itten 1919–22, rewritten by László Moholy-Nagy in 1923 | Six weeks of pure material exercises before designing anything |
| Workshop apprenticeship | Bauhaus dual-master system; Weimar 1919–25, Dessau 1925–32, Berlin 1932–33 | One day a week on a real site or in a real workshop |
| The Grand Tour | c. 1660–1840s; young men of means, Dover → Paris → Alps → Turin, Milan, Florence, Venice, Rome, Naples | Two weeks a year of deliberate travel with a sketchbook and a tape |
| Trade apprenticeship first | Zumthor, cabinetmaking **1958–62**; Kéré, carpentry then a 1985 vocational scholarship; Ando, carpentry then self-teaching; Piano, his father's construction firm | Learn one trade to competent-journeyman level |
| Site years before design years | Wang Shu spent **nearly a decade from c. 1990 working with craftsmen with no design responsibility** | 12 months of site work before you draw a detail you expect to be built |

## 1. The sketchbook and drawing discipline

Drawing by hand is not a representation technique; it is the mechanism by which an architect thinks slowly enough to notice things. The strongest documented cases:

- **Tadao Ando**, with no architectural education at all, taught himself by making detailed sketchbooks on all his travels through Kyoto and Nara and through Europe, Africa and America in the 1960s, and by tracing every drawing in a second-hand Le Corbusier volume he found in an Osaka bookshop — then travelling to Marseille to stand in the Unité d'Habitation he had traced.
- **Álvaro Siza** works in small ink sketchbooks, redrawing the same corner from an imagined standing eye height dozens of times until the movement sequence resolves.
- **Peter Rich Architects** exhibited hand-drawn work at the 2018 Venice Biennale — in the digital era, hand drawing has become a distinguishing professional signal, not a nostalgia.

**The discipline, concretely.** One A5 or A6 hardbound sketchbook at all times. A dated entry every day, minimum ten minutes. Three kinds of entry, rotated:
1. **Observation** — something built, drawn from life: a window reveal, a gutter, a door threshold, a shading device, a bond pattern. Annotate dimensions estimated by pacing or by a folding rule.
2. **Analysis** — a plan or section of a building you have been inside, drawn from memory that evening. Memory drawing is the test of whether you actually looked.
3. **Proposition** — an idea for something, drawn small, in plan and section together on the same sheet.

Never erase. Number the books. After a year you have 365 dated pieces of evidence, and — this matters commercially — a portfolio artefact no competitor can fake.

## 2. Site visits and reading buildings

A building visit is worthless as tourism and valuable as forensic work. Go with a task list:

- **Instruments:** 5 m tape, folding rule, phone with compass, a cheap thermometer or a phone hygrometer, and the sketchbook.
- **Record, in order:** orientation and sun path; the section (where does the ground meet the wall, where does the wall meet the roof); the ventilation path (where does air enter, where does it leave, is there a stack); the structural bay and span; every junction that is hiding a movement joint or a damp-proof course; and how the building is failing — cracking, staining, spalling, rust bleed, thermal discomfort.
- **Then draw the building from memory that night**, and only afterwards check against your on-site notes. The delta between the two is your real learning.

**Riken Yamamoto's** formation is the ambitious version of this: as a young architect he travelled with his teacher **Hiroshi Hara** across the Mediterranean in 1972 and Central and South America in 1974, surveying settlements, and concluded that the threshold between public and private space was a universal device. That single observation became a fifty-year body of work and the 2024 Pritzker Prize. A comparable Namibian survey — Owambo homesteads, Nama mat houses, Windhoek's colonial civic buildings, Katutura's self-built extensions — is completely unclaimed territory.

## 3. Model making

Physical models do three jobs a computer model does not: they force you to decide how things are supported, they make scale bodily, and they let other people, including clients and builders, see the thing without training.

- **Concept models** — cardboard and foam, 30 minutes, disposable, made in numbers. Make ten bad ones rather than one careful one.
- **Sectional models** — cut through the building at 1:50, because the section is where climate and structure live (see `05_patterns-of-thinking.md`).
- **Form-finding models** — **Frei Otto** (Germany, Pritzker laureate 2015) derived tensile roofs and gridshells from soap films, hanging chains and physical minimal-surface experiments; the physical model *computed* the structure. Hanging-chain models for arches and vaults are the direct ancestor of the tile-vault geometry used at the Mapungubwe Interpretation Centre.
- **1:1 mock-ups** — Zumthor builds full-size material samples for finishes and junctions. On a small budget this is a one-square-metre panel of the actual wall, built by the actual mason, standing in the sun for a month.

## 4. Precedent study methodology

Precedent study means *redrawing*, not collecting images. The method:

1. Choose a building that solves a problem you have — not one you admire.
2. Obtain or reconstruct plan, section and elevation, and **redraw all three at a single consistent scale**, by hand, on the same sheet. Consistency of scale is the whole point: you learn dimensions, not shapes.
3. Draw a **comparative diagram set**: structure, circulation, enclosure, servicing, and — in a hot climate — solar exposure and air path.
4. Write **one page** answering: what was the constraint, what was the move, what did it cost, what would fail here.
5. File it. Twenty of these is a personal reference library worth more than any monograph.

Build the library around problem types, not around famous names: courtyard house in a hot dry climate; ventilated double roof; incremental/expandable dwelling; low-cost long span; vault without formwork; retrofit of a failing block.

## 5. The atelier and the critique tradition

The École des Beaux-Arts model — dominant from the 19th century into the early 20th — organised students into **ateliers** under a practising *patron*, progressing by competition rather than by year. Students proved basic drawing skill before advancing; the sequence culminated in the **Grand Prix de Rome**, whose three trials ran for nearly three months and whose reward was a funded residency in Rome. **Rafael Moneo** effectively repeated that path in the modern era: a fellowship to the Spanish Academy in Rome from 1962 to 1965, which he called "fundamental to my career", spent reading, travelling and building an intellectual network before he had a practice.

The **charrette** comes from the same building: at the deadline a cart was wheeled among the students to collect their drawings and models, so they worked *en charrette*, finishing on the cart. Today a charrette is a compressed collaborative design session — the National Charrette Institute standard is at least three to four consecutive days, with all necessary disciplines present at key decision points and repeated feedback loops between subgroups and the whole assembly. It has become the standard tool of participatory planning; documented uses include Midland, Perth (1997), Nashville's post-tornado recovery (1999) and Scarborough Renaissance (2002).

The **Bauhaus** contributed the other half: founded 1 April 1919 in Weimar by Walter Gropius, it put every entrant through the **Vorkurs**, a preliminary course in material, colour and form before any specialisation — Johannes Itten's version (1919–22) drawing on Fröbel and Cižek, Moholy-Nagy's rewrite from 1923 pushing toward functional New Objectivity — and then into workshops under a dual mastership of form and craft. It moved to Dessau in 1925 (new building inaugurated late 1926, Hannes Meyer director 1928–30, Mies van der Rohe 1930–32) and to rented Berlin premises in 1932, closing under Nazi pressure in 1933.

**How to run a critique with no school.** Four to six people, monthly, same time. Work pinned up before anyone speaks. Rules: the author presents for three minutes and then is silent; each critic must state (a) what the project is *actually* doing, (b) one thing that is working, (c) the single most damaging unresolved issue; the author writes it all down and does not defend. Invite one experienced practitioner or builder per session and pay them if you can. This is the highest-yield hour in the whole curriculum.

## 6. Apprenticeship and the trade first

The register in `01` makes an uncomfortable point: an unusual share of the very best modern architects handled materials professionally before they designed with them. Zumthor was a cabinetmaker's son and served a cabinetmaking apprenticeship from 1958 to 1962, then spent from 1967 analysing and restoring historic villages for the Canton of Graubünden before opening a practice in 1979 — roughly twenty years between apprenticeship and first independent building. Kéré was a carpenter, and went to Berlin in 1985 on a vocational scholarship to learn roofing and furniture-making. Piano grew up in a family of builders and worked in his father's construction company after graduating in 1964. Wang Shu spent nearly a decade from about 1990 on site with craftsmen with no design responsibility at all before founding Amateur Architecture Studio in 1997.

**Minimum viable version:** arrange one day a week on a live site or in a workshop for a year. Not observing — working. Learn to set out, to mix, to strike a line, to hang a door, to point a wall. You will detail differently forever afterwards, and the builders you eventually instruct will know within one sentence whether you have done it.

## 7. Competitions as learning

Architectural competitions are 2 500 years old — the Acropolis (448 BC), medieval cathedrals, and Brunelleschi's win for the Florence Cathedral dome in 1419. Open competition in the modern sense emerged in the late eighteenth century; nineteenth-century England ran over 2 500 competitions in five decades. RIBA published rules in 1839 and formal regulations in 1872; the UIA now issues the international guideline framework. Types: open vs invited; project vs **ideas**; single vs multi-stage (with shortlisted semi-finalists sometimes paid an honorarium); anonymous vs named; recurrent (Europan) vs one-off.

For a self-directed learner the value is not the prize. It is that a competition supplies the three things practice withholds: a **deadline**, a **defined problem**, and **published comparison** — you can see what everyone else did with the same brief. Enter ideas competitions specifically; they cost only time and they are where a position gets formed. Note that competitions have launched careers precisely from nowhere: Utzon's Sydney Opera House win (1957) is the canonical case, and Aravena's, Kéré's and Vally's early visibility all came from competitive selection rather than from patronage.

## 8. Travel and the Grand Tour tradition

The Grand Tour ran from roughly 1660 until the 1840s, when railways ended it: upper-class young Europeans, at about twenty-one, went Dover → Calais/Ostend/Le Havre → Paris (language, dancing, fencing, riding) → Geneva or Lausanne → over the Alps → Turin, Milan, Florence, Venice, Rome, Naples. Inigo Jones acted as *cicerone* to the Earl of Arundel's Italian tour of 1613–14. The architectural residue of the tradition is the **measured drawing**: you do not understand a building until you have measured it.

Modern equivalents in the register: Ando's self-funded 1960s study journeys; Murcutt's two years of travel after graduating in 1961; Yamamoto's expeditions with Hara; Moneo's Scandinavian travels to Utzon's office and Aalto's Helsinki; Radić's insistence that travel was essential to his education. **Regional version for a Namibian:** Windhoek's German colonial core; Swakopmund; the Owambo north; Kolmanskop; then, in southern Africa, Great Zimbabwe, Mapungubwe, the Ndebele homesteads of Mpumalanga, Cape Dutch werfs, Pretoria's Norman Eaton and Pretoria Regionalist houses, Johannesburg's modernist stock, and the Red Location Museum in Gqeberha. Budget two weeks a year and come back with measured drawings, not photographs.

## 9. Reading regimes

Read narrowly and repeatedly rather than widely and once. A working minimum:

- **On seeing and atmosphere:** Zumthor, *Thinking Architecture*; *Atmospheres*. Short, cheap, re-readable.
- **On the discipline's arguments:** Venturi, *Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture* (1966); Rossi, *The Architecture of the City*; Koolhaas, *Delirious New York* — the three books behind three Pritzker laureates' entire positions.
- **On construction as culture:** Frampton, *Studies in Tectonic Culture*.
- **On climate:** Olgyay, *Design with Climate*; Givoni; and any current SANS 10400-XA / SANS 204 commentary **[ZA]**.
- **On practice as a social act:** Aravena's ELEMENTAL publications; Lari's writing on "Barefoot Social Architecture"; Liu Jiakun's *Narrative Discourse and Low-Tech Strategy* (1997).
- **Local first:** the National Archives of Namibia, NCAQS records, and the SAIA journal archive contain more usable material for Namibian work than any imported monograph.

Method: one book per month, with a written two-page précis. Liu Jiakun wrote novels before he wrote buildings; writing is how a position gets tested.

## 10. Journaling and note systems

Keep three separate records and do not merge them:

1. **The sketchbook** (analogue, dated, never edited) — observation and idea.
2. **The precedent library** (one folder per building: redrawn plan/section/elevation at consistent scale, diagram set, one page of text).
3. **The project log** (per job: decisions, who said what, dimensions confirmed, site instructions, photographs of everything before it is covered up). This third one is not learning — it is the document that wins the FMB-style award and defends you in a dispute.

## The curriculum, assembled

**Daily (20–40 min)** — sketchbook entry; ten minutes of reading.
**Weekly** — one full day on site or in a workshop; one redrawn precedent.
**Monthly** — one peer critique session with work pinned up; one book précis; one concept-model exercise (ten models, one hour).
**Quarterly** — one *esquisse*: a two-hour timed sketch problem set by someone else, developed for two weeks without changing the parti; one 1:1 mock-up of a real detail.
**Annually** — one ideas competition entered to the deadline; two weeks of measured-drawing travel; one written piece published somewhere, however small.
**Once, early** — a trade apprenticeship or its equivalent: twelve months of real construction work.

Run for five years, that is 1 800 sketchbook pages, 250 site days, 260 redrawn precedents, 60 critiques, 60 books, 5 competitions and a trade. That is a formation. It is roughly what Zumthor, Kéré, Ando and Wang Shu each did, in different orders, and none of them started with money.
