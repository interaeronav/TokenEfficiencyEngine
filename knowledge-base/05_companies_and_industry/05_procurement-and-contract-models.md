# Contract forms used in southern Africa — JBCC, NEC, FIDIC, GCC 2015 and domestic building agreements

**Summary.** Four standard-form families dominate southern African construction, and they are not interchangeable. **JBCC** is the building industry's consensus form for South African and Namibian building work. **GCC 2015** (SAICE) is the civil engineering equivalent and the default for South African public infrastructure. **FIDIC** governs internationally financed and cross-border work, with a colour-coded suite whose colours encode who does the design and who carries the risk. **NEC3/NEC4** is the collaborative, management-driven suite, adopted heavily in the UK and Hong Kong and used in South Africa by Eskom among others. Below all of them sits the real volume of the industry: small domestic building work done on a quotation, a handshake, or nothing at all.

> ⚠️ **Do not amend a standard form casually.** Each of these suites is an interlocking risk allocation. Deleting a clause that looks unfavourable usually breaks two others. If a client's bespoke amendments run to more than a few pages, the form's case law and industry familiarity no longer protect you and you are effectively on a bespoke contract.

## JBCC — the South African and Namibian building standard **[ZA] [NA]**

The **Joint Building Contracts Committee NPC (JBCC®)** is a non-profit representative body of building owners and developers, professional consultants, and general and specialist contractors. Its documents "portray the consensus view of the Joint Building Contracts Committee of good practice and **an equitable distribution of contractual risk** in the building industry". **All JBCC documents are approved by the cidb** and are selected for use by most South African state sectors. JBCC is drafted and reviewed by standing Drafting and Review Committees established in 2017.

### The suite

Only five JBCC documents carry **edition numbers**; everything else is dated only, because the supporting forms are designed to be compatible across editions:

1. **Principal Building Agreement (PBA)** — the main contract between employer and contractor.
2. **Nominated/Selected Subcontract Agreement (N/S) —** back-to-back subcontract for nominated and selected subcontractors.
3. **Minor Works Agreement (MWA)** — a shortened form for smaller or simpler projects.
4. **Small and Simple Works Contract.**
5. **Direct Contractors' Contract.**

These are supported by a set of standard forms — payment certificates, practical completion lists, contract instructions, extension of time claims, and so on — designed to simplify contract administration.

**Editions.** JBCC published **revised 2024 editions** (announced 16 July 2024, with new cover pages). The changes from the previous **May 2018** editions "primarily involve the correction of errors" and are described by JBCC as minimal, so users will not encounter significant differences. **The May 2018 editions remain valid for use until further notice** and remain on sale in hard copy while stock lasts. Documents are available in hard copy from stockists and electronically on the **e-JBCC platform** at ejbcc.co.za (launched June 2024).

### How the JBCC PBA works

- **Parties:** Employer and Contractor. The **Principal Agent** (usually the architect) administers the contract, issues instructions, certifies payment and practical completion, and grants extensions of time. Other **agents** (QS, engineers) have defined delegated functions.
- **Payment:** monthly **interim payment certificates** valuing work executed plus materials on site, less **retention** and less previous payments. The certificate is the payment trigger; the employer must pay the certified amount within the stated period.
- **Security:** the contract offers alternatives — a **payment guarantee** by the employer and a **construction guarantee** (or retention) by the contractor. This two-way security is one of JBCC's better features and is routinely and unwisely deleted by employers.
- **Completion:** **practical completion** (the works can be used for their intended purpose), then **works completion** (outstanding items done), then the **defects liability period**, then **final completion** and the final payment certificate.
- **Time:** the contractor claims **extension of time** under listed circumstances, some carrying cost and some not. Late claims are barred by time limits.
- **Penalties:** a stated daily penalty for late completion, deducted from certificates.
- **Disputes:** negotiation, then **adjudication**, then **arbitration** (or court, depending on election).

### Risk allocation
JBCC is genuinely balanced by international standards — it was drafted by a committee containing employers, consultants and contractors, and it shows. Design risk sits with the employer; construction risk with the contractor; ground conditions are dealt with expressly; and both parties provide security.

### Traps
- **Deleting the payment guarantee** while retaining the construction guarantee turns a balanced contract into a one-sided one and drives contractors' prices up.
- **Nomination.** If the employer nominates a subcontractor who fails, the risk is not simply the contractor's. Get the nomination mechanism right or use selected subcontractors.
- **The principal agent's dual role.** The principal agent is paid by the employer but must certify impartially. Where the principal agent is also the employer's employee, expect disputes.
- **Time bars.** Extension of time and loss-and-expense claims are time-barred. Contractors lose valid claims to diary-keeping failures more often than to merit.
- **Provisional sums and prime cost amounts** are a common route to uncontrolled cost growth.

## GCC 2015 — the South African civil engineering standard **[ZA]**

The **General Conditions of Contract for Construction Works, Third Edition 2015**, published by the **South African Institution of Civil Engineering (SAICE)**, is the default form for South African public civil infrastructure — SANRAL, water boards, municipalities, provincial roads departments. It is one of the four cidb-endorsed contract families.

Characteristics:
- **The Engineer** administers the contract and certifies, with the traditional dual role of employer's agent and impartial certifier.
- **Remeasurement against a bill of quantities** is the normal price basis, so quantity risk sits with the employer and rate risk with the contractor.
- Provisions for **extension of time, price adjustment (CPA formulae), variations, and dayworks**.
- **Adjudication** followed by arbitration.
- Drafted in South African statutory and construction context, with terminology familiar to SA engineers.

**When to use it:** South African public roads, water, sewer, bulk earthworks and structures where the employer's engineer has completed the design. **When not to use it:** contractor-designed work, building work (use JBCC), or internationally financed projects where the funder requires FIDIC.

> ⚠️ The GCC 2015 detail in this section — clause numbering, exact payment periods and the price adjustment formula — is **not verified against the published document** in this file. SAICE sells GCC 2015 and it is not freely available online. Treat clause-level statements about GCC as needing verification before use.

## FIDIC — the international suite **[global]**

FIDIC (the International Federation of Consulting Engineers) publishes the contracts used on almost all internationally financed construction. The colours are shorthand for the risk allocation.

### The history
- **1987:** Red Book (Works of Civil Engineering Construction) and Yellow Book (Electrical and Mechanical Works including Erection on Site).
- **1995:** Orange Book (Design-Build and Turnkey).
- **September 1999:** the "rainbow suite" of four new standard forms, all marked *First Edition 1999*:
  - **Construction Contract** (the new Red Book) — works designed by the Employer.
  - **MDB Harmonised Edition** of the Construction Contract, licensed to the multilateral development banks from 2005 for bank-financed projects only.
  - **Plant and Design-Build Contract** (the new Yellow Book) — works designed by the Contractor; supersedes both the 1987 Yellow Book and the 1995 Orange Book.
  - **EPC/Turnkey Contract** (the Silver Book).
  - Plus the **Short Form of Contract** and a **Dredgers Contract**.
- **2017:** Second Editions of the **Red, Yellow and Silver Books** (reprinted 2022 with amendments), and the **White Book** (Client/Consultant Model Services Agreement) 5th edition.
- **2019:** **Emerald Book** — Conditions of Contract for Underground Works.
- **2021:** **Green Book** — Short Form of Contract, 2nd edition.

### Choosing, in FIDIC's own words

**Small, short or simple work** — a contract price under roughly **US$500 000**, or construction time under about **six months**, or relatively simple or repetitive work: use the **Short Form (Green Book)**. It does not matter who designs, or what engineering discipline is involved.

**The Employer (or the Engineer) does most of the design** — traditional infrastructure, buildings, hydropower; the Engineer administers the contract, monitors construction and certifies payment; the Employer is kept fully informed and can vary the works; payment against bills of quantities or lump sums for approved work done: use the **Construction Contract (Red Book)**.

**The Contractor does most of the design** — plant and equipment designed to the Employer's outline or performance specification, or design-build/turnkey where the project must fulfil the **Employer's Requirements**, with the Engineer administering the contract and certifying payment: use the **Plant and Design-Build Contract (Yellow Book)**.

**Fixed-price turnkey with maximum price and time certainty** — the Contractor takes total responsibility for design and construction; the Employer does not wish to be involved in day-to-day progress provided the end result meets the specified performance criteria; the project is organised on a **strictly two-party basis without an Engineer**; and the Employer (or the sponsors and lenders) is **willing to pay more** in return for the Contractor bearing the extra risk of price and time certainty: use the **EPC/Turnkey Contract (Silver Book)**.

FIDIC gives two important qualifications on the Silver Book:
- For **infrastructure** projects on a fixed-price turnkey basis, **if underground works in uncertain or difficult ground conditions are likely, the risk of unforeseen ground conditions should be borne by the Employer**, and the Yellow Book's Sub-Clause 4.12 provisions would be appropriate.
- For **building** projects where the Employer or Architect has done some or most of the design, the Silver Book **may** be chosen with suitable modification regarding design responsibility.

### Traps
- **Silver Book on the wrong project.** The Silver Book deliberately gives the Contractor almost all risk, including site data and ground conditions. Using it on a tunnelling or deep-foundation project without modification is how contractors go bankrupt and employers end up in arbitration anyway. FIDIC itself warns against exactly this.
- **The Engineer's impartiality.** The Red and Yellow Books require the Engineer to make fair determinations. Employers who instruct the Engineer to protect the employer's position destroy the mechanism.
- **Sub-Clause 20 time bars.** FIDIC claim notices are strictly time-barred (28 days in the 1999 suite). This is the single most litigated provision in international construction.
- **Particular Conditions.** Every FIDIC contract in practice comes with Particular Conditions that amend the General Conditions. **Read those first** — they are where the risk actually sits.
- **DAAB/DAB.** The Dispute Avoidance/Adjudication Board is a real benefit and is frequently deleted for cost reasons, to everyone's eventual expense.

## NEC3 and NEC4 — the management-led suite

### History
Commissioned in **1986** by the newly formed legal affairs panel of the **Institution of Civil Engineers**, which asked **Martin Barnes** to draft "a radical new contract which would stimulate and not frustrate the application of good project management". A consultative draft appeared in **1991**; the final **New Engineering Contract** in **1993**. Early adopters included British Airports Authority, National Power, Scottish Hydroelectric — **and Eskom in South Africa**. The second edition (1995) introduced the **Engineering and Construction Contract (ECC)** name plus the Professional Services Contract, Adjudicator's Contract and short forms. **NEC3** launched in **2005**; the Term Service Contract and Framework Contract followed; the Supply Contract in 2010; the suite reached 39 documents in April 2013. Hong Kong trialled NEC3 on 30 pilot projects worth £1.2 bn from 2006 and **adopted NEC3 as the default contract for its £7 bn-a-year public works programme from 2015**. **NEC4** was published in **2017**.

### What NEC4 added over NEC3
New forms — the **Design Build and Operate Contract (DBO)**, the **Alliance Contract (ALC)** and the **Facilities Management Contract (FMC)** — plus improved clarity, and new support for **dispute avoidance, early contractor involvement and quality management**. The style, layout, terminology and core project management processes are unchanged from NEC3.

### The suite, grouped
- **Works:** ECC (Engineering and Construction Contract), ECSC (Short Contract) and their subcontracts; **ALC** for integrated delivery teams; **DBOC** for works extending into operation.
- **Services:** PSC (Professional Service Contract), PSS (Subcontract), PSSC (Short Contract); TSC (Term Service Contract) suite for asset management and maintenance; FMC for facilities management.
- **Supply:** Supply Contract and Supply Short Contract for high-value plant and materials through to stationery.

### The main options (ECC pricing options)
NEC's own framing of risk allocation maps directly onto the options:
- **Straightforward work, supplier can carry the risk** → a **fixed price** contract (priced contract with activity schedule or with bill of quantities).
- **Complex or large, risk pooled** → a **target cost** contract, sharing savings and overspends through a **pain/gain mechanism**.
- **Client best placed to manage the risk** → a **cost-reimbursable** contract (supplier paid costs plus an agreed fee), or a **management contract** (supplier paid a fee for managing the subcontractors delivering the works).

Optional **X clauses** let parties address issues up front and reflect public-policy priorities such as fair payment or **local content** provisions — directly relevant to Namibian and South African preference regimes. The suite also supports **framework contracts**.

### The mechanisms that make NEC different
- An express obligation on all parties **"to act in a spirit of mutual trust and co-operation"**.
- The **early warning process**: parties must give each other early warning of matters that could affect cost, completion or performance; a **register of early warning matters** is maintained; **early warning meetings** are held regularly to assign mitigating actions; the programme and budget forecasts must be updated to reflect agreed actions, producing **a single source of truth about project status**.
- A comprehensive list of **compensation events** describing when the supplier is entitled to more time or money, with a clear process for logging them.
- Escalating dispute resolution: senior representatives → adjudicator → tribunal, plus an optional **Disputes Avoidance Board** that periodically inspects work in progress and issues non-binding recommendations.

### Traps
- **NEC is administratively demanding.** The programme must be kept current and accepted; compensation events must be quoted and assessed within tight periods; failure to run the machinery properly is how NEC projects go wrong. A team without NEC-trained staff will do worse on NEC than on JBCC.
- **The Project Manager's role is powerful.** An inexperienced Project Manager who fails to reply within the stated period can create deemed acceptances.
- **Target cost is not a fixed price.** Clients who choose Option C and then behave as though they have price certainty are misunderstanding their own contract.

## What a small residential builder should actually use

For a house, an alteration, a boundary wall or a small commercial fit-out in Namibia or South Africa, JBCC's **Minor Works Agreement** or **Small and Simple Works Contract** is the correct starting point. But the honest answer for most small jobs is that a properly drafted **written agreement covering eight things** is worth more than an unfamiliar standard form filled in badly.

**The eight things any small building agreement must contain** — the CIF's own advice to Namibian clients, expanded:

1. **The parties**, correctly named — full legal entity names, registration numbers, physical addresses.
2. **The works**, described by reference to specific drawings and a specification, each identified by number and revision date. "Build a house as per plan" is not a scope.
3. **The price**, split into **materials and labour**, and stating whether it is a lump sum or a rate-based estimate. State VAT treatment explicitly.
4. **The programme** — a start date, a completion date, and what happens if it is missed.
5. **Payment terms** — how much is paid up front, the stage or monthly payment points, what triggers each payment (a stage certified as complete, not a date), the retention percentage, and when retention is released.
6. **Variations** — that no additional work is done without a **written** instruction stating the cost and the time effect. This clause alone prevents most small-builder disputes.
7. **Insurance** — contractor's public liability, workmen's compensation, and contract works (all-risks) insurance; who insures the works and to what value; the client to see the certificate.
8. **Defects** — a defects liability period (three to twelve months is normal for small works), what it covers, and how defects are notified and made good.

Add, if you can: **a dispute clause pointing to adjudication or mediation before litigation**, and **a materials-supplier letter** confirming the builder's account is in good standing.

> ⚠️ **[NA]** In Namibia there is **no NHBRC equivalent and no contractor licensing**. A homeowner's protections are entirely contractual and reputational. The CIF explicitly recommends contracting only CIF-registered companies, verifying the registration certificate by phone, and obtaining references from completed and in-progress sites, from material suppliers, from subcontractors and from the contractor's bank. **[ZA]** In South Africa, a new home for an owner-occupier must be built by a builder registered with the **NHBRC** and enrolled with it — this is statutory, and it is the single most important consumer protection in South African residential construction.

> ⚠️ **Never pay a large deposit against nothing.** The commonest failure mode in small residential construction is a builder who takes 30–50% up front, buys materials for another job, and disappears. Tie every payment to physical, verifiable completion of a defined stage — foundations cast and inspected, walls to wall-plate, roof structure erected and covered, first fix complete, practical completion — and hold retention.

## Choosing, in one table

| Situation | Use |
|---|---|
| House, alteration, wall, small fit-out **[NA] [ZA]** | JBCC Minor Works or Small and Simple Works; otherwise a written agreement covering the eight items above |
| Commercial or institutional building, employer-designed **[ZA] [NA]** | JBCC Principal Building Agreement |
| Subcontract under a JBCC main contract | JBCC Nominated/Selected Subcontract Agreement |
| SA public roads, water, bulk civils | GCC 2015 |
| Donor- or DFI-financed infrastructure (AfDB, World Bank, KfW) | FIDIC — usually the MDB Harmonised Construction Contract or Red Book |
| Contractor-designed plant, power, process, renewables | FIDIC Yellow Book |
| Project-financed fixed-price turnkey | FIDIC Silver Book — but move ground risk back to the Employer if there are significant underground works |
| Complex, uncertain-scope work with a capable client team | NEC4 ECC Option C (target cost) or the Alliance Contract |
| Term maintenance and facilities management | NEC4 TSC or FMC |

## Open questions

- **GCC 2015** could not be verified from a primary source in this pass; SAICE does not publish the document text freely. Clause numbers, payment periods, the CPA formula and dispute provisions are stated at a general level only and are marked as needing verification.
- The **current JBCC PBA edition number** (the 2024 editions supersede the May 2018 editions, which JBCC states remain valid) is not stated numerically here because JBCC's agreements page did not render for automated retrieval.
- FIDIC Red/Yellow/Silver 1999 First Editions remain in wide use alongside the 2017 Second Editions; which is used on a given project is a matter of the employer's or funder's choice and must be checked on each contract.
- Whether Namibian public entities have a standard prescribed form of contract for works under the Public Procurement Act 15 of 2015, and if so which, is not verified here.
- The exact NEC ECC main-option lettering (A through F) is stated in the NEC contract itself; the NEC public pages consulted describe the pricing models functionally rather than by letter, so the letter assignments are not asserted in this file.
