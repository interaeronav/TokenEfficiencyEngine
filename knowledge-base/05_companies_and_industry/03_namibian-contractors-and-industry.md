# The Namibian construction industry — CIF, contractors, public procurement and how a small builder gets work

**Summary.** Namibian construction is a **small, public-spending-dependent, lightly regulated** industry. It contributed roughly **1.4% of GDP in 2024** with total output of about **N$4.3 billion**, and there is **no statutory licensing of contractors at all** — the Construction Industries Federation of Namibia (CIF) states this plainly on its own website. The industry's central political conflict is the displacement of Namibian contractors by foreign, principally Chinese, firms on public works, which is legally structured by the **Public Procurement Act 15 of 2015**, the **Central Procurement Board of Namibia (CPBN)**, and the national-preference regime in Part 11 of that Act. For a small builder, work comes from four channels — private clients through referral, subcontracting to larger contractors, quotations under the procurement threshold, and formal tender — and the practical gatekeepers are the CIF tender letter, a track record, insurance, and access to working capital.

## The Construction Industries Federation of Namibia (CIF)

The CIF describes itself as **"the national voice of Namibia's construction industry"** — an autonomous, non-governmental and apolitical organisation governed by an Executive Committee of volunteer members elected at the annual general meeting from a range of construction-related sectors. Its membership spans multinational building and civil contractors, but "the bulk consists of smaller contractors and SMEs", plus the retail and wholesale building material trade, manufacturers and suppliers of materials and equipment, and specialist trades — electricians, joiners, plumbers, painters and steelworkers.

Three main categories: **Contractors**, **Trade members** (suppliers and affiliates), and a deliberately low-priced **SME category**.

### What CIF membership actually buys

Eight services, in the CIF's own ordering: (1) employment legislation advice via a contracted HR and labour consultant; (2) industrial and labour relations — the CIF negotiates the sectoral collective agreement with MANWU and lobbies for its gazetting; (3) provision of the industry pension fund; (4) government relations and lobbying on bills affecting construction; (5) health and safety training, guidance and referral to consultants; (6) marketing and public relations; (7) provision of information; (8) recruitment, education and training, including courses such as Working at Height (referencing the Occupational Health and Safety Act 85 of 1993) and Fire Fighting.

> ⚠️ Because Namibia has no contractor licensing regime, **the CIF's Code of Conduct is the only industry-level consumer protection that exists** — and the CIF is explicit that it "does not have any enforcement capacity". Its formal advice to a Namibian client is: ask to see the CIF registration certificate, and phone +264 61 417300 to verify it. If you contract a non-member, "the Federation will not be able to assist you with a complaint" and "your only remedy for unsatisfactory work then would be to instigate legal proceedings."

## Public procurement: the Public Procurement Act 15 of 2015

The Act came into force on **1 April 2017** and was substantially amended by **Act 3 of 2022**. Its long title covers the regulation of procurement of goods, works and services, the letting or hiring of anything, the acquisition or granting of rights, the disposal of assets by public entities, and **"preferences to categories of persons, goods manufactured, mined, extracted, produced or grown in Namibia, to Namibian registered small and medium enterprises"** and related empowerment categories.

### The institutional architecture

| Body | Function |
|---|---|
| **Minister of Finance** | Determines procurement policy including preferences, classification and categories of Namibian manufacturers, suppliers, contractors and service providers; issues Codes of Good Practice under s 70 |
| **Procurement Policy Unit (PPU)** | Policy, thresholds, monitoring, compliance; may refer non-compliance to the Namibian Police and Anti-Corruption Commission |
| **Central Procurement Board of Namibia (CPBN)** — s 8 | A juristic person that (a) conducts the bidding process on behalf of public entities for contracts **exceeding the prescribed threshold**, (b) enters into contracts on its own or a public entity's behalf, and (c) directs and supervises accounting officers in managing implementation of contracts it awarded |
| **Public entity procurement committees, procurement management units and bid evaluation committees** | Run procurement below the CPBN threshold |
| **Review Panel** — s 58 | Statutory review of procurement decisions; its decisions are made under s 60 |

The CPBN's own stated mission is "to ethically and efficiently manage procurement on behalf of public entities", with values of value for money, integrity, transparency, accountability, responsiveness and teamwork.

### Procurement methods

- **Open advertised bidding (s 28)** — invitation published in a newspaper of wide circulation, in selected international media for international bidding, and on the **public procurement portal**. A public entity may confer an advantage or preference to Namibian goods, services, suppliers or empowerment-category persons in an open advertised process.
- **Open national bidding (s 29)** — participation may be limited to **Namibian citizens**, or to a company, close corporation, co-operative, trust, partnership or joint venture registered in Namibia **in which Namibian citizens own 51% equity**. The limitation must be stated in the invitation.
- **Open international bidding (s 30)** — used where the value exceeds the prescribed threshold, or the goods/services are not competitively available from more than one Namibian supplier, or there is no response to national bidding.
- **Restricted bidding (s 31)** — where only a limited number of suppliers exist, or where the cost of evaluating many bids is disproportionate and the value is below threshold (then solicit from a **minimum of five suppliers**), or from a pre-approved supplier eligibility list.
- **Request for sealed quotations (s 32)** — for readily available standard goods, **small services**, or **small works**. This is the practical route by which a small builder gets government work.
- **Direct procurement** and **emergency procurement** — narrow exceptions.
- **Bid security or a bid securing declaration (s 45)** — the bidding documents must state the requirement; a bidder can forfeit the security in defined circumstances.

### Preferences — Part 11 (sections 69–72)

- **s 69** — despite anything to the contrary, the Minister may grant preferential treatment in pursuance of the government's developmental and empowerment policies.
- **s 70** — the Minister **must** issue **Codes of Good Practice on preferences** by notice in the Gazette, defining local preferences, price preferences, national preferences and "goods manufactured in Namibia"; setting qualification criteria (equity, labour, produce); setting indicators, weights and targets; and **giving priority to women and youth**, primarily to women disadvantaged by past discriminatory laws or practices. The Code of Good Practice on Preferences was launched by the Ministry of Finance to ease market access for youth, women, SMEs and manufacturers and to grant national preference to Namibian suppliers.
- **s 71 — national preferences.** The Board or public entity may limit participation on the basis of citizenship, and **must give preference and prescribed weights** to (i) goods manufactured, mined, extracted or grown in Namibia, and (ii) **works and services provided by Namibian citizens**. To qualify for the margin of preference a company or close corporation must be incorporated or registered in Namibia, have the **majority of members' interest or paid-up share capital owned by the Government or Namibian citizens**, and have a major part of net profits accruing to Namibian citizens with no contrary arrangements. Joint ventures must have member companies registered in Namibia, majority beneficial ownership and majority shares held by Namibian citizens, and a JV agreement containing the Minister's prescribed minimum terms and no arrangement diverting a major share of profit or dividend to non-Namibians.
- **s 72 — exclusive preference to local suppliers.** The Minister may prescribe categories of procurement **reserved exclusively for local suppliers** below a stated value. If the Board or public entity does not proceed with a reserved local supplier and procures unrestricted, it must record the reasons in the procurement file. If only one acceptable offer is received from a qualified local supplier it may still be awarded, subject to the price being fair and reasonable. If no acceptable local offer is received, the reservation is withdrawn and the requirement re-solicited unrestricted.
- **s 44(10)** — where bidding documents provide for a margin of preference to local suppliers, the applicable margin is at the rate determined by the Minister.

> ⚠️ **The 51% Namibian-ownership test in s 29 and the majority-Namibian-ownership test in s 71 are the two provisions a small Namibian contractor must be able to satisfy on paper.** They are the mechanical basis of "local preference" — and equally, they are what a foreign contractor structures around by forming a joint venture with a Namibian partner. The Act anticipates this: the JV tests in s 71(3)(b) exist precisely to defeat fronting arrangements.

### The 2026 regulation reform

Revised public procurement regulations **came into operation on 4 August 2026**. According to the Ministry of Finance's Procurement Policy Unit (deputy director Willibarth Haraseb), they bring **more than 80 additional public entities** — including subsidiaries of public enterprises and entities created after the previous regulations — into the framework, give those entities greater scope to procure without relying on centralised processes, **revise the procurement thresholds** originally set when the Act came into operation, and are intended to reduce administrative delays, decongest the CPBN and broaden supplier opportunity. A **Public Procurement Amendment Bill, 2025** has been through regional consultations and proposes, among other things, the establishment of a **Public Procurement Regulatory Authority**.

## Chinese contractors in Namibian public works — the central controversy

Chinese contractors are now embedded in Namibian public infrastructure at every scale:

- **Port of Walvis Bay New Container Terminal** — built by **China Harbour Engineering Company (CHEC)**, a CCCC subsidiary, on **40 hectares of land reclaimed from the bay**. Construction commenced mid-2014 and the terminal was commissioned on 24 August 2019 (Namport also records commissioning by the late President Hage Geingob in November 2019; the official launch event was 2 August 2019). It added **600 m of quay wall to the existing 1 800 m**, created berths 9, 10 and 11, and has a capacity of at least **750 000 TEU per annum**. The **African Development Bank approved a US$200 million loan in 2013** for the expansion, including four ship-to-shore cranes; AfDB stated the expansion would raise handling capacity from 355 000 to 1 005 000 TEU. CHEC's own representative described Walvis Bay as one of the company's most challenging projects. Container handling operations were **concessioned to Terminal Investment Limited (TiL, Switzerland) through Terminal Investment Namibia (TiN) for 25 years, effective 1 October 2024**.
- **Sores !Gaib 100 MW solar PV power station** near Rosh Pinah, Karas Region — NamPower contracted a Chinese-led joint venture of **China Jiangxi International Economic and Technical Cooperation Co. Ltd and Zhejiang Chint New Energy Development Co. Ltd** for EPC; groundbreaking 19 June 2025, roughly 18 months' construction.
- **NamWater pipeline work** — the Namibia Local Business Association (NALOBA) publicly criticised the award of a **N$263.7 million water pipeline tender to a Chinese firm**.

The resulting political friction is continuous and well documented:

- **CIF vs NamWater.** NamWater justified sidelining local contractors by citing poor workmanship, non-completion and project abandonment, with about **five projects valued at N$315 million** incomplete. CIF chief executive **Bärbel Kirchner** responded that NamWater blames local contractors "without differentiation and without being transparent about which contractor they are referring to", and that the underlying problem lies in NamWater's own procurement.
- **Prequalification as an exclusion mechanism.** Simonis Storm noted that only **three Namibian construction companies** made the shortlist for the Roads Authority's Karibib–Usakos road reconstruction, and asked pointedly whether local firms will be able to participate in the planned mega-projects in the south "if local construction companies cannot even participate in the construction of a highway between two small towns". The CIF contested the decision on the basis that the financial prequalification and technical requirements were unreasonable for Namibian firms.
- **Donor tender rules override local preference.** The Roads Authority's CEO Conrad Lutombi explained that the Karibib–Usakos process was subject to **KfW Development Bank tender rules**, under a loan agreement signed on **13 December 2022** between KfW and the Government of Namibia under the NDP5 support programme. This is the general case: **concessional foreign finance carries the funder's procurement rules, which can lawfully displace the Namibian preference regime.**
- **AfDB report dispute.** NALOBA and the CIF jointly criticised an African Development Bank report claiming Namibian companies lack the capacity to manage infrastructure projects and cannot compete against international firms.
- **Political intervention.** Minister of Works and Transport **Veikko Nekundi** instructed state-owned enterprises to stop awarding high-value contracts solely to foreign companies, stating that local businesses must be part of tenders **worth N$100 million and above**: "This thing of issuing N$100 million contracts to foreign companies, money flying out of the country, cannot continue."
- **CIF and MANWU jointly** called for the cancellation and re-advertising of parastatal tenders — Roads Authority, NamWater, the NAMFISA head office, NamPower's 70 MWp Rosh Pinah project — on the grounds that local contractors were excluded.

## Sector size and health

- **Share of the economy:** ≈1.4% of GDP (2024); output ≈N$4.3 bn; forecast +8.5% in 2025 on the back of monetary easing (the Bank of Namibia cut rates by 100 basis points from late 2024), public infrastructure investment and renewed private confidence.
- **2026 outlook:** Simonis Storm expects construction growth to moderate to **4–6%**, with public infrastructure spending as the main driver — bulk water supply upgrades, pipeline replacement in northern and coastal regions, electricity transmission strengthening and renewable-related grid infrastructure — constrained "by fiscal sequencing, procurement timelines, and execution capacity rather than funding intent alone".
- **Windhoek building activity (a useful high-frequency proxy):** **163 building plans approved in February 2026**, up from 109 in January (+49.5%) and up 7.9% year on year; approved value rose from **N$101.8 million to N$145.8 million**. The first two months of 2026 recorded **272 approved plans** in Windhoek.
- **Structural constraints:** delays in green hydrogen and oil and gas projects, limited fiscal space, and **shortages of serviced land**.
- **The long-run upside:** Simonis Storm identifies green hydrogen as potentially the largest prospective source of construction demand — the **Hyphen Hydrogen Energy** project in the //Kharas Region targets 2 GW of electrolysis with capital expenditure expected to exceed **US$10 billion** at full build-out, in a country whose entire annual GDP is roughly US$14–15 billion. Bank of Namibia governor Ebson Uanguta has estimated that developing the **Venus** oil discovery could require about **US$17 billion**. Both figures should be read as investment estimates, not committed spend.
- **The data gap:** the CIF has publicly called on government to release the **Labour Force Survey from the 2023 Population and Housing Census**, saying the sector "continues to function without any current employment data" and cannot quantify job losses or plan retraining.

## How a small Namibian builder actually gets work

There are exactly four routes.

### 1. Private clients, by referral
The largest source of work for small builders, and the least documented. The client is a homeowner, small business or farmer. The CIF's own guidance to those clients tells you what you will be judged on:
- **References** — completed projects the client can inspect, plus sites in progress.
- **CIF registration certificate** — and the client is told to phone the CIF to verify it.
- **Financial stability** — how long you have been trading, references from material suppliers, subcontractors and your bank. A letter from your material supplier protects the client against a supplier's claim over the property if you fail to pay.
- **Insurance** — a certificate of insurance covering workers' compensation, property damage and personal liability.
- **A signed contract or accepted written proposal** setting out the work, the material and labour cost, the duration, start and finish dates, and **payment terms including how much is paid up front**.

### 2. Subcontracting to established contractors
The CIF states that it is "frequently being asked by its more established contractors, to provide details of SMEs for sub-contracting purposes", and that larger contractors "are encouraged to subcontract some of their businesses and thereby supporting our efforts of transfer of skills". Joining the CIF as an SME member at N$1 228 per year puts a small builder on that list and into the CIF's networking events. **This is the single highest-leverage, lowest-cost action a small Namibian builder can take.**

### 3. Quotations below threshold — the practical route into public work
Under **s 32** of the Public Procurement Act, a public entity may use the **request for sealed quotations** method for **small works**. Under **s 31(1)(b)** restricted bidding must solicit from a minimum of five suppliers where the value is below threshold. Below-threshold work — school and clinic maintenance, minor town council contracts, regional council work, government office repairs — is procured by the individual public entity, not the CPBN, and this is where a Grade-equivalent small builder can realistically compete. The 2026 regulations expanded the number of entities procuring in this way.

### 4. Formal tender
For anything above the threshold you are bidding against the CPBN process, with bid security, formal prequalification, audited financials and a track record. The **CIF tender letter** is the entry document, and the CIF publishes its procedure:

> **CIF tender letter application — a two-day process.** Provide copies of the **company owner's ID** and the **company registration**. Complete the tender letter application form. Pay **N$80.00** (the cost of the letter and the Government Gazette). Attend a **compulsory presentation on the Government Gazette the following day, promptly at 09:30** — no tender letter is issued if the presentation has not been attended; late arrivals must attend the next day's presentation. The owner must collect the letter in person, or a representative may collect it with a letter of authority signed by the owner. If you have applied before and are on the CIF database, only the completed application form and payment of **N$75.00** are required, with the owner showing original ID. It remains a two-day process; collection at 09:30.

If a public procurement decision goes against you, the statutory remedy is the **Review Panel under s 58**, whose decisions are made under s 60.

### What actually stops small Namibian builders winning work
1. **Prequalification thresholds** set at levels no Namibian SME meets — financial capacity, minimum turnover, comparable project value.
2. **Bid security** requirements that tie up working capital or require a bank guarantee an SME cannot obtain.
3. **Donor procurement rules** (KfW, AfDB, World Bank) that lawfully override national preference.
4. **Cash flow.** Public entities pay slowly; a small builder financing two months of work on a 3% margin cannot survive one late certificate.
5. **No track record, and no way to build one** — the classic circularity, which the s 72 reservation mechanism and the SME preference categories exist to break, and which they only partially do.
6. **Disputes end in court.** Namibian construction disputes go to the High Court and, increasingly, to court-connected mediation — a 2026 dispute over N$800 000 of unpaid construction work between Manani Investments CC and August 26 Construction (Pty) Ltd was referred by Judge Shafimana Ueitele to accredited mediation rather than trial. Litigation is slower and more expensive than the value of most small builders' claims.

## Open questions

- **The actual threshold values** (the rand/dollar figures above which the CPBN, rather than the public entity, must run the procurement) are set by regulation and were revised with effect from 4 August 2026. They are not stated here because the current regulation text was not retrieved. This is the single most important missing number in this file — verify against the current Public Procurement Regulations before advising anyone.
- The margin of preference percentage determined by the Minister under s 44(10) is not stated here.
- Whether the Public Procurement Amendment Bill, 2025 has been enacted, and the status of the proposed **Public Procurement Regulatory Authority**, is unconfirmed.
