# South African contractors — the industry, the collapse, the collusion settlement and what remains

**Summary.** South Africa had, until about 2010, one of the strongest contracting industries in the developing world: half a dozen JSE-listed groups with real engineering depth, exportable skills and order books swollen by the 2010 FIFA World Cup and Eskom's new-build programme. Fifteen years later that industry has been hollowed out. **Basil Read and Group Five went into business rescue** (2018 and 2019 respectively) and effectively ceased to exist as listed groups; **Aveng** dismantled itself into two remaining businesses; **Murray & Roberts**, once the country's flagship contractor, adopted a business rescue plan on **8 April 2025**. What remains as healthy, listed, cash-generating South African contractors is essentially **WBHO** and **Raubex**, plus **Stefanutti Stocks** in recovery and a set of unlisted firms — Concor, Motheo, Tiber, GVK-Siya Zama, Liviero, Trencon — operating below them. Underlying all of it is the **cidb Register of Contractors**, which grades contractors 1 to 9 and functions as the licence to trade with the state.

## Key facts — the cidb grading system **[ZA]**

A cidb grade is written as **one digit and two letters**, e.g. `4GB` — grade 4, general building. Emerging contractors carry four letters, e.g. `4GBPE`. Registration is valid for three years and must be updated annually.

**Registration fees (cidb, as published 2026):** admin fee R450 (grades 1–2), R750 (grades 3–9); annual fee R0 (grade 1), R250 (2), R350 (3), R900 (4), R1 750 (5), R3 500 (6), R9 000 (7), R29 000 (8), R55 000 (9).

> ⚠️ The Construction Industry Development (CID) Regulations of 2004, as amended, **bind public sector clients to award construction contracts only to cidb-registered contractors**. Subcontractors do not currently need to register. Joint ventures do not register as such — each partner registers individually and the combined grading is derived using the cidb Joint Venture Calculator. Home builders are regulated by the **NHBRC** and are exempt from the RoC; labour-only contractors are also exempt.

**Potentially Emerging (PE) status** allows a contractor to be awarded a contract **one grade above** its registered level, provided the client has a development programme in place and provides support.

## The 2013 Competition Commission fine and the 2016/2017 Voluntary Rebuild settlement

Two separate but linked events. Conflating them is the most common error in commentary on this topic.

### 1. The 2013 fast-track settlement — a competition-law penalty

In **June 2013** the Competition Commission concluded a fast-track settlement process with **15 construction companies**, which paid a combined **R1.46 billion** in administrative penalties for collusive tendering. The conduct centred on **cover pricing and bid rigging** on public infrastructure, prominently including 2010 FIFA World Cup stadium contracts. The Commission's finding was that the firms had "an agreement in terms of which firms which were not interested in the projects or winning the tenders, or were not allocated to a project, would submit cover bids to ensure that those interested in particular bids, won them" — enabling the cartel to raise prices and pre-allocate work without the client's knowledge.

**Murray & Roberts alone paid R309 million** and was found to have committed **17 transgressions** of the Competition Act. It was not penalised for several further projects because the conduct fell outside the three-year window before the investigation. In November 2013 Murray & Roberts announced it was pursuing former executives implicated in the conduct.

### 2. The 2016/2017 Voluntary Rebuild settlement — a developmental settlement with government

Separately, **seven construction companies — Aveng, Basil Read, Group Five, Murray & Roberts, Raubex, Stefanutti Stocks and WBHO — agreed a settlement with the South African government worth R1.5 billion over 12 years**, implemented through the **Tirisano Construction Fund**, a trust established on **28 August 2017**. The stated purpose was "to transform, strengthen and rebuild the construction industry, and to support the government in its efforts to address poverty, inequality and unemployment." The fund is governed by a board of trustees drawn from government, the construction industry and SAFCEC, with the **Industrial Development Corporation appointed by National Treasury as trust administrator**. It runs seven programmes covering enterprise development, social infrastructure, engineering education, construction skills training and technical capacity building.

> ⚠️ The R1.46 bn (2013) is a **competition penalty**; the R1.5 bn / 12 years (2016–17) is a **negotiated developmental contribution**. They are not the same money and not the same legal instrument. `tirisano` means "working together" in Setswana.

## The post-2010 decline — what actually happened

Five compounding causes:

1. **The World Cup / Eskom pipeline ended.** Between roughly 2006 and 2011 South African contractors were building five stadiums, the Gautrain, and two of the world's largest coal-fired power stations. Murray & Roberts's order book reached R42 bn and revenue quadrupled to R32 bn over 2000–2010. When that pipeline closed, capacity built for it had nowhere to go.
2. **Public infrastructure spend collapsed and then failed to spend.** Budget allocations were not converted into projects; municipal capital under-spending became endemic.
3. **The Medupi and Kusile disasters.** Both were catastrophically over budget and late (see `06_case-studies-of-major-projects.md`). Contractors carried losses, claims, and reputational damage; Eskom's payment behaviour deteriorated.
4. **Reputational and licence-to-operate damage from the cartel.** The collusion findings destroyed public trust, invited "construction mafia" site disruption, and made large firms politically vulnerable.
5. **Fixed-price risk on offshore expansion.** Firms chased margins abroad — Group Five in Ghana, Aveng in Australia and Asia, Murray & Roberts in the Middle East — and took lump-sum risk in markets they did not control.

## Company by company

### WBHO (Wilson Bayly Holmes-Ovcon Limited) — the survivor
Head office 53 Andries Street, Wynberg, Johannesburg. JSE-listed. Divisions: **Building and Civil Engineering; Roads and Earthworks; Design and Construct; Construction Materials.**

- **Revenue R28.5 bn for FY2025 (year ended 30 June 2025)**, up 4% from R27.5 bn in FY2024.
- South African revenue R19.2 bn (2024: R18.9 bn, +1%); rest of Africa R3.7 bn (2024: R3.2 bn, +15%); UK revenue up 5%.
- Building and civil engineering order book **R13.4 bn (2024: R11.6 bn, +15%)**; roads and earthworks order book **R18.3 bn (2024: R14.6 bn, +26%)**, of which roadwork is 81%.
- Energy comprises 43% of the building and civils order book — a clear indication of where South African construction demand has migrated: renewables, transmission and data centres.
- Flagship current work: Steyn City Centre; Merak 2 & 3 Solar (2 × 100 MW); Karusa & Soetwater Wind (2 × 147 MW); Biomedical Research Institution; Pick n Pay Inland Distribution Centre; JNB11 data centre.
- WBHO's Australian subsidiary **Probuild** was placed into administration in 2022 after WBHO withdrew support — the group's single largest strategic reversal, and the reason it exited Australia.

**What WBHO is known for operationally:** conservative bidding, strong cash management, a genuinely diversified portfolio across building, roads and materials, and a willingness to walk away from bad work. It is the benchmark against which other South African contractors are judged.

### Raubex Group — the road builder that diversified
Founded **1974** by Koos Raubenheimer with a **R27 000 loan from his mother's pension savings and 45 employees**; first contract a bridge over the Vet River. Listed on the JSE in March 2007. Level 1 B-BBEE. Over **9 000 employees**. Operations throughout southern Africa and in Western Australia.

Five divisions: **Materials Handling and Mining; Construction Materials; Roads and Earthworks; Infrastructure; Australia.**

FY2026 results (year ended **28 February 2026**):
- **Revenue R22.05 bn**, up 4.6% (FY2025: R21.08 bn)
- Operating profit **R1.74 bn**, up 11.6%; group operating margin **7.9%** (2025: 7.4%)
- Profit before tax R1.64 bn; HEPS 611.3 cents
- **Order book R31.46 bn**, up 11.6% (2025: R28.18 bn)
- Roads and Earthworks revenue R7.13 bn; Construction Materials R3.50 bn; Materials Handling and Mining R4.13 bn; Infrastructure R4.35 bn (up 30.2%)
- Net asset value R7.87 bn; cash and equivalents R1.87 bn

**What Raubex is known for operationally:** vertical integration — it owns the quarries and asphalt plants that feed its road contracts, which is why it earns a 7.9% margin in an industry where 3% is normal. Accreditations include ISO 9001/14001/45001/17025, ASPASA, SARMA, SABITA and SANS 4001-BT3/BT4.

### Stefanutti Stocks — recovering
JSE-listed multidisciplinary group, **cidb Grade 9** (no limit on tender value), **Level 1 B-BBEE contributor**, ISO 9001:2015, 14001:2015, 45001:2018 and 27001:2022 certified — the first South African construction company to achieve ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (2023).

Disciplines: building (including one-stop data-centre delivery covering CSA and MEP), civils and marine, geotechnical (piling, lateral support, large-diameter oscillator piles), roads and earthworks, renewable energy balance-of-plant (civil and electrical), electrical and instrumentation, mechanical (water clarification, patented High-Rate Clarifier), oil and gas with in-house pipe-spool fabrication, mining services (materials handling, tailings management), and concrete rehabilitation.

Footprint: all South African provinces plus **Botswana, Eswatini, Namibia, Zambia and Zimbabwe**. In 2025 the Kusile Civil Works Joint Venture — a 50/50 JV between Stefanutti Stocks and WBHO — was awarded Eskom's Kusile Contractor of the Year Award.

Stefanutti Stocks went through a protracted restructuring and disposal programme following heavy losses in the late 2010s; it survived without entering business rescue.

### Murray & Roberts — from flagship to business rescue
Founded **1902**; head office Bedfordview, Ekurhuleni. Once the country's largest contractor. Over 2000–2010 its order book reached **R42 bn** and revenue quadrupled to **R32 bn**, driven by World Cup and Eskom infrastructure.

After exiting general contracting, the group repositioned around three "platforms" — Underground Mining, Energy Resources & Infrastructure, and Power Industrial & Water — with revenue of **R13.45 bn (FY2024)**. In **December 2024 the group announced a loss of R1.38 bn**. A **business rescue plan was adopted by an overwhelming majority of creditors on 8 April 2025**. Assets marked for sale included Cementation, Cementation Canada and Terra Nova Technologies. Differential Capital was announced as a new owner in the same period. Wikipedia records the company as defunct in 2025.

Murray & Roberts's construction legacy remains visible: Gautrain's main civil contract, Cape Town Stadium, the De Beers Venetia underground mine, and much of Johannesburg's skyline.

### Aveng — dismantled and rebuilt
JSE-listed. Aveng's restructuring strategy was to dispose of **Grinaker-LTA** (its South African construction business) and **Trident Steel** and the manufacturing businesses, retaining **McConnell Dowell** (Australia/NZ/Southeast Asia infrastructure) and **Moolmans** (contract mining) as its two core businesses. Trident Steel was sold for **R700 million**. Aveng completed a debt restructure and rights issue in **March 2021**.

The practical consequence: Aveng is no longer a South African contractor in any meaningful sense. It is an Australasian infrastructure and African contract-mining business with a JSE listing.

### Group Five — collapsed
A JSE-listed diversified construction, engineering and infrastructure concessions group, and one of the seven Tirisano settlement parties. Group Five entered **business rescue in 2019** following severe losses, principally on offshore engineering and power work, and was progressively broken up and sold in pieces. It no longer exists as a listed group.

### Basil Read — collapsed
A JSE-listed contractor with more than six decades of history in South African infrastructure — described on its own website as having built "homes to clinics and hospitals, schools, roads and highway systems". After a 2014 operational review aimed at restoring profitability, the group continued to deteriorate and entered **business rescue in 2018**. Its assets were disposed of and it ceased to operate as a listed group. It was one of the seven Tirisano settlement parties.

### Concor — the reconstituted independent
Registered in Johannesburg on **28 April 1948** by engineer Fernando Piccinini with M. Barnabo, B. Chiozzi, U. Mantelli and V. Cini, originally "Construction Corporation". First major project: the Rand Sports Stadium, Johannesburg; then the Pretoria and Johannesburg power stations, and the **Storms River Bridge** (built 1953–1956, designed by Riccardo Morandi of Rome, for many years South Africa's highest and longest single-span bridge). Last reported revenue as a listed company: **ZAR 1.6 billion (2005)**. Concor was acquired by Murray & Roberts and merged into Murray & Roberts Construction.

In **September 2016** a black-owned consortium led by the Southern Palace Group acquired Murray & Roberts's Infrastructure and Building businesses, and the business was **renamed Concor in May 2017**. It operates in South Africa, Lesotho, Botswana, Namibia, Mozambique and the UAE, with more than 3 400 employees. Recent work has included the De Aar Wind Farm, the R72 highway between Port Alfred and the Fish River, Century City Urban Square, and Embassy Towers in Sandton.

### The unlisted building contractors
- **Motheo Construction Group** — one of South Africa's largest black woman-owned building contractors, historically dominant in subsidised and social housing, education and health buildings.
- **Tiber Construction** — Johannesburg-based commercial and industrial building contractor with a long track record in Gauteng office and retail development.
- **GVK-Siya Zama** — building contractor operating nationally with a strong presence in the Eastern Cape, Western Cape and KwaZulu-Natal.
- **Liviero Group** — building, civils and mining services contractor originating in KwaZulu-Natal.
- **Trencon Construction** — Gauteng building contractor known for high-end commercial and institutional work.

These firms occupy the space that the collapsed listed groups vacated: cidb Grade 8–9 building work in the R100 m–R1 bn range, without the offshore exposure that killed the listed players.

## What remains — the honest summary

- **Two healthy listed contractors**: WBHO (R28.5 bn, FY2025) and Raubex (R22.05 bn, FY2026, R31.46 bn order book).
- **One listed recovering contractor**: Stefanutti Stocks.
- **One repurposed group**: Aveng, now Australasian.
- **Three gone**: Basil Read, Group Five, Murray & Roberts (as a construction group).
- **A viable unlisted middle tier**: Concor, Motheo, Tiber, GVK-Siya Zama, Liviero, Trencon.
- **The demand mix has shifted decisively** from public infrastructure to **energy** — renewables balance-of-plant, transmission, and hyperscale data centres. WBHO's order book is 43% energy; Raubex has a dedicated Infrastructure Division focused on renewables. Any analysis of the South African industry that still assumes public roads and buildings are the main market is a decade out of date.
- **Site-level extortion ("construction mafia")** — organised disruption of sites by groups demanding a share of contract value — has become a material cost and risk on South African projects and is a factor in contractor pricing and site security.
