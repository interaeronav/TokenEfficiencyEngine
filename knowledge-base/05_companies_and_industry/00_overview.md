# How the construction industry is structured — global, southern African and Namibian

**Summary.** Construction is not a manufacturing industry that happens outdoors. It is a **temporary-coalition industry**: for every project a new organisation is assembled from firms that have never worked together, it builds one prototype, and then it dissolves. Everything strange about the industry — the thin margins, the adversarial contracts, the litigation, the boom-bust cycles, the poor productivity record — follows from that fact. The canonical value chain runs **client → funder → consultants → main contractor → subcontractors → suppliers → labour**, with risk pushed steadily downhill and cash pulled steadily uphill. Understanding who sits where in that chain, and which delivery model has been chosen, tells you almost everything about how a given project will behave.

## Key facts

| Layer | Who they are | What they sell | How they get paid | Typical net margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Client / employer | Government, parastatal, developer, mine, private owner | The requirement and the money | n/a — pays | n/a |
| Funder | Commercial bank, DFI (AfDB, DBSA, DBN, KfW), export credit agency, bondholders | Capital, and with it conditions | Interest, fees | n/a |
| Principal agent / lead consultant | Architect (buildings) or engineer (civils) | Design authority + contract administration | % of works value, or time-based | 10–25% on fee income |
| Other consultants | Quantity surveyor, structural/civil/wet-services/electrical engineer, fire, acoustics, town planner, H&S agent | Specialist design and cost control | Fee scale or lump sum | 10–25% |
| Main contractor | The firm that signs the building contract | Assembly, coordination, programme, risk | Monthly payment certificates less retention | **1–5%** typical; 5–8% is a good year |
| Subcontractors | Electrical, plumbing, HVAC, roofing, glazing, ceilings, tiling, joinery, earthworks, piling | Trade packages | Monthly, via the main contractor | 3–10% |
| Suppliers / merchants | Cement, brick, steel, timber, board, hardware | Materials, sometimes on 30-day terms | Invoice, often COD for small builders | 5–15% |
| Labour | Own employees, labour brokers, labour-only subcontractors | Hours | Weekly / fortnightly | n/a |

> ⚠️ The main contractor is usually the **lowest-margin, highest-risk** party in the entire chain. A 3% net margin means a single 5%-of-value dispute wipes out the profit on two projects. This is why contractors care so much about variation orders, extensions of time, and getting paid on time — and why an owner who squeezes the contractor's margin to zero is buying a claim, not a discount.

## The value chain, step by step

### 1. The client and the brief
Everything starts with a client who has a need and access to money. Clients fall into recognisably different species, and each behaves differently:

- **Public clients** — ministries, municipalities, roads authorities, water utilities, school and health departments. They are constrained by procurement law (**[NA]** the Public Procurement Act 15 of 2015; **[ZA]** the Public Finance Management Act, Municipal Finance Management Act and the cidb prescripts). They are slow, transparent on paper, price-driven, and often poor at paying on time.
- **Parastatals / SOEs** — NamPower, NamWater, Namport, TransNamib, Roads Authority **[NA]**; Eskom, Transnet, SANRAL **[ZA]**. Larger, more technical, and the source of most megaproject failure in the region.
- **Private developers** — retail, residential, industrial, logistics. Fast, commercially sharp, driven by yield and letting dates. They pay well if you hit programme.
- **Mines and industry** — mine owners are among the most sophisticated construction clients in southern Africa. They pay properly and expect shutdown-grade planning.
- **Private individuals** — houses, alterations, boundary walls. The smallest clients but the largest number of transactions, and the least protected.

### 2. The funder shapes the project more than people admit
Whoever provides the money writes rules into the project. A KfW-funded Namibian road must follow KfW tender rules; an AfDB-funded port expansion follows AfDB procurement; a World Bank project uses the FIDIC MDB Harmonised Construction Contract. These rules routinely override national preference policy and are one of the most common reasons a local contractor is shut out of a "local" project. **[NA]** In 2023 the Roads Authority publicly explained that prequalification for the Karibib–Usakos road rehabilitation was governed by KfW tender rules under an NDP5 loan agreement signed 13 December 2022, not by Namibian preference rules alone.

### 3. The consultant team
In building work the **architect is conventionally the principal agent**: the client contracts with the architect, and the architect appoints and coordinates the rest of the consultant team and administers the building contract. The CIF's own public guidance to Namibian homeowners describes exactly this route — engage an architect, who as principal agent contracts the quantity surveyor and subsequently the general contractor.

Core roles:
- **Architect** — design, statutory approvals, aesthetic and functional coordination, contract administration, issuing of payment certificates and practical completion.
- **Quantity surveyor (QS)** — bills of quantities, cost planning, valuation of work done, valuation of variations, final account. In JBCC and GCC projects the QS is the price referee.
- **Structural / civil engineer** — the load-bearing skeleton, foundations, geotechnical interpretation, and (in civils) usually the principal agent role instead of the architect.
- **Wet services, electrical, mechanical, fire, acoustic, traffic, environmental** — as project scale requires.
- **Health and safety agent** — statutory in South Africa under the Construction Regulations; strongly advisable in Namibia.

In civil engineering the equivalent figure is **"the Engineer"** (FIDIC Red Book, GCC 2015) or **"the Project Manager"** and **"the Supervisor"** (NEC). The Engineer is nominally the client's agent but is contractually obliged to act fairly when certifying — a genuine dual role that generates a great deal of dispute.

### 4. The main contractor
The main contractor takes the design and turns it into a building for a price and by a date. Critically, the main contractor typically **self-performs only 20–40% of the work** on a building project — concrete, brickwork, and site management — and buys the rest as trade packages. On a large fit-out or data-centre job self-performance can approach zero: the main contractor is effectively a management and risk-bearing entity.

What the main contractor is actually selling is:
- **Coordination** — sequencing thirty trades through the same space without collision.
- **Programme risk** — the promise of a completion date, backed by penalties.
- **Financial intermediation** — paying subcontractors and suppliers before the client's certificate clears.
- **Single-point accountability** — one throat to choke.

### 5. Subcontractors — three legal species
The cidb distinguishes three types, and the distinction matters because it determines who carries the risk of the subcontractor's failure:

- **Domestic subcontractor** — chosen freely by the main contractor. The main contractor carries all the risk.
- **Nominated subcontractor** — nominated by the employer; the main contractor is obliged to appoint them. Risk is shared, and JBCC has an elaborate mechanism for this.
- **Selected subcontractor** — selected by the contractor in consultation with the employer.

> ⚠️ Nomination is the single most common source of "it isn't my fault" disputes in southern African building contracts. If the employer nominates a subcontractor who goes insolvent, the employer generally cannot simply hold the main contractor liable for the resulting delay.

### 6. Suppliers and merchants
Below the subcontractors sit the material suppliers and builders' merchants. For a small builder, the merchant is also the **de facto lender**: a 30-day account is working capital. The CIF explicitly warns Namibian homeowners about this: obtain a letter from the contractor's material supplier, because if the contractor does not pay the supplier — even after the homeowner has paid the contractor — the supplier may have rights against the property.

## Delivery models — the six that matter

| Model | Who designs | Who builds | Price basis | Client risk | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Traditional / design-bid-build** | Client's consultants | Contractor selected on price | Bills of quantities, remeasured or lump sum | High design risk, low price risk | Design is complete and stable; public tender required |
| **Design and build (D&B) / turnkey** | Contractor | Contractor | Lump sum against Employer's Requirements | Low, if the brief is watertight | Client wants price certainty and can write a performance spec |
| **EPC / turnkey** | Contractor | Contractor | Fixed price, minimal client involvement | Lowest — but you pay a premium | Process plants, power plants, project-financed work |
| **Construction management** | Client's consultants | Many trade contractors direct to client | Cost of trade packages + CM fee | Highest — client holds every trade contract | Fast-track, complex, sophisticated client |
| **Management contracting** | Client's consultants | Management contractor holds trade packages | Prime cost + fee | High | Fast-track with a buffer between client and trades |
| **Alliance / IPD / target cost** | Everyone jointly | Everyone jointly | Target cost with pain/gain share | Shared | Uncertain scope, long programme, capable parties |

NEC states the underlying principle bluntly: risks should be allocated to the party best placed to manage them; for straightforward work a fixed price is correct, for complex or large work a target cost with pain/gain share is correct, and where the client is best placed to carry risk a cost-reimbursable or management contract is correct. Most disasters in southern African infrastructure are the result of picking a fixed-price model for a project whose scope was not yet knowable.

## What "the industry" looks like at the two ends of the scale

**At the top.** A handful of very large firms — Chinese state groups, VINCI, ACS/Hochtief, Bouygues — with revenues in the tens or hundreds of billions of US dollars, operating internationally, competing on balance sheet and financing as much as on construction skill. See `01_global-major-contractors.md`.

**At the bottom.** The overwhelming majority of construction firms everywhere are small. The CIF describes its own membership as including multinational building and civil contractors "though the bulk consists of smaller contractors and SMEs". **[ZA]** In South Africa the cidb Register of Contractors grades contractors 1 to 9; a Grade 1 contractor may only be awarded public contracts up to R500 000, while a Grade 9 has no limit. The pyramid is extremely bottom-heavy.

**The gap in between is the real problem in Namibia and South Africa.** There are very small builders and there are very large international firms, and comparatively few mid-sized locally owned contractors with the balance sheet, bonding capacity and track record to win a N$200 million or R500 million contract. Prequalification criteria written for international bidders therefore exclude local firms almost automatically — a recurring and politically live complaint in Namibia.

## How money actually flows

1. Contractor does work during month 1.
2. Contractor submits a **payment claim / application** at month-end.
3. QS or Engineer values it; principal agent issues a **payment certificate** (JBCC: within a set period after the valuation date).
4. Client pays the certified amount, less **retention** (commonly 5–10%, halved at practical completion, released at end of defects liability).
5. Contractor pays subcontractors and suppliers — often only after being paid, which is why "pay-when-paid" clauses are so contested.

The contractor is therefore financing the project for 30–75 days on a 2–4% margin. **This is the mechanism by which a technically competent contractor goes insolvent.** Almost every southern African contractor failure of the last fifteen years — Basil Read, Group Five, Murray & Roberts' construction businesses, Esor — has cash flow and claim exposure as the proximate cause, not bad building.

## Cyclicality, and why the region's industry shrank

Construction is the most cyclical major sector in almost every economy, because it is the sector that absorbs the swing in capital investment. **[ZA]** South Africa's industry peaked around the 2010 FIFA World Cup infrastructure programme and has contracted structurally ever since. **[NA]** Namibia's construction sector accounted for roughly **1.4% of GDP in 2024** and was projected by Simonis Storm Securities to be the fastest-growing sector in 2025 at about **8.5% growth**, with total output near **N$4.3 billion** — a large percentage on a very small base. That number is the single most useful corrective to the assumption that Namibian construction is a big industry: it is not. It is small, project-lumpy, and dominated by public capital spending.

> ⚠️ **[NA]** There is **no legal requirement in Namibia for contractors to be licensed**. The CIF states this plainly on its own website and notes it may change with the establishment of the anticipated National Planning and Construction Council. Practically, this means a Namibian client's only real protection is contractual and reputational: references, insurance certificates, a written contract, and (the CIF's own recommendation) using a CIF-registered member so that a complaint mechanism exists at all.

## Open questions

- The status of Namibia's proposed National Planning and Construction Council — whether it has been enacted, and whether contractor licensing will follow — is not confirmed from a primary legislative source here.
- Current employment figures for the Namibian construction sector are unavailable; the CIF has publicly complained that Labour Force Survey data from the 2023 Population and Housing Census has not been released.
- Precise average net margins by tier are indicative industry rules of thumb, not figures drawn from a published statistical series.
