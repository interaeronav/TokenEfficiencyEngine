# Ten major project case studies — what went right, what went wrong, and the transferable lesson

**Summary.** Ten projects, chosen for what they teach rather than for fame: three African infrastructure projects (Medupi, Kusile, Walvis Bay New Container Terminal), one African rail PPP (Gautrain), one African dam (GERD), one African commercial building (Kigali Convention Centre), and four international benchmarks (Burj Khalifa, Crossrail, Sydney Opera House, the Big Dig), plus Neom as a live cautionary tale. The pattern that emerges is monotonous: **projects fail on the definition of scope and the maturity of design at the moment a price is fixed, and on the capacity of the owner's organisation — almost never on construction technique.**

> ⚠️ All costs are stated with currency and, where known, the year of the estimate. Comparing a 1957 estimate with a 1973 outturn without adjusting for inflation is meaningless — the Sydney Opera House entry below is explicit about this.

## 1. Burj Khalifa, Dubai — the triumph

**Client:** Emaar Properties. **Architect:** Skidmore, Owings & Merrill; design architect Adrian Smith. **Contractors:** a joint venture of **Samsung C&T** (South Korea), **BESIX** (Belgium) and **Arabtec** (UAE). **Value:** US$1.5 billion / AED 5.5 billion. **Height:** 828 m architectural, 829.8 m to tip.

**Programme:** construction began **12 January 2004**; exterior structure completed **1 October 2009**; officially opened **4 January 2010**; the last major planned facility became operational **23 January 2011**, at which point the tower was fully functional.

**What went right.** A tower nearly twice the height of the previous record holder was delivered in **under six years from start to opening**, on a defined budget, by a joint venture that combined Korean high-rise engineering, Belgian marine and civil expertise and Emirati local delivery capacity. The **buttressed core** structural system, developed by SOM, made the height economically achievable. Construction techniques — self-climbing formwork, high-strength concrete pumped to record heights, GPS-based surveying to control lean — were pushed hard but were extensions of known methods, not inventions.

**What went wrong.** Much of the building was still unfinished internally at opening — a common consequence of driving to a fixed opening date. The project also drew sustained criticism over migrant labour conditions.

> **Transferable lesson:** height and technical ambition are not what kill projects. A **clear single client, a complete design before construction, and a joint venture assembled for complementary capability rather than lowest price** will deliver an extraordinarily difficult building on time.

## 2. Gautrain, Gauteng — the qualified success

**Client:** Gauteng Provincial Government, as a Public-Private Partnership under the Public Finance Management Act. **Concessionaire:** Bombela Concession Company (Pty) Ltd, holding a **19½-year concession** for design, construction, financing, operation and maintenance; shareholders included **Bombardier Transportation, Bouygues, Murray & Roberts, the Strategic Partners Group (a broad-based BEE consortium), RATP, the J&J Group and Absa Bank**. **Main civil contractor:** Murray & Roberts.

**Programme:** environmental authorisation 25 April 2004; government go-ahead 7 December 2005; commercial close February 2006; **20-year PPP contract and concession agreement signed 28 September 2006**, when construction started. Built in two phases: the first (45 months) OR Tambo–Sandton, opened **8 June 2010, days before the FIFA World Cup**; the second (54 months) completing the network, **full service from 7 June 2012**.

**Cost estimate timeline — the important part:**

| Year | Estimate |
|---|---|
| 2002 | R3.5 billion |
| 2003 | R7 billion (for the EIA process) |
| 2005 | R20 billion (US$3.7 bn), on award |
| 2006 | **R25.2 billion, approved** |
| 2010 | R26 billion |
| 2011 | R25.4 billion (US$3.66 bn) |

**What went right.** The airport link opened on time for the World Cup — an absolutely hard deadline. The Gautrain works: it carries passengers reliably, it triggered substantial property development along its corridor, and it demonstrated that a large PPP could be financed and delivered in South Africa. The client's own position, stated by Gautrain Management Agency CEO Jack van der Merwe, was that the concession was **fixed-price, fixed-scope and fixed-period**, escalating only with CPI above the Reserve Bank's prediction, on provincial breach, or on scope change.

**What went wrong.** The cost grew **seven-fold** between the figure announced to the public in 2002 and the figure contracted in 2006. Critics — including Jeremy Cronin, then chair of the National Assembly's transport portfolio committee — alleged in March 2008 that the cost had "quietly crept up to R35 billion" below the radar. The project was never debated in the Gauteng Provincial Legislature or subjected to significant public debate before being approved and tendered.

> **Transferable lesson:** a project can be delivered competently and still constitute a public accountability failure. **The number the public is told at announcement is the number they will hold you to.** Announcing a figure before the design and scope exist guarantees a "cost overrun" narrative even where the contracted price is later honoured.

## 3. Medupi Power Station, Limpopo — the failure

**Client:** Eskom. **Location:** Lephalale. **Key contractors:** Alstom (turbines), Hitachi (supercritical boilers). **Capacity:** six units of 794 MW = **4,584 MW**; the largest dry-cooled coal-fired power station in the world.

**Programme and cost:** originally conceived in 2007 as **three units totalling 2,292 MW at an estimated R32 billion**. As built, the project was initially expected to cost **R69 billion**, updated to approximately **R80 billion by 2007 (2007 rands)**, and revised to **R154 billion (2013 rands)**. Completion of the first two units was expected by 2012; the first unit (unit 6) was commissioned and handed over on **23 August 2015**, with units 5 to 1 following at roughly nine-month intervals. In 2021 an explosion at unit 4 caused damage with repair costs reported at around **R2 billion**.

**What went wrong.**
- **The scope changed fundamentally after the budget was set** — three units at 2,292 MW became six units at 4,584 MW.
- **Technical defects at scale:** steam piping pressure problems, weld re-checks on the boilers, ash system blockages.
- **Design defects shared with Kusile.** In February 2019 Eskom's GM for group technology reported that **R8 billion** would be needed to fix design defects at Medupi and Kusile.
- **Labour disputes and site instability** compounded the delays.
- **Governance failure**, culminating in a presidentially announced forensic probe into the delays and cost overruns.

> **Transferable lesson:** **never allow the scope to double after the budget is public.** And do not commit to first-of-a-kind supercritical boiler technology, on a greenfield site, in a country with no recent large-plant construction experience, on a fixed schedule, with a client organisation that had not built a power station in two decades.

## 4. Kusile Power Station, Mpumalanga — the same failure, again

**Client:** Eskom. **Location:** Witbank, Emalahleni Local Municipality. **Key contracts:** **Hitachi Power Africa** awarded the boiler contract worth **R18.5 billion** in February 2008; **Alstom S&E** awarded the turbine island works valued at **R13 billion** in February 2008. Construction began **2008**; commission date **10 March 2017**. **Construction cost R233 billion (US$15.76 billion)** against an estimate of **R118 billion**. The World Bank approved a **US$805 million (R5.78 billion)** loan.

**What went wrong.**
- **Boiler design:** temperatures the spray water-cooling system could not cope with.
- **Flue gas desulphurisation (FGD)** integration failures: the FGD was not designed for the flue gas volume required when the bag-house filter is operated with PAN filter material, and the same defect was expected to recur on units 4, 5 and 6 sharing the same chimney.
- **Corruption.** Hitachi Power Africa's contracts became the subject of a US Securities and Exchange Commission action over payments to an ANC-linked entity; **Hitachi agreed to pay US$19 million to settle the charges**. Hitachi Power Africa was rebranded Mitsubishi Hitachi Power Systems Africa in February 2014.
- Contracts worth a combined **R10 billion** featured in a scandal with an estimated R75 million lost through irregular activity.
- At one point Kusile was **five years past its original completion date and an estimated R80 billion (US$5.4 billion) over budget.**

**What went right — a genuinely important detail.** The civil works were built competently by South African contractors: in 2025 the **Kusile Civil Works Joint Venture (Stefanutti Stocks and WBHO, 50/50)** was awarded Eskom's **Kusile Contractor of the Year Award**.

> **Transferable lesson:** **Kusile's failures were procurement, engineering-integration and integrity failures, not construction failures.** The concrete was fine. Running two identical megaprojects concurrently, in the same country, with the same unproven technology, the same suppliers and the same thin client organisation, doubled the exposure to a single mode of failure instead of diversifying it.

## 5. Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam — delivered late, strategically decisive

**Client:** Ethiopian Electric Power. **Contractor:** **Salini Impregilo (now Webuild)** of Italy. **Value:** a **US$4.8 billion contract awarded on 31 March 2011 — one day after the project was made public — without competitive bidding**. Total construction cost reported as more than **US$5 billion**.

**Programme:** foundation stone laid **2 April 2011** by the Prime Minister. The expectation was that the first two turbines would be operational **after 44 months of construction, i.e. early 2015**. In reality the dam produced electricity for the first time on **20 February 2022** (375 MW), a second 375 MW turbine followed in August 2022, and the third and fourth (400 MW each) in August 2024. Reservoir filling ran in five phases from **July 2020 to October 2024**. The plant was **formally inaugurated on 9 September 2025**, with **5,150 MW** installed across 13 turbines (2 × 375 MW and 11 × 400 MW).

**What went wrong.** Roughly **ten years later than the initial turbine target**. The direct award without competitive bidding removed price tension at the outset. Financing was largely domestic — government bonds and public subscription — which constrained cash flow. Electromechanical works were reportedly re-let after problems with the original supplier. The project also generated a decade-long international dispute with Egypt and Sudan over Nile water rights.

**What went right.** It was built, in a country with limited heavy-civil capacity, largely with domestic finance, and it fundamentally changed Ethiopia's energy position, with long-term power export agreements including up to 400 MW to Kenya.

> **Transferable lesson:** **a directly awarded, domestically financed megaproject can be completed — but expect the schedule to roughly double and accept that you have given up commercial leverage on day one.** For a state that regards a project as strategically existential, that may be a rational trade; for a commercial client it is not.

## 6. Kigali Convention Centre, Rwanda — the expensive landmark

**Owner-developer:** **Ultimate Concept Limited**, a Rwandan company formed in 2007 by three Rwandan corporate investors pooling resources. **Architect:** Roland Dieterle (Germany). **Operator:** Radisson Blu. **Value:** **US$300 million**. **Programme:** construction began **2009**, completed **2016** — seven years.

**Components:** a 292-room five-star Radisson Blu hotel over six floors; a conference centre seating **2,600**; the Kigali Information Technology Park with **32,200 m²** of rentable office and retail space; and a museum.

**What went right.** It works as intended, and as national infrastructure it has repaid itself in soft power: it hosted the **African Union Summit (July 2016)**, the **World Economic Forum for Africa 2016**, the **2022 Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting**, multiple Transform Africa Summits and the International Conference on Learning Representations in 2023. Kigali's positioning as an African conference destination is substantially the product of this one building.

**What went wrong.** Seven years for a single building complex is a very long programme, and US$300 million is a very large capital cost for a country of Rwanda's GDP. The project changed contractors and had well-documented funding interruptions during construction.

> **Transferable lesson:** for a small economy, **an iconic building can be a legitimate strategic investment rather than a vanity** — but only if the operating model (a hotel operator, a conference pipeline, a lettable IT park) is designed in from the start. The Kigali Convention Centre works because it is three revenue-generating businesses in one envelope, not because it is beautiful.

## 7. Walvis Bay New Container Terminal, Namibia — the local benchmark

**Client:** Namibian Ports Authority (Namport). **Contractor:** **China Harbour Engineering Company (CHEC)**, a subsidiary of CCCC. **Financing:** the **African Development Bank approved a US$200 million loan in 2013** for the container terminal expansion on 40 ha of reclaimed land, including four ship-to-shore cranes; AfDB was described by Namport board member Johannes Kangandjera as "the majority financier".

**Programme:** construction commenced **mid-2014**; the terminal was **commissioned on 24 August 2019**. President Hage Geingob officially launched the refurbished harbour on **2 August 2019** together with the Namport board chairperson and an AfDB representative. (Namport's own later release describes the terminal as "commissioned in November 2019 by the late President Dr Hage Geingob" — the dates in Namport's own publications are inconsistent and should be treated with care.)

**Scope delivered:** **40 hectares of new land reclaimed** from the bay, created by dredging the port and using the dredged sand to form the land, linked to the existing port by a causeway. The terminal comprises quay walls, paved areas, buildings, roads, railway lines, ship-to-shore quay cranes and rubber-tyred gantry cranes. It adds **600 m of quay wall to the existing 1,800 m** and creates berths 9, 10 and 11 (berth 9 being the new passenger liner berth). Capacity **at least 750,000 TEU per annum**; AfDB stated the expansion would raise handling capacity from **355,000 to 1,005,000 TEU**. Freeing the old container terminal allowed it to become a multi-purpose bulk and break-bulk facility, and the additional quay length permits rehabilitation of the existing quay walls with minimal disruption.

**What happened next.** Namport concessioned the container handling operations to **Terminal Investment Limited (TiL, Switzerland)** via its Namibian subsidiary **Terminal Investment Namibia (TiN)** for **25 years, effective 1 October 2024**, to attract private capital for channel widening and deepening, new equipment and systems. Standard Bank Namibia and RMB Namibia subsequently structured funding for a **N$2.1 billion** TiN modernisation programme.

**The uncomfortable part.** In the **2024 World Bank / S&P Global Container Port Performance Index, Walvis Bay ranked 376th of 405 ports worldwide.** Namport attributed the result to the Red Sea crisis and regional disruption. CHEC's own representative described Walvis Bay as one of the most challenging of the more than 100 projects the company has undertaken across 90 countries.

> **Transferable lesson:** **building the asset is the easy half.** Namibia now has a terminal with three times the capacity it can fill, financed by an AfDB loan that must be serviced, and it has had to concession operations to a global terminal operator to make the asset perform. Demand forecasting and the operating model deserve at least as much scrutiny as the construction contract — and a small country building ahead of demand is making a long bet, not a safe one.

## 8. Crossrail / the Elizabeth line, London — late, over budget, and worth it

**Client:** Transport for London. **Value:** initial budget **£14.8 billion**, revised to **£18.9 billion by May 2022**. **Programme:** approved **2007**; construction began **2009** on the central section and connections; originally planned to open in **2018**; repeatedly delayed, including several months attributable to the COVID-19 pandemic. Queen Elizabeth II opened the line on **17 May 2022**; passenger services on the core section began **24 May 2022** with 12 trains per hour in each direction. Revenues were forecast in 2018 at nearly £500 million in 2022–23 and over £1 billion from 2024 to 2025.

**What went right.** Enormously complex tunnelling under central London — twin bores through Victorian sewers, existing Tube tunnels, building foundations and the Thames — was executed **without the tunnelling itself becoming the critical path**. Crossrail established a **Tunnelling and Underground Construction Academy** in 2011 to build the skills base it needed, which is a genuinely exemplary piece of long-range planning. Framework agreements let in April 2009 across 17 firms distributed risk sensibly. The finished railway transformed east-west capacity in London.

**What went wrong.** The **systems integration and signalling** — three different signalling systems having to hand over seamlessly at speed — proved far harder than the civil works, and this was where the years were lost. Announcement of the 2018 opening date was maintained internally long after it was achievable.

> **Transferable lesson:** on a modern infrastructure project, **the risk has migrated from civils to systems integration and commissioning.** Budget schedule float at the *end* of the programme, not the beginning, and treat the testing and commissioning phase as a major project in its own right with its own manager, budget and critical path.

## 9. Sydney Opera House — the masterpiece that broke every rule

**Client:** New South Wales Government. **Architect:** **Jørn Utzon**, whose design was selected from 30 finalists by a jury including Eero Saarinen and announced in Sydney on **29 January 1957**. **Engineer:** Ove Arup and Partners. **Contractors:** Civil & Civic (stage I podium), **MR Hornibrook** (stages II and III — shells and completion, **A$56.5 million** for the completion contract).

**Programme and cost — the numbers that matter:**

| | |
|---|---|
| Original 1957 estimate | **£3,500,000 (A$7 million)** |
| Original completion date | **26 January 1963** (Australia Day) |
| Actual opening | **20 October 1973**, by Queen Elizabeth II |
| Final cost | **A$102 million** (≈A$1,082 million in 2022 terms) |
| Outcome | **Ten years late and 1,357% over budget in real terms** |

**What went wrong, precisely.**
- **Construction began before the design existed.** Stage I started **2 March 1959**, on political rather than technical grounds. Work on the podium was not completed until February 1963, delayed by weather, unexpected difficulty diverting stormwater, **construction beginning before proper construction drawings had been prepared**, and changes to the original contract documents. The consequence: **the podium columns were not strong enough to support the roof structure and had to be rebuilt.**
- **The roof geometry was unsolved when construction started.** Between 1957 and 1963 the design team went through **at least twelve iterations** of the shell form — parabolas, circular ribs and others — before arriving at the spherical-segment solution that made the shells buildable. Hornibrook manufactured **2,400 precast ribs and 4,000 roof panels** in an on-site factory and developed an adjustable steel-trussed "erection arch" to build them.
- **The brief was acoustically impossible.** After Utzon's resignation, acoustic advisor Lothar Cremer confirmed that Utzon's original design allowed only **2,000 seats** in the main hall, and that increasing this to the **3,000 specified in the brief would be disastrous for the acoustics.**
- **Client relations collapsed.** A change of government in 1965 placed the project under the Ministry of Public Works. By February 1966 Utzon was owed more than $100,000 in fees; he **left the project on 28 February 1966** and left Australia, never to return. Even at October 1966 the cost incurred was only **A$22.9 million** — under a quarter of the final figure. The interiors were completed by the Hall, Todd and Littlemore partnership.

**What went right.** It is arguably the most recognisable building of the twentieth century, a UNESCO World Heritage site, and it won Utzon the Pritzker Prize in 2003 with a citation calling it "one of the great iconic buildings of the 20th century".

> **Transferable lesson:** **starting construction before the design is resolved is the single most expensive decision available to a client.** Everything that went wrong at Bennelong Point — rebuilt columns, twelve roof iterations, an acoustically impossible brief, a broken client-architect relationship — flows from a political decision to break ground in 1959 for reasons that had nothing to do with readiness. Also: the building's cultural value eventually dwarfed its cost overrun, which is true of almost nothing else on this list. **Do not use Sydney as precedent for tolerating overruns.**

## 10. The Big Dig (Central Artery/Tunnel Project), Boston — the governance failure

**Client:** Massachusetts Turnpike Authority (MTA). **Management joint venture:** **Bechtel Corporation and Parsons Brinckerhoff**, engaged to provide preliminary designs, manage design consultants and construction contractors, track cost and schedule, advise the MTA on project decisions and in some instances act as the MTA's representative.

**Programme and cost:** planning began **1982**; environmental impact studies from 1983; federal funding appropriated 1987 over a presidential veto; ground broken **1991**. Originally scheduled for completion in **1998 at an estimated US$2.8 billion**. When construction began, the cost including the Charles River crossing was estimated at **US$5.8 billion**. The project was **completed in December 2007 at US$14.6 billion**; total expenses eventually passed **US$15 billion**, and **with interest reached US$21.93 billion**.

**What went wrong.**
- **Owner capability.** The MTA "had little experience in managing an undertaking of the scope and magnitude of the CA/T Project". It combined its own employees with joint-venture employees into an integrated project organisation — intended to improve efficiency, but which **"hindered MTA's ability to independently oversee project activities because MTA and the joint venture had effectively become partners in the project."** This is the central lesson of the Big Dig.
- **Seven years of environmental permitting** before construction, during which inflation greatly increased the original cost estimates.
- **Design controversy consumed the schedule.** The Charles River crossing was disputed throughout design; 27 alternative plans were rejected as too costly; the "Scheme Z" variant was imposed over objections against a looming deadline.
- **Quality failures with fatal consequences.** Leaks, design flaws, and a ceiling collapse that killed a motorist. Bechtel and Parsons Brinckerhoff agreed to pay **US$407 million** in restitution; several smaller companies paid a combined **≈US$51 million**.
- The project manager was **fired in 2000** over cost overruns; his replacement had to accept an US$8.55 billion cap on federal contributions. Surface treatments in the original plan were dropped because of the highway overruns.

**What went right.** The elevated Central Artery was replaced by a tunnel and the surface reclaimed as parkland; Boston's waterfront and North End were reconnected to the city. As an urban outcome it worked.

> **Transferable lesson:** **never let your project manager and your owner's representative be the same organisation.** The Big Dig's defining error was structural, not technical: the client outsourced management to a consortium and then merged its own oversight staff into that consortium, leaving nobody with both the capability and the independence to challenge the numbers.

## 11. Neom, Saudi Arabia — the live cautionary tale

**Client:** the Public Investment Fund of Saudi Arabia; chaired by Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman. **Initial estimated cost: US$1.6 trillion.** By 2025 estimated costs were reported **in excess of US$8.8 trillion — more than 25 times the annual Saudi budget.**

**Status.** In 2024 Neom was reported to have been **substantially scaled back** from its original plan. By 2025 **new contracts for Neom had dried up**, the megaproject had been substantially scaled down or suspended, and **there was no mention of Neom in Saudi Arabia's pre-budget statement for 2026.** The Line — the 170 km linear city — has been criticised from the outset for unrealistic costs and timelines, an impractical shape, environmental harm and human rights violations, including the forced removal of thousands of people and the razing of villages. The Sindalah island resort held an opening party in October 2024 but was reported to have offered only limited or invitation-only access and remained closed to the general public as of 2025.

> **Transferable lesson:** **no amount of capital substitutes for a viable programme.** Neom is the clearest modern demonstration that a project whose scope is set by ambition rather than by engineering feasibility will not converge, regardless of the sponsor's balance sheet. Watch what happens to the contractors: firms that staffed up for Neom and priced work on the assumption of continuity are the ones that will absorb the consequences.

## The pattern across all eleven

| Cause of failure | Projects exhibiting it |
|---|---|
| Price fixed before scope was defined | Gautrain, Medupi, Kusile, Sydney Opera House, Big Dig, Neom |
| Construction started before design was complete | Sydney Opera House, Medupi, Kusile |
| Owner organisation too thin or compromised | Medupi, Kusile, Big Dig |
| First-of-a-kind technology on a fixed schedule | Medupi, Kusile |
| Systems integration underestimated | Crossrail, Kusile |
| Corruption / integrity failure | Kusile, Medupi (probe) |
| Demand or operating model unresolved | Walvis Bay NCT, Neom |
| **Delivered well** | Burj Khalifa, Kigali Convention Centre, Walvis Bay (construction), Gautrain (delivery), Crossrail (civils) |

**The single most repeated lesson on this page:** the moment at which a project's fate is determined is not on site. It is the day someone commits publicly to a number and a date for a scope that does not yet exist.
