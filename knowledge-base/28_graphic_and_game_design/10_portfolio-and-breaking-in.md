# Portfolio and breaking in — the practical career file

**Summary.** A portfolio is not a gallery of your best work; it is an argument that you can do a specific job. This file states what a graphic design portfolio must show, how a game design portfolio differs, how an environment art portfolio differs again, which personal projects actually convert, how game jams and modding function as credentials, how the internship pipeline works, how to network at GDC and online, what the application and interview process looks like in each field, and an honest account of the entry-level market in 2026.

## Key facts

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Graphic design portfolio | **6–10 projects**, process visible, one point of view |
| RCA MA application format | PDF portfolio **max 25 slides**, 300-word statement, **2-minute video** |
| VFS Game Design entry submission | Game design synopsis: concept ≤1 page, story outline ≤1 page, mechanic diagrams and world map 1–2 pages |
| Game design portfolio | **Playable builds**, not documents |
| Environment art portfolio | **2–4 fully realised environments**, in-engine, with wireframes and breakdowns |
| UK junior game roles | 9.4% of postings (2022) → **2.9%** (2023) |
| Game industry layoffs | >10,500 (2023); ~14,600 (2024); ~4,600 + 22 studios closed (H1 2026) |
| US graphic design job growth | **2%** 2024–34, ~20,000 openings/yr, mostly replacement |
| Sheridan Bachelor of Game Design | mandatory **co-op work term** in year 3 |
| Stardew Valley | one person, ~4.5 years, **50 million copies by Feb 2026** |

> ⚠️ In both fields, the single most common cause of rejection is not weak craft — it is a portfolio that does not match the job. A generalist portfolio sent to twenty different roles converts worse than three targeted portfolios sent to three.

## 1. What a graphic design portfolio must show

**Six to ten projects. Not twenty.** The weakest piece sets your perceived level, so the edit is the work.

**Each project needs:**
1. **A one-sentence problem statement.** What was the brief, who was it for, what constraint mattered.
2. **The outcome, shown properly.** Real photography or high-quality renders at a size where typography is legible. Mockups are acceptable; mockups doing the work the design should be doing are not.
3. **Visible process.** Sketches, iterations, rejected directions, the reasoning. Studios hire the thinking, not the artefact.
4. **Range within the piece** — a wordmark alone is not an identity; show the system applied.

**What must appear somewhere across the set:**
- **A typographic piece at length** — a book, a report, a long editorial. This is what separates designers from decorators and it is the first thing a serious studio looks for.
- **An identity system** with a guidelines document. Twenty pages minimum. See `02 §8`.
- **Something digital** — a product, a site, a design system.
- **Something you initiated.** Self-authored work is the only place a point of view is visible.
- **Evidence you can write.** Every caption is a writing sample.

**What kills a graphic design portfolio:**
- Logo grids with no application.
- Every project in a different unrelated style, which reads as no position rather than as range.
- Rebrands of Apple, Nike or Spotify — every reviewer has seen four hundred and they carry no constraint.
- Unspecified imagery — stock photography, AI-generated filler, or images whose provenance you cannot state.
- Dribbble-style single-screen UI shots with no flow, no research and no problem.
- A PDF that is 80 MB, or a site that takes eight seconds to load.

**Format.** A personal site (self-built, Cargo, Semplice, Format) **and** a PDF, because half of studios want each. Keep the PDF under 20 MB. Name it `firstname-lastname-portfolio-2026.pdf`. Applications to schools have their own formats — the RCA, for example, specifies a **maximum 25-slide PDF**, a 300-word statement and a **2-minute video proposal**.

## 2. What a game design portfolio must show

**The rule: playable beats describable.** A design document nobody can play is an assertion. A build they can run in ninety seconds is evidence.

**The core of it:**
1. **Two to four finished games.** Finished means: it has a start, an end, no crashes, a title screen, and someone who is not you can play it without instructions. Small and finished beats ambitious and abandoned, every time.
2. **A downloadable or browser-playable build for each** (itch.io is the standard host), plus a 60–90 second gameplay video for reviewers who will not download.
3. **A written breakdown per project:** what you designed, what the intent was, what you playtested, what the data said, what you changed, and what you would do differently. **Name your specific contribution on team projects.** Vagueness here reads as dishonesty.
4. **Evidence of iteration.** Before-and-after. A level that changed after playtesting, with the reason.

**By sub-discipline:**
- **Level designer:** two to three levels, playable, with blockout images alongside the finished version, an annotated top-down map showing the intended path and sightlines, an intensity/pacing chart, and a written account of the teaching sequence. Mods for *Half-Life 2*, *Portal 2*, *Skyrim*, *Doom* or CS map-making are directly legible.
- **Systems designer:** the spreadsheet. Show the model, the curves, the simulation, the tuning rationale, and the outcome data. A well-balanced board or card game is excellent evidence because the whole system is inspectable.
- **Narrative designer:** a Twine or Ink piece with genuine branching, a quest implemented in a real engine or mod, bark and systemic dialogue samples, and one structural analysis of an existing game.
- **Technical designer:** working systems in-engine with source available — an ability framework, an inventory system, a procedural generator, an AI behaviour set — plus any tool you built for someone else.

**What kills a game design portfolio:**
- A 40-page design document for an unmade MMO.
- "Concepts" and "ideas" with nothing built.
- One enormous unfinished project.
- Team projects where your contribution is unstated.
- No playable build.

## 3. What an environment art portfolio must show

Different again, and the most standardised of the three. ArtStation is the de facto platform; a personal site is optional.

- **Two to four fully realised environments**, rendered **in-engine** (Unreal or Unity, real-time, with the engine stated), at 1920×1080 or higher, with several composed camera angles per scene.
- **Breakdowns for each**: wireframes, texture sheets, material graphs, the modular kit shown laid out flat, triangle counts, texture memory, and lighting setup.
- **Reference boards** alongside the result, showing what you were targeting. This demonstrates observation, which is the actual skill.
- **A modular kit demonstration** — the same kit assembled into three different spaces.
- **One prop or asset breakdown** at high detail: high-poly, low-poly, bake, texture, final.

**What kills it:** Megascans-only scenes with no authored assets; scenes with no lighting design; screenshots at 1280×720; no wireframes; no polycounts; a portfolio that is entirely one biome. The craft itself is covered in **`25_environmental_asset_creation`**, files `01`–`09`.

## 4. Personal projects that actually work

**In graphic design:**
- **A daily or weekly practice** with a hard constraint — a poster a week for a year, a type specimen a day. The volume is the point, and the public commitment is what makes it happen.
- **A real client with a real problem**, however small and however cheap. A local restaurant's menu system teaches more than five self-briefed rebrands.
- **A self-published artefact** — a zine, a book, a risograph print run. Physical objects circulate and get remembered.
- **A reconstruction project** — rebuild a Swiss poster or a Pentagram identity exactly, then write up what you learned. This is the single most efficient learning exercise in the field and it makes an unusually interesting portfolio piece if framed honestly.

**In games:**
- **One small finished game per quarter.** Scope so small it feels trivial, then finish it. Four finished small games in a year beats one unfinished big one in every hiring conversation.
- **A game jam entry you polished afterwards** — the jam gives you the seed, the following month gives you the portfolio piece.
- **A tool or plugin** that other developers use. This is the highest-conversion single item for technical roles because adoption is external validation.
- **A public technical write-up.** A blog post explaining how you solved a specific problem, with code. It functions as a portfolio piece, a networking device and evidence you can communicate.

**In both:** finish things and put them where people can see them. An unfinished project has negative value — it costs you the time and gives you nothing to show.

## 5. Game jams as a credential

Jams are the fastest way to produce finished work, meet collaborators, and prove you can ship under constraint. They function as a genuine line on a CV.

| Jam | Cadence | Duration | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ludum Dare** | ~2×/year | 48 h (Compo, solo, all assets from scratch) / 72 h (Jam, teams) | The oldest and most respected; peer-rated |
| **GMTK Game Jam** | Annual | 48 h | Run by Game Maker's Toolkit; the largest by entry count; strong design-theory focus |
| **Global Game Jam** | Annual, January | 48 h | Physical sites worldwide — the best one for *meeting people in your city* |
| **js13kGames** | Annual | 1 month | 13 kB budget; a real technical credential |
| **7DRL** | Annual | 7 days | Roguelike-specific |
| **itch.io jams** | Continuous | Hours to months | Hundreds running at any time; find one matching your goal |

**How to use a jam properly:**
1. **Scope for the last day, not the first.** Whatever you plan, cut it in half. Then cut it again.
2. **Build a vertical slice of the core loop in the first six hours.** If it isn't fun with placeholder art by then, change it or restart.
3. **Reserve the final 25% of the time for polish and submission.** Unsubmitted jam games are the norm and they are worth nothing.
4. **Ship with a title screen, a restart, and no crashes.** These three things separate a jam entry from a jam artefact.
5. **Afterwards, spend a month turning the best one into a real portfolio piece.**

**As a credential specifically:** placing in the top 10% of Ludum Dare or GMTK is a legible signal. Twenty unremarkable entries are not. Depth of one over breadth of many.

## 6. Modding as a route

Modding remains one of the most effective and least-used routes into games, for a simple reason: **you inherit a working game, so everything you make is playable immediately and comparably.** A reviewer can judge your level against professionally made levels in the same game.

**The routes that work:**
- **Level and map making** — Source engine (Hammer), *Doom* (WADs), *Skyrim* (Creation Kit), *Half-Life 2*, *Portal 2*, CS maps, Rocksmith-style custom content, Roblox and Fortnite Creative/UEFN. Valve has hired directly from its modding community for two decades.
- **Total conversions** — the historical route into the industry. *Counter-Strike*, *Team Fortress*, *DotA*, *Killing Floor*, *PUBG* (via ARMA mods), *Stanley Parable*, *Black Mesa* all began as mods and several became studios.
- **Tool and framework mods** — SMAPI for *Stardew Valley*, SKSE for Skyrim, Forge/Fabric for Minecraft. Building infrastructure that other modders use is the strongest possible technical signal.
- **Game-adjacent open source** — contributing to Godot, O3DE, Bevy, or an engine plugin. Public commit history is verifiable evidence in a way a portfolio is not.

**The parallel in the student world:** DigiPen's *Narbacular Drop* (2005) got its team hired by Valve and became *Portal*; *Tag: The Power of Paint* (2008) became *Portal 2*'s paint mechanic. Student and mod work is taken seriously when it is genuinely good.

**One caution:** check the mod licence and EULA before commercialising or before including proprietary assets in a public portfolio.

## 7. The internship pipeline

**In graphic design**, the internship is the primary hiring channel. Most junior studio roles are filled by former interns. Apply in the winter for summer placements, apply to more places than feels dignified, and target studios whose work you can discuss specifically. A three-month internship where you were useful converts to an offer more often than any portfolio.

**In games**, internships are concentrated at large publishers with formal programmes — EA, Ubisoft, Riot, Epic, Blizzard, Sony, Microsoft — and they run on an academic calendar with applications typically opening 6–9 months ahead. They are extremely competitive and were among the first things cut in 2023–24.

**The structural alternative that works better: the co-op or mandatory placement.** Sheridan's Bachelor of Game Design includes a **mandatory co-op work term** in the spring/summer of third year; Goldsmiths' BA Design includes a **professional placement in year 2**; the Swedish yrkeshögskola model builds an industry internship (LIA) into the qualification. These are structurally superior to self-organised internships because the institution has the relationships and the student has the leverage. If you are choosing a programme, weight this feature heavily.

**If no internship is available:** contract and freelance work, however small; a studio's outsourcing partner; a games-adjacent employer (serious games, simulation, XR, advertising, architectural visualisation) where the same skills apply and the hiring bar is lower. Lateral entry works. See `01_architecture`-adjacent visualisation work and `13_software_unreal_engine` for the archviz route specifically — it is currently one of the most accessible real-time-3D employment markets.

## 8. Networking — GDC and online

### GDC

The Game Developers Conference (San Francisco, annually, usually March) is where the industry's hiring, dealmaking and knowledge-sharing concentrate. Practical notes:

- **Passes are tiered and expensive.** The **Expo Pass** is the cheap one and gets you the show floor, the career pavilion and most of the sponsored sessions — which is where the networking happens anyway. The full **All Access** pass gets the talks, but almost all of the important talks reach the **GDC Vault** eventually.
- **Scholarship and diversity programmes** exist and are worth applying to well in advance.
- **The value is not in the talks.** It is in the hallway, the parties, the roundtables and the career pavilion. Plan the people, not the schedule.
- **Have something to show on a phone.** Thirty seconds of gameplay you can hand someone beats a business card.
- **Follow up within 48 hours**, specifically, referencing what you actually talked about.

**Regional and cheaper alternatives:** Develop:Brighton, Gamescom and Devcom (Cologne), Reboot Develop, Nordic Game (Malmö), PAX (multiple), IndieCade, A MAZE (Berlin and Johannesburg), and **[ZA]** Africa Games Week, Playtopia and rAge in South Africa. For graphic design: the **AIGA** conferences and local chapters, Typographics (New York), TypoBerlin, OFFF (Barcelona) and Design Indaba **[ZA]** (Cape Town).

### Online

- **Where games people actually are:** Discord servers for specific engines, disciplines and jams; Bluesky and Mastodon (games development largely migrated off Twitter/X between 2023 and 2025); the r/gamedev and engine-specific subreddits; Polycount and 80 Level for art; the Ludum Dare and itch.io communities.
- **Where design people are:** It's Nice That, AIGA Eye on Design, Design Observer, the Brand New commentary community, Are.na, and local AIGA/D&AD chapters.
- **What actually builds a network:** publishing useful things consistently. A technical write-up, a free tool, a breakdown of your own work, an honest post-mortem. Being helpful in a Discord for a year builds more professional capital than any amount of connection-requesting.
- **What does not work:** cold-messaging strangers asking for a job; asking to "pick your brain"; posting only when you want something.

## 9. The application and interview process

### Graphic design

1. **Application** — portfolio (site + PDF), CV, and a short covering note that references the studio's actual work.
2. **Portfolio review / first interview** — you walk through three projects. Prepare a five-minute version and a fifteen-minute version of each. Expect "why did you do that?" on every decision. "It looked better" is an acceptable answer only if you can then say *why* it looked better.
3. **Test project** — increasingly common, often unpaid, sometimes substantial. **Paid tests are the ethical norm; ask.** A test that takes more than a day's work and is unpaid is a red flag about the employer.
4. **Team interview / culture fit.**
5. **Offer.** Negotiate. The first number is not the last number.

### Games

1. **Application** — portfolio, CV, and often a studio-specific questionnaire. Games hiring is disproportionately referral-driven, so a warm introduction is worth more than a strong cover letter.
2. **Screening call** with a recruiter — role fit, salary expectation, availability, visa status.
3. **Design or art test** — usually 1–3 days of work, ideally paid. Design tests commonly ask you to critique a level, design an encounter, or balance a system. Art tests ask for a specific asset to a specific spec, which is a test of following the brief as much as of craft.
4. **Discipline interview** — deep technical questions. For programmers this is a coding interview; for designers it is a design-reasoning conversation ("here is a mechanic, tell me how you would teach it"); for artists it is a portfolio deep-dive.
5. **Cross-discipline panel** — can you talk to programmers, artists and producers?
6. **Offer**, often with relocation and visa steps that take months.

**Questions worth asking them:** What is the team size and how is it structured? What is the current phase of the project? How is design feedback given and by whom? What happened to the last person in this role? What is your crunch history in the last two years, specifically? How is scope decided and who can cut it?

## 10. The honest state of the entry-level market in 2026

### Games

**It is bad, and specifically bad at the entry level.** The numbers, again: over **10,500 roles cut in 2023**, **~14,600 in 2024** — more than 2022 and 2023 combined — continued cuts through 2025, and **~4,600 more with 22 studio closures in the first half of 2026**. Over 30 studios closed outright across 2023–25 and Embracer alone cut 7,761 people, closed or divested 44 studios and cancelled 80 projects. Epic cut over 1,000 in March 2026; Bungie ~300 in June 2026; Meta's Reality Labs closed three studios in a single day in January 2026.

**Junior roles were eliminated first.** UK junior postings fell from **9.4% of the market in 2022 to 2.9% in 2023**. The consequence is that every entry-level opening now competes against experienced developers who were laid off. A junior candidate is not competing against other juniors; they are competing against a five-year mid-level who will take the role.

**What this means practically:**
1. **Provable, day-one productivity is the only differentiator.** Not potential. Not enthusiasm. A shipped thing, a tool people use, a system you built.
2. **Specialise.** "Game designer" is the most contested and least-hired title. Technical designer, technical artist, tools programmer, gameplay programmer, UI engineer and QA automation are all measurably easier to enter.
3. **Widen the definition of "games".** Serious games, simulation, training, XR, medical and defence visualisation, architectural visualisation, virtual production, museum and exhibition interactive, and gambling/social casino all hire the same skills, often at higher pay, and are far less contested. Real-time 3D skills are more portable in 2026 than at any previous point.
4. **Consider the geography.** Studios cluster: Montréal, Vancouver, Warsaw, Stockholm, Malmö, Helsinki, Tokyo, Seoul, Shanghai, Austin, Seattle, Los Angeles, Guildford/Brighton, Dundee. Willingness to relocate materially changes the odds; visa routes usually require a degree (see `05`).
5. **The independent route is genuinely open.** *Stardew Valley*: one person, four and a half years, **50 million copies by February 2026**. *Silksong*: a tiny team, over 7 million copies in three months. Distribution is solved; discovery is the hard problem, and it is a marketing problem, not a permission problem.
6. **Do not do unpaid "revenue share" work for a stranger's startup.** The overwhelming majority of these ship nothing. If you are going to work for free, work for free on your own project.

### Graphic design

**Structurally flat rather than collapsing.** US employment growth of **2% for 2024–34** against a 3% all-occupations baseline, ~20,000 US openings a year that are almost entirely replacement rather than expansion, and a median wage of **US$61,300** (May 2024) with the 10th percentile below **US$37,600**. The pressure is from template platforms at the low end, in-house marketing teams in the middle, and generative tooling absorbing routine production.

**But:** the adjacent digital roles pay substantially more — **web and digital interface designers at US$98,090**, **art directors at US$111,040** (both May 2024) — and are growing at **7%** rather than 2%. The practical implication is unambiguous: **learn the digital product side.** A designer who can do typography *and* build a design system is in a different market from one who can only do the first.

**What is working in 2026:**
- Freelance and studio-of-one practice, especially serving international clients from a lower-cost base — which is the strongest available position for a **[ZA]/[NA]** southern African designer.
- Specialism: type design, editorial, packaging, motion, design systems, brand strategy. Generalists are the most exposed.
- In-house at product companies, where design sits close to engineering.
- The top end of identity work, which has not been automated and shows no sign of being.

**The one thing that has not changed in either field:** the person who has finished and published a body of work is in a completely different position from the person who has not. Everything in this file reduces to that.
