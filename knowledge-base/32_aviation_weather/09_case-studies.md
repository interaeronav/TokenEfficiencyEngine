# Weather accidents and incidents that changed the industry

**Summary.** Almost every rule in `03` and `06` was written after someone died. This file gives, for each case: the facts as established by the investigating authority (or the best verified source available), the meteorological mechanism, and — the point of the exercise — what changed. Read them for the mechanism, not for the blame.

## 1. BOAC 911 — mountain wave, 5 March 1966

**Facts.** A Boeing 707-436, **G-APFE**, departed Tokyo Haneda for Hong Kong on a clear day and routed close to **Mount Fuji** for the passengers' benefit. Near Gotemba the aircraft encountered turbulence and broke up in flight. **124 died** (113 passengers, 11 crew).

**Mechanism.** The investigation concluded the aircraft met **abnormally severe turbulence over Gotemba imposing a gust load considerably in excess of the design limit**. Powerful **mountain waves in the lee of Fuji**, breaking down into small-scale turbulence, are the accepted explanation. Peak structural loading was assessed at around **7.5 g** — evidenced by a passenger's camera jamming during the breakup. The **vertical stabiliser failed first**, then the ventral fin and engine pylons, then the remaining tail and the outer wings.

**What changed.** Fleet inspections of the 707/720 found **stress cracking in vertical stabiliser bolt holes was a widespread issue**, and corrective maintenance followed. More broadly, this accident — on a clear, beautiful day, in an aircraft deliberately flown close to a mountain — established mountain-wave turbulence as a first-order structural hazard rather than a discomfort, and it is the reason mountain wave appears on SIGWX charts and in SIGMETs as its own category.

**The lesson.** Clear air and a beautiful view are not evidence of a benign atmosphere. Severe turbulence in the lee of a large isolated peak in a strong perpendicular flow is a structural risk, and the only defence is not going there.

## 2. Pan Am 759 — microburst on takeoff, 9 July 1982

**Facts.** A Boeing 727-235, **N4737** (*Clipper Defiance*), took off from New Orleans International in convective conditions, struck trees shortly after liftoff, and crashed into a residential area of **Kenner, Louisiana**. **153 died**: 145 on board and 8 on the ground.

**Mechanism.** A **microburst-induced windshear** during liftoff — a downdraft combined with a rapidly decreasing headwind — that the crew had insufficient time to recognise or counteract. The NTSB found the cause to be the encounter with microburst-induced windshear, combined with **the limited capability of then-current windshear detection technology**.

**Contributing.** The investigation found the crew had been given a **two-hour-old weather recording** despite airport procedures calling for hourly updates — a briefing-currency failure that is entirely recognisable today in any operation where the ATIS is not refreshed promptly on a convective day.

**What changed.** Together with Delta 191 three years later, this accident drove the FAA's programme of **windshear detection systems at airports and on board aircraft in the US by 1993**, and the airborne windshear detection development that followed.

**The lesson.** On a convective day, weather information ages in minutes, not hours. A microburst that did not exist when the ATIS was recorded can be fully developed by the time you line up.

## 3. British Airways 9 — volcanic ash, 24 June 1982

**Facts.** A Boeing 747-236B, **G-BDXH** (*City of Edinburgh*), en route Kuala Lumpur–Perth at **37 000 ft**, flew into the ash cloud from **Mount Galunggung, West Java**. All four **Rolls-Royce RB211** engines failed sequentially between **13:42 and 13:43 UTC**. The aircraft glided down to about **13 500 ft** before restarts began at **13:56 UTC**; all four eventually restarted, one was shut down again, and the aircraft landed at **Halim Perdanakusuma, Jakarta** on three engines with a windscreen so abraded the crew could barely see out. Everyone survived.

**Mechanism.** **The dry ash cloud did not appear on the weather radar, which is designed to detect moisture.** Ash abraded the airframe and engines, melted in the combustors and resolidified in the turbines, and deprived the engines of the ability to sustain combustion. Descent cooled the deposits, which cracked off, allowing relight.

**What changed.** Indonesian authorities initially reopened the airspace near Galunggung, then closed it permanently after a **Singapore Airlines 747 suffered similar damage on 13 July 1982**. Airways were rerouted and an ash-monitoring watch was established. This pair of events is the direct origin of the **International Airways Volcano Watch** and the **VAAC system**.

**The lesson.** Weather radar does not see volcanic ash, and it does not see dry hail or ice crystals either. The absence of a radar return is not the absence of a hazard.

## 4. Air Canada 143 — the Gimli Glider, 23 July 1983

**Facts.** A Boeing 767-233, **C-GAUN**, ran out of fuel at **41 000 ft** over Red Lake, Ontario, and glided for **17 minutes** to a dead-stick landing on a decommissioned airbase at **Gimli, Manitoba**, that had been converted to a drag strip. Two tyres blew out. **All 69 on board survived**, with 10 minor injuries.

**Mechanism (systems, not weather).** A defective **fuel quantity indicating system** forced reliance on manual dripstick measurement. The conversion from volume to mass was done with the **imperial density figure 1.77 lb/L instead of the metric 0.803 kg/L**, on an aircraft that was Air Canada's first metric type. The crew believed they had about **13 597 kg** of fuel when they had **6 169 kg** — less than half of what was required. The error was made twice: at Montreal and again at the Ottawa stopover.

**What changed.** The Board of Inquiry recommended immediate standardisation of units across the fleet, improved fuelling procedures, better training, and the standardised safety measures already used by US and European carriers.

**Why it is in a weather file.** Because it is the cleanest available demonstration that **the number on the flight plan is a chain of assumptions, and a units or datum error anywhere in that chain is invisible until it is fatal.** The same class of error applies to weather: a QNH in inches read as hectopascals, a temperature in Fahrenheit, a visibility in statute miles read as kilometres, an RVR in feet read as metres, a wind in m/s read as knots. Every one of those substitutions has been made on a line flight.

## 5. Delta 191 — microburst on approach, 2 August 1985

**Facts.** A Lockheed **L-1011-385-1 TriStar**, **N726DA**, on approach to **Dallas/Fort Worth**, flew through a thunderstorm on final and crashed short of the runway at Irving, Texas. **137 died** (136 on board, 1 on the ground).

**Mechanism.** A **severe microburst** on short final. The crew applied maximum thrust but could not overcome the downdraft and the tailwind. The NTSB probable cause was **the flight crew's decision to fly through a thunderstorm, the lack of procedures or training to avoid or escape microbursts, and the lack of hazard information on wind shear.**

**What changed.** More than any other single accident:
- The FAA **mandated onboard windshear detection systems for commercial aircraft**.
- **NASA developed airborne windshear detection and alerting** using Doppler radar, which became today's **predictive windshear (PWS)** systems.
- **Mandatory pilot training** in microburst recognition and the **escape manoeuvre** — the manoeuvre in `03` §1.7 is a direct descendant of the FAA *Pilot Windshear Guide* written after this accident.
- Airport **alerting and notification systems** were improved (LLWAS expansion, and eventually **TDWR**).

Together with Pan Am 759, Delta 191 is the reason microburst accidents to US commercial aircraft essentially stopped after **USAir 1016 in 1994**.

**The lesson.** The entire modern windshear defence — avoid, detect, escape — was written in response to this accident. Using it is not optional, and the *avoid* limb does most of the work.

## 6. TACA 110 — dual flameout in hail and heavy rain, 24 May 1988

**Facts.** A Boeing **737-3T0**, **N75356**, with **CFM56** engines, descended into an **intense thunderstorm** near New Orleans with heavy torrential rain, hail and turbulence. **Both engines flamed out at 16 500 ft.** The crew made a successful forced landing on a grass levee beside NASA's **Michoud Assembly Facility**. Nobody sustained more than minor injuries and the aircraft had only minor hail damage.

**Mechanism.** **Water ingestion caused both engines to flame out during descent at low engine RPM.** At flight idle the core is at its least able to tolerate a large water/hail mass flow, and the combustor flame is at its least robust.

**What changed.** CFM International modified the CFM56:
- a **sensor to activate continuous combustor ignition** in heavy rain or hail;
- a **redesigned nose cone and fan blade spacing** to deflect hail away from the core;
- **additional bleed doors** to drain water from the engine.
The aircraft itself was repaired, flown out from the levee, and served until 2016.

**The lesson.** Engine anti-ice and continuous ignition exist for exactly this. Descending at idle through heavy precipitation is the worst combination available; the procedure of selecting continuous ignition and carrying a little thrust in heavy rain is a direct product of this event.

## 7. KLM 867 — volcanic ash, 15 December 1989

**Facts.** A Boeing **747-406M**, **PH-BFC** (*City of Calgary*), flew into the ash cloud from **Mount Redoubt, Alaska**, at about **41 000 ft**. All four **General Electric CF6-80C2B1F** engines failed. The aircraft descended more than **14 000 ft** before two engines were restarted, and it landed safely at Anchorage. **All 245 on board survived.**

**Mechanism.** Ash melted and formed **a glass coating inside the engines that also fooled the engine temperature sensors** — an important detail, because it means the instrumentation misled the crew about the engines' state.

**Cost.** Over **US$80 million**; **all four engines required replacement**. The aircraft returned to service and flew for KLM until **14 March 2018**.

**What changed.** This event, seven years after BA9, demonstrated that the 1982 lesson had not propagated far enough. It substantially reinforced the North Pacific ash-monitoring effort and the **Anchorage VAAC's** role, and drove the establishment of routine ash-avoidance procedures for the Alaskan and Kamchatkan routes now used by every trans-Pacific great-circle flight.

**The lesson.** Ash disables the very instruments you would use to diagnose it. Trust the pattern — multiple engines misbehaving at once in a region with an active volcano — over the individual gauges.

## 8. Aeroperú 603 — blocked static ports, 2 October 1996

**Facts.** A Boeing **757-23A**, **N52AW**, departed Lima at **05:42 UTC** with 70 on board. Immediately after takeoff the crew had erratic instruments and a cascade of contradictory warnings. At night, over water, with no visual reference, and with ATC's own radar altitude readouts derived from the aircraft's corrupted transponder data appearing to corroborate the cockpit indications, the crew descended believing they were higher than they were. The left wingtip struck the Pacific about **25 minutes** after the emergency declaration. **All 70 died.**

**Mechanism.** **Adhesive tape left over the static ports** after cleaning and polishing. With the static system sealed, the altimeters, vertical speed indicators and airspeed indicators all became meaningless while continuing to display plausible values — the pressure trapped in the system made the altimeters read approximately the departure elevation regardless of actual height. Overspeed and stall warnings triggered simultaneously.

**Aftermath.** The maintenance worker received a two-year suspended sentence for negligent homicide in 1998; Boeing and Aeroperú settled in international arbitration by December 1999. **Boeing later acknowledged the design weakness in relying on adhesive tape rather than removable maintenance covers.**

**What changed.** Static port protection is now by **conspicuous "REMOVE BEFORE FLIGHT" covers**, not tape; the pre-flight external inspection specifically covers static ports; and unreliable-airspeed procedures — **pitch and thrust settings from a table, memorised** — became a standard part of transport-category training.

**Why it is in a weather file.** Because the pressure altimeter and the ASI are meteorological instruments. Everything in `01` §3 about altimetry assumes a valid static source. When that assumption fails, no amount of altimetry knowledge helps — only the pitch-and-power table does.

## 9. Southwest 1248 — contaminated runway overrun, 8 December 2005

**Facts.** A Boeing **737-7H4**, **N471WN**, landed at **Chicago Midway** on runway 13C in snow. Nearly **8 inches of snow** lay on the ground, visibility was below 1 mile, and **RVR was 4500 ft**. The aircraft overran the end of the runway, went through the airport perimeter fence and onto a road, killing **a six-year-old boy in a car**; 11 others were injured.

**Mechanism and contributing factors.**
- **Thrust reversers were deployed 18 seconds after touchdown**, by which time only about 1000 ft of runway remained.
- The aircraft needed about **5300 ft** to stop; only **4500 ft** remained after touchdown.
- There was an **8 kt tailwind against a 5 kt limit**.
- The **autobrake system was newly introduced** on the fleet with inadequate pilot familiarisation.

**NTSB probable cause**: the pilots' failure to use available reverse thrust promptly to decelerate, resulting in a runway overrun, with unfamiliarity with the new autobrake system distracting from reverse deployment.

**What changed.** This accident is the origin of the modern runway condition framework:
- The FAA convened the **TALPA ARC** (Takeoff and Landing Performance Assessment Aviation Rulemaking Committee).
- Its output — the **Runway Condition Assessment Matrix (RCAM)** and the **runway condition code** — was **implemented in the US in 2016**, and adopted internationally by ICAO as the **Global Reporting Format**.
- **Engineered materials arresting systems (EMAS)** were installed at Midway.
- Enhanced training in **landing distance assessment and safety margins** became standard.

**The lesson.** Landing distance on a contaminated runway is not a number you look up once at dispatch. It is an in-flight calculation using the actual conditions at the actual landing weight with the actual wind, and every element of the deceleration plan — autobrake, reverse, spoilers — has to be used on time.

## 10. Air France 447 — ITCZ, ice crystals, and a stall, 1 June 2009

**Facts.** An Airbus **A330-203**, **F-GZCP**, en route Rio de Janeiro–Paris, entered a **mesoscale convective system within the ITCZ** at cruise. **228 died** (216 passengers, 12 crew). The wreckage was located on the ocean floor in 2011.

**Mechanism.** **Ice crystals accumulated in the pitot probes**, producing temporary inconsistencies between the measured airspeeds. The autopilot disconnected and the flight control law reconfigured. The crew did not apply the unreliable-airspeed procedure, made inappropriate nose-up control inputs, and **never recognised the resulting aerodynamic stall**. The aircraft remained stalled throughout a **3.5-minute descent from 38 000 ft**.

**The meteorological chain, step by step:**
1. Deep convection in the ITCZ over the tropical Atlantic, at night, with tops far above the cruise level.
2. High concentrations of **small ice crystals** in the anvil and upper outflow, giving **little or no radar return** — the crew had no reflectivity-based reason to think they were in anything hazardous.
3. Crystals ingested by the **Thales pitot probes**, partially melting inside them and refreezing, blocking the total-pressure port.
4. Airspeed inconsistency → autopilot and autothrust disconnect → alternate law → a startled crew at night in turbulence with no external reference.

**What changed.**
- An **Airworthiness Directive in August 2009** mandated replacement of the affected Thales probes with Goodrich units on A330/A340 fleets.
- Fundamental revision of **stall recognition and recovery training**, including the recognition that at high altitude a stall recovery requires *lowering* the nose and accepting large altitude loss, and the removal of the old "minimum altitude loss" recovery philosophy.
- Renewed emphasis on **manual handling at high altitude**.
- Recommendations for **clearer airspeed-inconsistency indication** and improved stall warning behaviour at high angle of attack (the AF447 stall warning stopped and restarted in a way that reinforced the wrong control input).
- Rules on **flight recorder locator beacon duration** and deployable/streaming recorders followed from the search difficulty.
- Industry-wide work on **ice-crystal icing** (leading to the Appendix D certification environment) and on high-altitude ice crystal engine events.

**The lesson.** Cruising near tropical convection at night is the highest-risk routine environment in long-haul aviation. Radar does not see ice crystals; the ITCZ is 400 nm wide; and the failure that results is not an engine failure but an *information* failure that puts a crew into an unfamiliar control law at 02:10 in the morning. Avoid by margin, know the unreliable-airspeed pitch/thrust figures without looking them up, and remember that at altitude the aircraft can and will stall.

## 11. Eyjafjallajökull — the 2010 European ash closure

**Facts.** The eruption began **14 April 2010**. European airspace was largely closed **15–23 April**, with intermittent closures into mid-May. **Over 95 000 flights cancelled in the six-day ban**; later estimates give **107 000 over eight days**, about **48 % of global air traffic**, affecting roughly **10 million passengers**. IATA estimated **€148 million per day** in airline losses; total industry losses around **€1.3 billion**; UK airports lost about **£80 million over 6.5 days**.

**Mechanism.** A subglacial basaltic-andesite eruption producing fine, widely-dispersed ash, injected into a persistent northwesterly flow that carried it directly over the busiest airspace in the world. **London VAAC** issued the dispersion forecasts.

**The regulatory problem.** The pre-2010 position was **zero tolerance** — any forecast ash meant closure — which, applied to a dispersion model's forecast plume, closed a continent. There was no agreed concentration threshold below which flight was acceptable, and no airborne means of measuring concentration.

**What changed.**
- **21 April 2010**: the UK CAA introduced safety thresholds of **200–2000 µg/m³**.
- **18 May 2010**: the enhanced-procedures ceiling was raised to **4 mg/m³**.
- A **zone framework** replaced blanket closure: no-fly, enhanced-procedures and low-contamination zones, plus the **Time Limited Zone** concept.
- **Engine manufacturers were required to define ash ingestion limits** for their products — previously nobody had.
- **European ATM coordination** (Network Manager / Single European Sky) accelerated.
- Operators developed **volcanic ash safety risk assessments and contingency plans**, which is now a standard element of an operator's management system.
- Airborne ash detection research (infrared imaging systems) followed.

**The lesson.** A hazard with no agreed quantitative threshold defaults to zero tolerance, and zero tolerance applied to a probabilistic forecast is economically catastrophic. The industry's response — define the concentration limits, define the zones, define the aircraft limits — is a template for how to handle any new hazard, and it took an eruption to force it.

## 12. Emirates 521 — hot day, windshear, long landing, failed go-around, 3 August 2016

**Facts.** A Boeing **777-31H**, **A6-EMW**, arriving at **Dubai International** in an **ambient temperature of 48 °C** with **windshear** affecting the approach. The aircraft touched down **1100 m beyond the threshold at 162 kt**. The **RAAS** issued a "LONG LANDING" advisory and the crew initiated a go-around. **The autothrottle did not respond when TOGA was selected.** The aircraft climbed to about **85 ft** before sinking back onto the runway with the gear partially retracted, sliding and catching fire. **All 300 occupants survived** with 32 injured; **one firefighter, Jassim al-Balooshi, died** in the response.

**Findings.** The GCAA concluded the flight crew **did not effectively scan and monitor the primary flight instrumentation** and were unaware the autothrottle had not engaged, and that **reliance on automation and lack of training in go-arounds from close to the runway** significantly affected performance.

**The meteorological contribution.**
- **48 °C** gives a density altitude of roughly 3500–4000 ft at a near-sea-level airport, so the true airspeed and ground speed at any indicated speed are ~6–7 % higher, the landing distance is ~14 % longer, and the available thrust is well below flat-rated.
- **Windshear on the approach** produced the speed and glidepath excursions that led to the long touchdown.
- The **go-around from a touchdown or near-touchdown state** — the "baulked landing" case — is the least-practised and most performance-critical manoeuvre available, and it is at its worst in exactly these conditions.

**What changed.** The accident drove industry attention to **go-around training from very low energy states and from the runway**, to **automation monitoring** (specifically the requirement to verify thrust is actually increasing rather than assuming the automation has done it), and to the hot-day landing performance case.

**Why it matters to a Doha crew.** This is the local environment, on the local network, in the local season, at the neighbouring hub. Everything in `06` §4 about hot-day performance and everything in `03` §2 about windshear converges in this accident.

## 13. Gulf dust events — the September 2015 Middle East episode

In early **September 2015** an exceptionally severe and widespread dust event affected Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Israel, Jordan, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Qatar and the UAE, with visibility falling to a few hundred metres over a large area for several days, hospitals overwhelmed by respiratory cases, and widespread flight cancellations and diversions across the region including at Beirut, Baghdad and the Gulf hubs.

**Mechanism (as generally described).** An unusually strong and prolonged northerly/northwesterly flow over the Tigris–Euphrates basin, over soils left exceptionally dry and disturbed by drought and conflict-related land-use change, lifted an enormous quantity of fine dust and carried it southwest and southeast. The event was distinguished from a routine shamal by its **duration, depth and areal extent**.

**Operational consequences.** Suspended dust reduces visibility over an area far too large to divert around, degrades slant visual range far more than the reported horizontal visibility suggests, and — unlike fog — is not confined to the surface layer, so it affects the whole approach and the climb-out. It also produces a sustained maintenance burden through engine and airframe erosion.

**The lesson that does hold regardless.** Regional dust events are a **capacity and diversion problem, not a single-aerodrome problem**. When the whole basin goes below minima simultaneously, the alternate must be outside the basin, and the fuel to get there has to have been loaded before departure. This is structurally the same problem as the Gulf winter fog case in `07` §3.

## 14. GNSS jamming and spoofing — the live case

Since 2022 there has been a large and sustained increase in deliberate GNSS interference affecting civil aviation, concentrated around conflict zones: the eastern Mediterranean and Levant, the Black Sea and Baltic, the Caucasus, northern Iraq and Iran, and the Gulf corridors. Reported effects on transport aircraft include:

- **Jamming**: loss of GNSS position with `GPS PRIMARY LOST`, loss of RNP capability, unavailability of GNSS-based approaches, and reversion to DME/DME or IRS-only navigation with a growing position error.
- **Spoofing**: acceptance of a false position, corruption of the IRS position (which then persists after leaving the affected area), false EGPWS terrain alerts, corrupted GNSS time, and in reported cases complete loss of the aircraft's navigation capability requiring ATC vectors and, in at least one widely reported event, a diversion.

**Responses adopted by regulators and operators:** EASA and other authorities have issued Safety Information Bulletins; operators have issued crew bulletins for the affected FIRs; procedures now cover cross-checking GNSS against conventional navaids, not accepting GNSS-derived time, IRS realignment on the ground after a spoofing event, and — in some cases — avoiding the affected airspace altogether.

> ⚠️ **This entry could not be verified from a fetched source in this session.** Specific incidents, dates, aircraft and operator names have deliberately not been given, because none could be confirmed. **Consult the current EASA Safety Information Bulletin on GNSS outages and interference, the IATA/EUROCONTROL GNSS interference material, and the applicable NOTAMs and operator bulletins** before treating any of this as operational guidance. `needs-verification`

**Why it belongs alongside the weather cases.** The failure mode is identical to Aeroperú 603 and to AF447: a sensor produces plausible but wrong information, the automation accepts it, and the crew's mental model is corrupted before anyone realises a failure has occurred. The defence is the same in all three: know what the raw, independent, physically-grounded sources are saying — pitch and power, the standby instruments, the conventional navaids — and be willing to disbelieve a plausible display.

## 15. What the whole list teaches

Five patterns run through every case above:

1. **The hazard the sensor cannot see is the dangerous one.** Ash, dry hail, ice crystals and dry microbursts are all invisible to weather radar. Blocked static ports and spoofed GNSS produce plausible readings. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.
2. **Weather information ages fast.** A two-hour-old briefing (Pan Am 759), a TAF that expires before your ETA, an ATIS recorded before the cell developed — all the same failure.
3. **Margin, not skill, is the defence.** Delta 191 and Pan Am 759 were flown by competent crews. The defence against a microburst is not to fly through it. The defence against a mountain wave is not to be in its lee.
4. **The performance environment compounds everything.** Hot day, contaminated runway, tailwind, high weight — each individually manageable, together the Emirates 521 and Southwest 1248 accidents.
5. **The industry only quantifies a hazard after it has cost enough.** Ash concentration thresholds after 2010; the RCAM after 2005; windshear systems after 1985; probe standards after 2009. The corollary: a hazard that has not yet been quantified is not therefore safe — it is merely unmeasured.
