# Global circulation, air masses, fronts and regional regimes

**Summary.** The atmosphere is a heat engine transporting energy from the equator to the poles. It cannot do that with a single overturning cell because the Earth rotates, so it does it with three cells per hemisphere, two jet streams, and — in mid-latitudes — a stream of baroclinic eddies we call depressions. Air masses are the reservoirs; fronts are the boundaries between them; jet streams are both the steering current and the energy source. This file builds that picture from the top down and then walks the regional regimes an airline actually operates in, with the Arabian Gulf treated in the detail a Doha-based crew needs.

## 1. The general circulation

### 1.1 Why three cells

A non-rotating Earth would run one giant Hadley cell per hemisphere. Rotation breaks it. Air moving poleward aloft conserves angular momentum, accelerates eastward, and by about 30° latitude is moving so fast relative to the surface that the flow becomes dynamically unstable and the poleward transport can no longer be done by a direct thermal cell. So:

**The Hadley cell (0–30°) — thermally direct.** Warm, moist air rises at the ITCZ, releasing enormous latent heat; flows poleward at the tropopause; radiatively cools and subsides in the **subtropical high-pressure belt** around 25–35°; returns equatorward as the **trade winds** (NE in the northern hemisphere, SE in the southern). The subsiding branch is why every major desert on Earth — Sahara, Arabian, Thar, Kalahari/Namib, Atacama, Australian — sits at that latitude. Doha lies squarely under it.

**The Ferrel cell (30–60°) — thermally indirect.** Driven not by direct heating but by the eddies (depressions) themselves; it is a statistical residual, not a physical conveyor you could point at. Surface flow is the mid-latitude **westerlies**.

**The Polar cell (60–90°) — weak, thermally direct.** Subsidence over the pole, surface easterlies flowing out to the polar front.

Two convergence zones define the weather:
- The **ITCZ** at the Hadley cells' ascending branch — deep convection, no fronts (temperature contrast is negligible), heavy rain, tops to FL550+.
- The **Polar Front** at ~50–60° where cold polar air meets warm subtropical air — the birthplace of mid-latitude depressions.

### 1.2 The subtropical high-pressure belt

Not a continuous ring but a chain of semi-permanent anticyclones: the **Azores/Bermuda High**, the **Pacific (Hawaiian) High**, the **St Helena High**, the **Mascarene High**, the **Australian High**. They shift north in the northern summer and south in the northern winter. They matter operationally because:

- Subsidence produces a **trade-wind inversion** at 1500–2500 m, capping convection over vast ocean areas, producing shallow trade cumulus, excellent CAVOK conditions, and a sharp haze top.
- Their western flanks feed moisture into monsoons and hurricane tracks; their eastern flanks bring cold upwelling and coastal stratus (California, Namibia, Peru, Morocco).
- The **Azores High ridging** over Europe is the difference between a mild Atlantic westerly regime and a blocking pattern that parks fog over northern Europe for a week.

### 1.3 Jet streams

A jet stream is a narrow ribbon of very strong wind near the tropopause, formed wherever there is a strong horizontal temperature gradient through a deep layer. **Thermal wind** is the whole explanation: the vertical shear of the geostrophic wind is proportional to the horizontal temperature gradient, so a strong front at the surface produces a jet above it.

| | Polar front jet | Subtropical jet |
|---|---|---|
| Latitude | **30–60°**, highly variable | ~**30°**, more steady |
| Height | ~FL300, at the polar tropopause break | ~FL390–450 |
| Season | Strongest and furthest equatorward in **winter** | Strongest in **winter**, weak/absent in summer |
| Associated surface feature | Polar front / depression families | Poleward edge of the Hadley cell; not usually a surface front |
| Turbulence | Frequent CAT on the cold (poleward) side and below the core | CAT common, often in clear air with no cloud clue |

Structure worth carrying:
- **Core** where wind is maximum; speed falls off much faster on the **poleward/cold side** (typically ~20 kt/60 nm laterally) than the equatorward side. **Vertical shear** of the order of 5–10 kt/1000 ft below the core.
- **Jet streaks** — mesoscale wind maxima embedded in the jet. Their **entrance and exit regions** have transverse ageostrophic circulations. The **left exit** and **right entrance** are the classic upper divergence quadrants that force surface cyclogenesis. If you remember only one thing about jet dynamics, remember that: surface lows deepen under the left-front quadrant of a jet streak (northern hemisphere).
- **Tropopause folds** — the tropopause dips down under the jet on the cold side, bringing dry stratospheric (high potential vorticity, high ozone) air down into the troposphere. Visible as a dark stripe on the water vapour channel; a reliable CAT indicator.
- **Climate signal**: transatlantic wintertime CAT is projected to increase by roughly **60 % (light), 95 % (moderate) and 150 % (severe)** by CO₂ doubling.

Operational use is direct: eastbound North Atlantic and Pacific flights ride the jet; westbound avoid it. A 1952 Pan Am Tokyo–Honolulu flight cut 18 hours to 11.5 by using it. On the Doha network, the **subtropical jet across the Middle East, northern India and China** dominates DOH–East Asia sectors in winter, giving 100–150 kt tailwinds eastbound and brutal headwinds westbound.

### 1.4 The ITCZ

The ascending branch of the Hadley circulation, where the NE and SE trades converge. Characteristics:

- Not a line but a **zone**, often several bands, frequently double (a northern and southern ITCZ over the eastern Pacific).
- Migrates with the **thermal equator**, lagging the sun by ~6 weeks. **Far more migration over continents than oceans** — over Africa it swings from ~20°N in July to ~15°S in January; over the central Pacific it barely leaves 5–10°N.
- Deep convection with **CB tops routinely FL450–FL550**, embedded, often at night (the diurnal maximum over ocean is early morning, over land late afternoon/evening).
- Very little horizontal temperature contrast → **no fronts**, no reliable satellite-derived structure at low level, weak surface winds ("the doldrums").
- The aviation hazard is the convection: severe turbulence, hail, ice-crystal icing at cruise, lightning, and the difficulty of picking a deviation route through a 400-nm-wide band at night on radar. **Air France 447** was lost after crossing an MCS within the ITCZ.

Crossing points that matter to a Gulf-based operator: the Atlantic ITCZ on South America routes (typically 2–10°N, worst boreal summer), the African ITCZ on southern Africa routes (position depends on month), and the Indian Ocean ITCZ / monsoon trough on Africa and Indian Ocean island routes.

## 2. Air masses

An **air mass** is a body of air with near-uniform temperature and humidity in the horizontal, acquired by long residence over a source region with uniform surface characteristics. Source regions are where the circulation is slow — the subtropical highs, the polar highs, and large continental interiors.

### 2.1 Classification

Two letters, sometimes three:

| Moisture (lower case) | Latitude/temperature (upper case) |
|---|---|
| **c** continental (dry) | **A** Arctic / **AA** Antarctic |
| **m** maritime (moist) | **P** Polar |
| | **T** Tropical |
| | **E** Equatorial |
| | **M** Monsoon (some schemes) |

A third letter is sometimes added for the thermal relationship to the surface: **w** (warm — air warmer than the surface it is moving over) or **k** (cold — air colder than the surface).

| Air mass | Typical source | Character on arrival | Aviation signature |
|---|---|---|---|
| **cA/cP** | Siberia, northern Canada, Arctic basin in winter | Very cold, very dry, stable at source | Crystal-clear; becomes **cPk** and violently unstable over warm sea → snow showers, squalls, severe icing in convective cloud, sharp CB tops |
| **mP** | North Atlantic, North Pacific | Cool, moist, conditionally unstable | The classic "polar maritime" airstream behind a cold front: showers, good visibility, cumulus, bumpy below FL100 |
| **mT** | Azores/Bermuda High, Bay of Bengal | Warm, very moist, stable when moving poleward (**mTw**) | Advection fog and sea fog, low stratus, drizzle, poor visibility, smooth flight; warm sector of a depression |
| **cT** | Sahara, Arabian Peninsula, Thar | Very hot, very dry, unstable near the surface, capped aloft | Dust and haze, huge density altitude, superadiabatic surface layer, dust devils; the Gulf summer air mass |
| **mE** | Equatorial oceans | Hot, extremely moist, deeply unstable | Deep convection, ITCZ weather |

### 2.2 Modification

The label tells you the *history*; the weather comes from the *modification in transit*. Warm air moving over a cold surface is stabilised from below (fog, stratus, drizzle, poor visibility — a **w** air mass). Cold air moving over a warm surface is destabilised from below (showers, cumulus, good visibility, turbulence — a **k** air mass). The single most useful diagnostic on approach is: *is this air mass warmer or colder than the surface it is crossing?*

## 3. Fronts

A **front** is the sloping boundary between two air masses, marked by a discontinuity in temperature, dewpoint (better: θw), wind direction, and pressure tendency. The front is the *warm* boundary of the cold air; the cold air always forms the wedge.

### 3.1 Warm front

- **Slope 1:100 to 1:150** — very shallow. Cloud appears ~600–700 nm ahead of the surface front.
- **Cloud sequence approaching from ahead**: Ci → Cs (halo) → As (sun as through ground glass) → Ns with continuous rain → low stratus and stratus fractus (scud) in the rain-cooled air.
- **Precipitation**: continuous, moderate, ahead of the surface front. Duration 4–8 hours.
- **Freezing rain risk** where rain from the warm air aloft falls through a sub-freezing layer beneath — the worst icing environment in aviation.
- **Wind**: backs ahead of the front, veers on passage (northern hemisphere). Southern hemisphere reverses the sense.
- **Pressure**: falls steadily ahead, steadies after passage.
- **Temperature**: rises on passage. Dewpoint rises more.
- **Visibility**: deteriorates progressively; often poor in the warm sector afterwards (mTw over a cold surface).
- **Turbulence**: generally light. The hazard is icing, low cloud and low visibility, plus **frontal wave** convection embedded in the Ns (invisible to the eye, visible on radar).

### 3.2 Cold front

- **Slope 1:50 to 1:80** — steeper, and the leading edge is often blunt/undercut by friction.
- Two flavours:
  - **Ana-cold front** (active): air rises up the frontal surface. Deep Ns/Cb, a broad band of heavy precipitation extending behind the surface front, slower moving.
  - **Kata-cold front** (inactive): subsiding air aloft caps the front; shallow cloud, drizzle, a narrow band. Common in decaying systems.
- **Cloud**: Cb and TCu in a narrow band, often organised into a line; Ac/As behind.
- **Precipitation**: heavy showers, hail, thunder. Duration typically 1–2 hours.
- **Wind**: sharp **veer** on passage (NH), often with a squall.
- **Pressure**: falls to the front, then rises sharply.
- **Temperature and dewpoint**: fall sharply.
- **Visibility**: excellent behind, except in showers.
- **Turbulence**: moderate to severe. Low-level windshear at the surface front is a routine approach hazard.
- **Pre-frontal squall line**: can form 50–200 nm ahead of a fast-moving cold front where the upper cold air overruns and destabilises a potentially unstable warm sector. Frequently more violent than the front itself.

### 3.3 Occluded front

When the faster cold front overtakes the warm front, the warm sector is lifted clear of the surface.

- **Cold occlusion**: the air behind the cold front is *colder* than the air ahead of the warm front, so it undercuts both. Cold-front-like weather; more common over continents in winter... in the classical Norwegian description, over Europe and North America the *warm* occlusion is the more common maritime type.
- **Warm occlusion**: the air behind is *warmer* than the air ahead, so it rides up over the pre-existing cold air. Warm-front-like weather with an upper cold front aloft.
- **Weather**: a mixture — extensive layer cloud, prolonged precipitation, embedded convection, and the **triple point** (where the occlusion, warm and cold fronts meet) is often the most active area and the seed of a secondary depression.
- On a chart, the occlusion is the alternating red semicircles and blue triangles on the same side.

### 3.4 Stationary front

Neither air mass advancing; the isobars run parallel to the front. Symbol: blue triangles one side, red semicircles the other. Weather resembles a warm front and **persists for days** — the classic cause of a multi-day low-ceiling/low-visibility spell. Frontal waves develop preferentially along stationary fronts.

### 3.5 Cross-sections at a glance

```
WARM FRONT (moving right to left across you)         COLD FRONT (moving left to right)
      Ci    Cs    As      Ns                                     Cb
   ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ 1:150                            /| 1:60
  warm air overrunning ------->                        warm air  / | <---- cold air
 --------------------------------                    ----------/--+---------
        cold air wedge                                   cold air wedge advancing

```

## 4. Frontal wave development and the depression life cycle

### 4.1 The Norwegian (Bjerknes) polar-front model

Developed in Bergen 1917–1923; still the model behind the symbols on every surface chart.

1. **Initial (stationary front)** — cold air to the north, warm to the south, sharp θw gradient, wind shear across the front.
2. **Frontal wave** — a small perturbation forms; a kink appears in the isobars; pressure begins to fall at the wave apex. The wave moves along the front at roughly the speed of the geostrophic wind in the warm sector.
3. **Open wave / mature depression** — a distinct warm sector with a warm front ahead and a cold front behind, central pressure falling, cloud shield in the classic comma shape. Deepening rate of 1 hPa/h is normal; **>1 hPa/h sustained for 24 h at 60° latitude defines "explosive cyclogenesis" — a bomb.**
4. **Occlusion** — the cold front catches the warm front from the centre outward; the warm sector is pinched off aloft; the low reaches its **maximum depth at or shortly after the start of occlusion**, then fills.
5. **Decay** — the system becomes a cold vortex with wrapped-up frontal remnants; a **secondary depression** often forms at the triple point or on the trailing cold front, and the family repeats. Depressions travel in **families** of three to five along the polar front, each further south than the last, until a cold outbreak ends the sequence.

### 4.2 The conveyor-belt model

The modern refinement, which explains the satellite picture better than the Norwegian model does. Three airstreams, each defined in a frame moving with the system:

- **Warm conveyor belt (WCB)** — the principal ascending airstream. Originates in the warm sector boundary layer ahead of the cold front, flows poleward, rises up the warm frontal surface from ~900 hPa to ~300 hPa, and turns anticyclonically to become the leading cirrus shield. This is where almost all the depression's precipitation comes from and where the frontal cloud head is generated. An **anticyclonically-turning WCB** gives the classic broad warm-front cloud; a **cyclonically-turning WCB** wraps back into the low centre and gives the "cloud head".
- **Cold conveyor belt (CCB)** — flows *westward* relative to the ground beneath the warm front, ascends slowly, and wraps around the low centre. It produces the low cloud and precipitation north of the warm front and it is the airstream that ends up in the **sting jet** in explosively deepening systems.
- **Dry intrusion** — descending stratospheric air behind the cold front, high potential vorticity, very low humidity. On the **water vapour channel it is the dark slot** that spirals into the low. Where the dry intrusion overruns the WCB you get potential instability and the pre-frontal squall line. It is also the CAT/tropopause-fold region.

**Sting jet**: in a rapidly deepening cyclone, a descending mesoscale jet at the tip of the wrapped cloud head can bring 80–100 kt to the surface over a small area (the 1987 Great Storm, Cyclone Ulli). Operationally: the strongest surface winds in a bomb are not necessarily at the cold front.

### 4.3 Cyclogenesis — what actually makes a low deepen

Surface pressure falls where there is **net upper-level divergence** above. The sources:

1. **Baroclinic instability** — the fundamental mechanism: a horizontal temperature gradient with vertical shear releases available potential energy through tilted, growing waves. Eady and Charney growth rates give an e-folding time of ~1–2 days and a preferred wavelength of ~4000 km, which is exactly the observed spacing of Atlantic depressions.
2. **Jet streak dynamics** — left-exit / right-entrance divergence.
3. **Positive vorticity advection (PVA)** ahead of an upper trough.
4. **Upper-level PV anomaly** ("PV thinking") — a stratospheric intrusion approaching a low-level baroclinic zone induces cyclonic circulation beneath it; if the phasing is right the two lock and mutually amplify. This is the modern diagnosis a forecaster actually uses.
5. **Diabatic heating** — latent heat release in the WCB, which is why the deepest bombs happen over warm western boundary currents (Gulf Stream, Kuroshio) in winter. **East-coast cyclogenesis off Cape Hatteras and off Japan is the reason winter North Atlantic and North Pacific crossings are what they are.**
6. **Orographic lee cyclogenesis** — Genoa lows in the lee of the Alps, Colorado lows east of the Rockies, and lee lows east of the Andes.

Anticyclogenesis is the mirror: subsidence, upper convergence, warming aloft, drying, inversion formation.

### 4.4 Anticyclones

| Type | Structure | Weather |
|---|---|---|
| **Cold (thermal) anticyclone** | Shallow, cold-cored, decreasing with height, over cold continents in winter (Siberian High) | Intense surface inversion, fog and freezing fog, very poor visibility, trapped pollution, extreme cold |
| **Warm (dynamic) anticyclone** | Deep, warm-cored, increases with height, subtropical belt and blocking highs | Subsidence inversion, haze layer with a sharp top, fine and stable, poor low-level visibility, excellent above |
| **Blocking high** | Warm, quasi-stationary, deflects the westerlies; often paired with a cut-off low to the south (omega block, Rex block) | Persistent weather regime for 5–15 days; a forecast headache and a fuel-planning one |

Gradient wind in an anticyclone is *stronger* than geostrophic for the same pressure gradient, but anticyclonic pressure gradients are usually weak, so the winds are light. In a depression, gradient wind is *weaker* than geostrophic — and there is a theoretical maximum pressure gradient beyond which no balanced flow exists, which is why very tight lows spin up rather than simply gradient-balance.

## 5. Regional regimes

### 5.1 North Atlantic

Winter (Nov–Mar) is the operational challenge. The Icelandic Low and Azores High set up a strong pressure gradient; the polar jet runs at 130–200 kt with cores over 200 kt not rare; depressions form off Cape Hatteras and track NE, deepening explosively over the Gulf Stream. Consequences:

- **NAT OTS** shifts far south for westbound tracks and far north for eastbound; the daily eastbound track set is published around 2200 UTC for a 30°W crossing between 1130 and 1900 UTC, westbound around 1400 UTC for 0100–0800 UTC crossings, normally FL340–FL400.
- CAT on the poleward side of the jet, frequently moderate, occasionally severe.
- Greenland tip jets and barrier winds; mountain wave turbulence over southern Greenland and Iceland.
- Winter LVP at northwest European airports (frontal fog, advection fog, radiation fog under blocking highs).
- Summer: jet weaker and further north, tracks more direct, and the hazard shifts to the tail of the North Atlantic hurricane season recurving storms into the shipping/track area (Sept–Oct).

### 5.2 North Pacific

Similar dynamics, longer over-water legs, and a stronger jet. The **Aleutian Low** in winter is one of the deepest semi-permanent features on Earth. Cyclogenesis off Japan over the Kuroshio; the jet across Japan regularly exceeds 200 kt in January and is the strongest routinely encountered by commercial aviation. Anchorage/Alaska adds volcanic ash (the Aleutian arc, Redoubt, Cleveland, Bogoslof) and severe icing. The **PACOTS** track system is the Pacific analogue of the NAT OTS. Typhoon recurvature (Jun–Nov, peak Aug–Sep) affects Japan/Korea terminal operations.

### 5.3 Europe

A battleground between mP, cP, mT and cT air. Winter: Atlantic westerly regimes alternating with blocking; the blocked regimes produce persistent freezing fog and low stratus over central Europe (the "Hochnebel"). Spring/autumn: frontal passages with embedded convection. Summer: continental heating gives afternoon/evening thunderstorm clusters over the Alps and central Europe, and MCSs that move northwest overnight; Genoa lows and Vb-tracks give Alpine flooding. Mediterranean: the **mistral, bora, tramontana, sirocco** are all channelled or downslope winds with severe turbulence signatures; autumn cut-off lows over Iberia and Italy produce extreme rainfall and occasional **medicanes** (Mediterranean tropical-like cyclones).

### 5.4 The Middle East and the Arabian Gulf — the home regime

This is the environment a Doha-based crew lives in, so it gets the detail.

**Structure.** The Gulf sits under the descending branch of the Hadley cell, between the Zagros Mountains to the NE and the Arabian shield to the SW. The dominant flow is the northwesterly **shamal** channelled down the Gulf axis.

**Winter (Nov–Mar).**
- Mid-latitude troughs penetrate far enough south to bring frontal cloud and rain; most of Qatar's ~75 mm annual rainfall falls Dec–Mar.
- **Winter shamal**: generated by a strong cold front crossing the mountains of Iran and the strengthening of the Arabian high behind it, with a deep trough east of the Gulf. Occurs **2–3 times a month December to February**; a strong event lasts **3–5 days** and happens once or twice a winter. Northwesterlies 25–40 kt, dust to several thousand feet, visibility down to 1000–3000 m, occasionally below. Winter shamals are more frequent near Lavan Island, Halul Island and Ras Rakan.
- **Fog**: the Gulf's most under-rated hazard. Cold, clear, calm nights over a warm sea and moist desert surface produce **radiation and advection fog at OTHH, OMDB, OTBD, OBBI, OKKK** typically 0200–0900 local, October to March, with visibility collapsing from 10 km to below 200 m in twenty minutes. Winter Gulf fog is the reason LVP capability and a robust alternate policy matter more here than the climate summary suggests.
- Occasional thunderstorms with hail on active troughs.

**Spring (Apr–May).** Transition. Convective outbreaks along the Zagros and over Saudi Arabia; **haboobs** from thunderstorm outflow; the highest frequency of blowing dust with convective triggers. Temperatures already at 40 °C+ by May (Doha May mean maximum **39.4 °C**).

**Summer (Jun–Sep).**
- **Summer shamal** — the "**Barih al-Sarayat**"/*forty-day* shamal — a persistent northwesterly driven by the thermal low over Iraq/Iran interacting with the Arabian high, running from roughly early June into July, with a secondary pulse later. Weaker gradient than the winter shamal but far more persistent; it lifts dust continuously and can hold visibility at 3000–5000 m in haze for weeks.
- **Extreme heat**: Doha July mean maximum **42.4 °C**, record **50.4 °C**; June mean maximum 42.2 °C with record 49.1 °C. See `01` and `06` for the performance consequences.
- **Very high dewpoints on the coast** — sea-surface temperature in the Gulf exceeds 33 °C in August, so dewpoints of 25–30 °C occur with air temperature in the high 30s. Wet-bulb temperatures approach human survival limits.
- Almost no rain; occasional convection over the Hajar Mountains of Oman and the Asir of Saudi Arabia.
- **Convective dust storms (haboobs)** — outflow from Zagros or Saudi convection races across the desert as a density current, arriving with a wall of dust up to **2000 m deep and 100 km wide**, moving at up to **70 km/h**, with visibility going to near zero in minutes and a 30–50 kt wind shift. These are the events that shut OMDB/OTHH without much warning and they are poorly predicted by global models.

**Autumn (Oct–Nov).** Rapid cooling, the start of the fog season, and occasional tropical systems from the Arabian Sea (see `07`).

### 5.5 South Asia and the monsoon

The **southwest (summer) monsoon** is the largest seasonal circulation reversal on Earth. Differential heating between the Asian landmass and the Indian Ocean, amplified by the Tibetan Plateau acting as an elevated heat source, reverses the low-level flow to southwesterly and pulls the ITCZ (as the **monsoon trough**) far north over India.

- **Onset** near Thiruvananthapuram in **early June**; progression northward; **covers the whole country by mid-July**.
- **Withdrawal** begins **early September**, complete by **early October**.
- The **northeast (winter) monsoon** bursts around **20 October** and lasts about **50 days**, bringing rain to Tamil Nadu, coastal Andhra Pradesh and Sri Lanka.
- **Aviation impact**: monsoon depressions tracking WNW from the Bay of Bengal, embedded CB in continuous cloud, thunderstorms at almost every Indian aerodrome in the season, heavy rain reducing visibility to 500 m or less, contaminated runways at airports whose drainage was designed to a different century, and a **tropical easterly jet** aloft at ~FL450 over southern India in summer. The monsoon onset and withdrawal dates published by the India Meteorological Department are a planning input for the whole DOH–India network.
- **Break monsoon** periods with the trough displaced north give a hiatus of fine weather over central India and torrential rain along the Himalayan foothills.
- The **Somali (Findlater) jet** — a low-level southwesterly jet at ~1–1.5 km off the East African coast reaching 40–50 kt — feeds the monsoon and is a low-level shear consideration for East African coastal operations.

### 5.6 Southeast Asia

Two monsoons (NE Nov–Mar, SW May–Sep) plus a very warm ocean means deep convection is available year-round. The **Maritime Continent** has the highest lightning density on Earth. Sumatra squalls (nocturnal squall lines crossing the Malacca Strait, Apr–Nov), the diurnal land/sea breeze convergence over Java and Borneo, and the northwest Pacific typhoon track make this a region where the tactical radar work never stops. Volcanic ash is a constant background risk — Indonesia and the Philippines have more frequently active volcanoes than anywhere else (`07`).

### 5.7 Africa

- **North Africa**: Saharan heat low in summer; **Harmattan** from **late November to mid-March**, a dry, dusty NE trade off the Sahara carrying **0.5–10 µm** dust into West Africa, producing haze that can hold visibility at 1000–3000 m for days and which costs airlines millions annually in cancellations and diversions. Saharan dust outbreaks cross the Atlantic as the **Saharan Air Layer**, which also suppresses Atlantic hurricanes in June–July.
- **West Africa**: the ITCZ and the associated **African Easterly Jet** at ~600 hPa; **African easterly waves** propagate west every 3–5 days and are the seeds of most Atlantic hurricanes. **Mesoscale convective systems** over the Sahel are among the most intense on Earth by cloud-top temperature; squall lines cross Nigeria/Niger with 50+ kt gust fronts.
- **East Africa**: two rainy seasons (long rains Mar–May, short rains Oct–Dec) as the ITCZ crosses twice; high-elevation aerodromes (Addis Ababa 7 625 ft, Nairobi 5 330 ft) combine density altitude with afternoon convection.
- **Southern Africa**: a summer-rainfall regime (Oct–Apr) driven by the ITCZ/Congo air boundary and easterly waves, with intense afternoon and evening thunderstorms over the Highveld and northern Namibia/Botswana; a winter dry season with clear, stable, subsident conditions and coastal fog on the Atlantic side. The Benguela upwelling produces persistent advection fog and stratus along the Namibian coast — Walvis Bay and Swakopmund are fog-prone year round with a maximum in winter. **Northern Namibia (Ohangwena/Okongo) receives ~590 mm/yr at Eenhana, essentially all of it between November and April, in convective storms** (cross-reference `namibia.climate`). Cut-off lows bring the most damaging South African rainfall events, mainly in the transition seasons.

### 5.8 Australia

Subtropical ridge across the continent, moving north in winter and south in summer. Winter: cold fronts crossing the Bight into the southeast, strong westerlies, mountain wave over the Great Dividing Range and Tasmania. Summer: the monsoon trough over the north with tropical cyclones (Nov–Apr), and the "**Top End**" build-up with spectacular convection including the **Morning Glory** roll cloud over the Gulf of Carpentaria (Sep–Nov) — a solitary wave with a severe turbulence signature. Southeast Australia summer heat and the associated bushfire smoke create their own operational problem (pyrocumulonimbus, visibility, engine ingestion).

### 5.9 South America

- The **ITCZ over the Atlantic** must be crossed on every Europe/Gulf–southern South America sector, typically 2–10°N, worst boreal summer and autumn.
- The **Amazon** provides deep convection with tops to FL550 and near-daily MCSs.
- The **Andes** are the world's most consistent mountain-wave generator: a north–south barrier over 6000 m across the mid-latitude westerlies, producing severe mountain wave turbulence and rotor over Mendoza/Santiago and lee waves detectable a thousand kilometres downstream.
- **Argentine mesoscale convective complexes** east of the Andes are among the deepest and most hail-productive storms measured anywhere, with satellite-derived cloud tops and updraft signatures exceeding the US Great Plains.
- **Pampero** — a cold front surge from the southwest across Argentina/Uruguay with a squall.
- **Zonda** — the Andean föhn, a hot dry downslope wind on the Argentine side, with severe turbulence.
