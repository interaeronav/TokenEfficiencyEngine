# Seasonal climatology for long-haul route planning

**Summary.** Climatology does not tell you what tomorrow's weather is. It tells you what to *expect* to have to plan for, which season is expensive, and where the recurring problems live. This file is organised as a route planner's reference: the major long-haul corridors, the Gulf home base month by month, the tropical cyclone basins with their seasons, the mountain wave regions, and the volcanic hazard along the routes.

## 1. The North Atlantic

### 1.1 Winter (November–March)

The defining feature. A strong meridional temperature gradient drives the polar jet to 130–200 kt, with cores over 200 kt several times a season. Cyclogenesis off Cape Hatteras over the Gulf Stream, deepening explosively as it crosses.

**Planning consequences:**
- **Eastbound**: the OTS is drawn to exploit the jet; a Europe-bound flight from North America can gain 60–90 minutes. Tracks sit further south than the great-circle when the jet does.
- **Westbound**: the OTS is drawn to *avoid* the jet, often far north via Greenland/Iceland or far south via the Azores. Westbound North Atlantic sectors in January are the classic case of a route that is 200 nm longer and 40 minutes shorter.
- **CAT** on the cold side of the jet, frequent moderate, occasional severe. Ride reports on the NAT are the primary tool.
- **Turbulence-driven level restrictions** interact with the OTS: you cannot simply change level on a track, so request early.
- **Icing and LVP** at European destinations; frontal fog, advection fog and radiation fog under blocking highs.
- **Greenland and Iceland**: tip jets, barrier winds, and severe mountain wave. Keflavík and Sondrestrom/Kangerlussuaq as ETOPS alternates are frequently weather-limited.
- **Space-based ADS-B** (since March 2019) makes tactical deviations easier to obtain than they were.

### 1.2 Summer (June–September)

Jet weaker and further north, tracks more direct, minima less of an issue. The hazard shifts to:
- **Atlantic hurricane season (1 June – 30 November, peak ~10 September)** — recurving storms can affect the Caribbean, the US eastern seaboard, Bermuda, the Azores, and occasionally western Europe as extratropical remnants. Average season: **14 named storms, 7 hurricanes**.
- **Convection over North America and Europe** at the ends of the sector.
- **Saharan Air Layer** suppressing early-season Atlantic development but adding dust to the tropical Atlantic.

## 2. The Pacific

**North Pacific winter** is the North Atlantic's stronger sibling: the Aleutian Low is among the deepest semi-permanent features on Earth, cyclogenesis over the Kuroshio off Japan is prolific, and the jet over Japan routinely exceeds 200 kt in January. **PACOTS** is the track system. Long over-water legs mean ETOPS alternates in the Aleutians and the Kuriles, which are themselves weather-marginal, icing-prone and — in the Aleutian arc — volcanically active.

**Typhoon season** in the western North Pacific runs effectively year-round with a **minimum in February–March** and a peak in **August–September**; **25.7 tropical cyclones** per year on average. Recurving typhoons affect Japan, Korea and Taiwan terminal operations, and the outflow of a recurving typhoon can strengthen the jet dramatically for the following days.

**Trans-Pacific southern routes** (to Australia/New Zealand) cross the South Pacific Convergence Zone and the South Pacific cyclone basin (austral summer). **The Southern Ocean route** to New Zealand meets the strongest sustained westerlies on the planet.

## 3. The Arabian Gulf, month by month

The home climatology for a Doha-based operation. Doha's normals (1992–2021) frame it: **July mean maximum 42.4 °C, record 50.4 °C; January mean maximum 22.6 °C**; annual rainfall about **75 mm**, essentially all between November and April.

| Month | Dominant regime | Primary operational issue |
|---|---|---|
| **January** | Winter troughs; frequent winter shamals; longest nights | **Fog** (radiation/advection, typically 0200–0900 local), LVP, alternate availability. Occasional TS with hail on active troughs |
| **February** | As January; shamal frequency still high (2–3/month Dec–Feb) | Fog; blowing dust behind fronts |
| **March** | Transition; increasing convection | Convective dust (haboob) begins; TS; still some fog |
| **April** | Warming rapidly; unstable | **Haboobs**, TS, blowing dust; first hot-day performance considerations. North Indian Ocean cyclone peak begins |
| **May** | Mean max 39.4 °C, record 47.7 °C | Heat performance; dust; convection over the Zagros and Oman mountains |
| **June** | Mean max 42.2 °C, record 49.1 °C. **Summer shamal begins** | **Peak performance limitation**; continuous suspended dust reducing visibility to 3000–5000 m for weeks |
| **July** | **Mean max 42.4 °C, record 50.4 °C.** Summer shamal | Maximum density altitude; payload limitation; dust haze |
| **August** | Mean max 41.4 °C, record 48.6 °C; sea surface > 33 °C | Extreme heat plus **very high dewpoints (25–30 °C)** on the coast; occasional Oman-mountain convection |
| **September** | Mean max 39.2 °C | Heat easing; residual dust; **Arabian Sea cyclone risk** for Oman/Yemen routes |
| **October** | Mean max 35.6 °C; **North Indian Ocean cyclone peak (Oct–Nov)** | Fog season begins late month; TC risk toward Oman/Pakistan/India |
| **November** | Mean max 29.7 °C; **Harmattan season starts in West Africa** | Fog; first winter troughs; rain begins |
| **December** | Mean max 24.8 °C; winter shamal season starts | **Fog and LVP**, shortest days, alternates in the same air mass |

**The two recurring planning problems:**

1. **Summer performance.** The July/August departure at high weight is the year's binding constraint. See the worked case in `06`. The mitigation is schedule (night departures), runway selection, and payload management, not clever airmanship.
2. **Winter fog with correlated alternates.** OTHH, OTBD, OMDB, OMAA, OBBI, OKKK can all be below minima on the same winter morning because they share one shallow, moist, radiatively-cooled boundary layer over one basin. The alternate must be outside the air mass — Muscat, Salalah, Riyadh, Kuwait, or further — and that costs fuel. This is the single most consequential piece of local climatology for a Doha crew.

**Arabian Sea tropical cyclones** are a small but real risk to Oman, Yemen, southern Iran and Pakistan operations, with the **double peak in April–May and October–November** characteristic of the North Indian basin. They are individually rare (4–6 storms per year across the whole North Indian basin, most in the Bay of Bengal) but the Arabian Sea has produced several intense landfalling systems in recent decades.

## 4. The Indian subcontinent

- **Southwest monsoon**: onset near **Thiruvananthapuram in early June**, covering the whole country by **mid-July**; **withdrawal from early September, complete by early October**. Every Indian destination in the network changes character for four months: embedded CB in continuous cloud, visibility in heavy rain below 1000 m, contaminated runways, and thunderstorm-driven holding.
- **Pre-monsoon (April–May)** is the season of the **Nor'westers/Kalbaisakhi** over eastern India and Bangladesh — violent, short-lived squall lines with hail and 60+ kt gusts, and the most dangerous convection of the Indian year for aviation. Also the season of extreme surface heat and the associated performance penalties at Delhi, Ahmedabad and Jaipur.
- **Northeast monsoon** bursts around **20 October** for ~**50 days**, bringing the rainy season to Chennai and the southeast coast.
- **Winter (Dec–Feb)**: **the Indo-Gangetic Plain fog season** — a persistent, deep, multi-day radiation fog under a stable inversion, aggravated by aerosol, that closes Delhi and much of northern India repeatedly. Delhi is a CAT IIIB aerodrome for exactly this reason, and crew CAT III currency is an operational necessity on the route, not a nicety.
- **Western disturbances** — mid-latitude troughs that reach northern India in winter, bringing rain, snow in the mountains, and the wind shifts that break the fog.
- **Tropical easterly jet** over southern India in summer at around FL450.

## 5. The ITCZ crossings

**Africa routes (Gulf/Europe to southern Africa).** The ITCZ position over Africa swings from roughly **20°N in July to 15°S in January**. Southbound in the austral summer you cross it over central Africa in its most active state; in the boreal summer you cross it over the Sahel where the MCSs are the most intense on Earth by cloud-top temperature. Practical points: cross at night with the radar worked properly, expect deviations of 50–150 nm, fuel for them, and remember that **surveillance and communication coverage over central Africa is poor**, so a large deviation is a coordination problem as well as a flying one.

**South America routes.** The Atlantic ITCZ sits typically **2–10°N**, worst in the boreal summer and autumn. The crossing on Europe/Gulf–Brazil/Argentina sectors is routine, nocturnal, and is where **AF447** was lost. Beyond it, the Amazon provides near-daily deep convection, and the Argentine plains provide some of the most intense mesoscale convective complexes measured anywhere.

**Indian Ocean routes.** The ITCZ/monsoon trough over the Indian Ocean, plus the SW Indian Ocean cyclone basin in the austral summer, affects Gulf–Indian Ocean island and Gulf–southern Africa sectors.

## 6. Southern Africa

- **Summer (October–April)** is the thunderstorm season across the Highveld, Botswana, Zimbabwe and northern Namibia — near-daily afternoon and evening convection, driven by the ITCZ/Congo Air Boundary and easterly waves, with tops to FL450 and severe hail over the Highveld. Johannesburg's high elevation adds density altitude to the convective picture.
- **Winter (May–September)** is dry, stable and subsident inland, with excellent visibility and smooth conditions, and **coastal fog and stratus on the Atlantic side** from the Benguela upwelling (Walvis Bay and the whole Namib coast).
- **Cut-off lows** produce the most damaging South African rainfall events, mainly in the transition seasons (April–May and September–October).
- **Northern Namibia (Ohangwena/Okongo/Eenhana)** receives about **590 mm/yr at Eenhana**, essentially all of it in convective storms between **November and April**, with the rainiest months January–March. The dry season is completely dry. Ondangwa's records show a daily temperature range of 18–20 °C in the dry season narrowing to ~12 °C in the peak rain months. See `namibia.climate` for the full station profile, which is the reference for any operation into Ondangwa, Eenhana or Ohangwena.
- **Regional aerodromes** in southern Africa often have limited approach aids, no radar, and no alternate within useful range — so the summer convective season is a fuel and planning problem out of proportion to the size of the aircraft involved.

## 7. West Africa

- **Harmattan (late November – mid-March)** — the dry, dusty NE trade off the Sahara. Dust of **0.5–10 µm** produces haze that can hold visibility at 1000–3000 m across a huge area for days, sometimes blocking the sun. It costs airlines millions annually in cancellations and diversions, and it is a genuine problem for Lagos, Abuja, Kano, Accra, Ouagadougou, Bamako and Dakar. It is **not** a SIGMET-worthy dust storm — it is a persistent, widespread visibility reduction that a TAF handles poorly.
- **Monsoon season (May–October)** — the ITCZ over the Sahel, with the **African Easterly Jet** and **African easterly waves** every 3–5 days. Squall lines with 50+ kt gust fronts and dust ahead of them. Nocturnal MCSs. These waves become Atlantic hurricanes downstream.

## 8. Mountain wave regions

| Region | Character |
|---|---|
| **The Andes** | The most reliable severe mountain wave on Earth: a 6000 m+ north–south barrier across the mid-latitude westerlies. Severe wave and rotor at Mendoza and Santiago; waves detectable a thousand kilometres downstream; the **Zonda** föhn on the Argentine side. Stratospheric wave flight records are set here |
| **The Rockies** | Colorado Front Range downslope windstorms (Boulder), Sierra Nevada waves, and severe wave turbulence on transcontinental US routes in winter |
| **Greenland** | Both coasts; tip jets off Cape Farewell; barrier winds; the **piteraq** katabatic outflow. Directly relevant to NAT westbound northern routings and to Kangerlussuaq/Narsarsuaq as alternates |
| **Iceland and Scandinavia** | Wave and rotor at Keflavík and in the Norwegian fjord aerodromes |
| **The Alps** | Föhn on both sides, severe turbulence around Innsbruck, Zurich, Geneva, Milan; a routine consideration on Europe sectors |
| **The Himalaya and Karakoram** | The world's highest terrain under a strong subtropical jet in winter; severe wave and CAT on Gulf/Europe–East Asia routes overflying northern India, Nepal and western China. Also a drift-down and depressurisation problem: MEA/MORA are extreme and the escape routes are long |
| **The Zagros and Elburz** | Directly north and northeast of the Gulf; wave turbulence on eastbound Doha departures and on Iran overflights, and the generator of the winter shamal |
| **Japan (Mt Fuji), Taiwan, Korea** | Severe wave in the winter jet; **BOAC 911 broke up in Fuji wave turbulence in 1966** |
| **New Zealand Southern Alps, Tasmania** | Strong wave in the roaring forties |
| **The Hajar (Oman) and the Asir (Saudi Arabia)** | Regional wave and convection near Muscat and Abha |

**Planning rules**: expect the SIGWX mountain-wave symbol to be real; expect altitude excursions at cruise near and downstream of these ranges when the flow is strong and perpendicular; expect the lee-side approach at a mountain aerodrome to be the turbulent one; and remember that the **minimum safe altitudes in these regions require cold-temperature correction** (`01`) in winter, which is exactly when the wave is strongest.

## 9. Volcanic regions along the major routes

| Region | Relevance |
|---|---|
| **Iceland** | Eyjafjallajökull (2010), Grímsvötn, Katla, Bárðarbunga, and the Reykjanes eruptions from 2021 onward. Directly under the North Atlantic tracks and the European approach airspace — **the highest-consequence volcanic region in the world for aviation by traffic exposure** |
| **Kamchatka and the Kurils** | Shiveluch, Klyuchevskoy, Bezymianny. Under the great-circle Europe/Gulf–Japan/Korea routes and the North Pacific tracks |
| **The Aleutians and Alaska** | Redoubt (**KLM 867, 15 December 1989, all four CF6 engines failed at ~41 000 ft, >US$80 million damage**), Cleveland, Bogoslof, Pavlof, Augustine. Under the North Pacific great-circle routes |
| **Indonesia and the Philippines** | The highest density of frequently active volcanoes on Earth: Merapi, Semeru, Sinabung, Krakatau/Anak Krakatau, Agung, Raung, **Galunggung (BA 9, 24 June 1982, all four RB211s failed)**, Taal, Mayon, Pinatubo. Directly under the Gulf–Southeast Asia/Australia routes |
| **Japan** | Sakurajima (near-continuous), Aso, Shinmoedake, Fuji (dormant) |
| **Papua New Guinea and Vanuatu** | Ulawun, Manam, Tavurvur, Ambae — under Australia/Pacific routes |
| **The Andes** | Puyehue-Cordón Caulle (2011 — the ash circled the southern hemisphere and disrupted Australia and New Zealand), Calbuco, Villarrica, Copahue, Sabancaya, Ubinas. Under South American and trans-Pacific southern routes |
| **Central America and Mexico** | Popocatépetl (chronic, directly affecting Mexico City), Fuego, Santa María, Turrialba |
| **East African Rift** | Nyiragongo, Nyamuragira, Ol Doinyo Lengai, Erta Ale — under the Gulf–southern Africa corridor |
| **Italy and the Mediterranean** | Etna and Stromboli — recurrent short-notice closures of Catania and disruption to Sicilian and southern Italian airspace |
| **Hawaii** | Kīlauea, Mauna Loa; and volcanic smog (vog) affecting island operations |

**Planning**: the VAAC advisories and volcanic ash SIGMETs are the operational products; the underlying climatology tells you which corridors need a contingency plan. On the Doha network, **Indonesia, Kamchatka and Iceland** are the three that most often produce a route change.

## 10. Building a route-planning mental model

The synthesis, by season, for a Gulf-based long-haul operation:

**Northern winter (Dec–Feb).** Home base: fog, LVP, correlated alternates, winter shamal. Europe/North America: strong jet, CAT, LVP, de-icing, contaminated runways. East Asia: very strong subtropical and polar jets, Japanese cyclogenesis. India: Indo-Gangetic fog. Southern Africa/South America/Australia: convective season, ITCZ active in the southern hemisphere, southern-hemisphere cyclone season.

**Northern spring (Mar–May).** Home base: haboobs and convective dust, heat building. India: pre-monsoon Nor'westers and extreme heat. North Indian Ocean: first cyclone peak. Europe: transition, frontal convection.

**Northern summer (Jun–Aug).** Home base: maximum heat and performance limitation, summer shamal, continuous dust haze. India/Southeast Asia: monsoon. Atlantic: hurricane season building. West Pacific: typhoon peak. Africa: Sahel MCSs, ITCZ far north. North Atlantic/Pacific: benign tracks.

**Northern autumn (Sep–Nov).** Home base: fog season begins, heat eases. Atlantic: hurricane peak (~10 September) and recurvature. North Indian: second cyclone peak. West Africa: Harmattan begins. India: monsoon withdrawal then NE monsoon burst.
