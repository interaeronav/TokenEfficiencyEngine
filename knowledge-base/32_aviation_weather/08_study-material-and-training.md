# Study material, training resources and a self-study plan

**Summary.** There is more free, high-quality aviation meteorology material available now than at any time in the history of the profession, and most pilots use almost none of it. This file catalogues the reading in three tiers — exam texts, professional/academic texts, and the free official corpus — then the online tools, then a structured plan that takes a working line pilot from "passed 050 once" to genuinely fluent in about six months of modest effort.

## 1. ATPL theory books

These are exam books. They are optimised for the question bank, they are shallow on physics, and they contain occasional errors that survive because the errors match the questions. Use one as a spine and then read something better.

| Book | Character |
|---|---|
| **CAE Oxford Aviation Academy / Oxford ATPL Ground Training Series — Meteorology volume** | The traditional European standard. Comprehensive, dry, well-diagrammed, strong on the classical synoptic material and the code groups. The best single spine for a European-syllabus exam. Formerly "Jeppesen/Oxford Aviation Training", now published under the CAE Oxford imprint |
| **Jeppesen EASA ATPL Meteorology** | Cleaner layout, better colour graphics, slightly lighter treatment. Good if you find Oxford heavy going |
| **Padpilot ATPL Meteorology** | Digital-native (iBooks/Apple Books), genuinely well designed, with animations and embedded self-tests. The most pleasant to read of the exam books, and the best for revision on a layover |
| **Bristol Groundschool (BGS) notes and question bank** | The distance-learning provider whose notes and question bank many self-study candidates use. The question bank practice is the point; the notes are functional |
| **Rod Machado / Gleim / ASA (US)** | For FAA-syllabus candidates; Gleim's *Aviation Weather and Weather Services* is the standard US self-study consolidation of the FAA material |

**Honest assessment**: none of these books will teach you why the saturated adiabatic lapse rate varies, why the ensemble spread matters, or how to work an airborne radar. They will get you through 050. Treat them as a checklist of topics, not as an education.

## 2. Professional and academic texts

This is where you actually learn meteorology.

| Book | Level | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Wallace & Hobbs, *Atmospheric Science: An Introductory Survey*** (Academic Press) | Undergraduate/graduate | **The standard.** Comprehensive, rigorous but readable, superb on thermodynamics, radiation, cloud microphysics and dynamics. If you buy one serious book, buy this one. Requires calculus but the physical explanations stand alone |
| **Holton (and Hakim), *An Introduction to Dynamic Meteorology*** (Academic Press) | Graduate | The dynamics text: geostrophic and gradient balance, vorticity, quasi-geostrophic theory, baroclinic instability, and the theory behind cyclogenesis and the jet stream. Mathematically demanding. Read it if you want to *understand* why depressions form rather than memorise the Norwegian model |
| **Ahrens, *Meteorology Today*** (Cengage) | Introductory | The best-written introductory survey in the field. No calculus. Excellent diagrams. If Wallace & Hobbs is too much, start here and come back |
| **Lester, *Aviation Weather*** (Jeppesen) | Professional pilot | Written specifically for the aviation audience by a meteorologist; the best bridge between meteorology and the flight deck |
| **Buck, *Weather Flying*** (McGraw-Hill) | Professional pilot, practical | Robert N. Buck's classic, later revised by Robert O. Buck. Not a textbook — a career airline pilot's judgement about weather, written beautifully. Read it for the decision-making, not the physics. Still the single best book on *what to do about* weather |
| **Bluestein, *Synoptic–Dynamic Meteorology in Midlatitudes* (2 vols)** | Graduate | The deep reference on fronts, cyclones and mesoscale structure |
| **Markowski & Richardson, *Mesoscale Meteorology in Midlatitudes*** | Graduate | The modern reference on convection, supercells, squall lines and downbursts. The best single source on the physics behind everything in `03` §1 |
| **Emanuel, *Atmospheric Convection*** and ***Divine Wind*** | Specialist / general | The former is the theory of convection; the latter is a beautifully written general-audience history of hurricanes |
| **Doviak & Zrnić, *Doppler Radar and Weather Observations*** | Specialist | If you want to understand what your radar and the ground radars are actually doing |
| **Kalnay, *Atmospheric Modeling, Data Assimilation and Predictability*** | Graduate | The reference for everything in `05` |
| **Bader et al., *Images in Weather Forecasting*** (Met Office) | Professional forecaster | The classic satellite-interpretation atlas. If you want to read satellite imagery properly, this is the book |

## 3. The free official corpus

This is the material that is authoritative, current, free, and almost universally ignored.

### 3.1 FAA

- **FAA-H-8083-28, Aviation Weather Handbook** — the consolidated handbook. Physics in the first half, products in the second. Free PDF from faa.gov. **Read it cover to cover once.** It is better written than any of the ATPL books and it costs nothing.
- **AC 00-6B, Aviation Weather** and **AC 00-45H, Aviation Weather Services** — the predecessor documents whose content 8083-28 consolidates. Still useful, still cited.
- **AC 91-74, Pilot Guide: Flight in Icing Conditions**; **AC 00-54, Pilot Windshear Guide** — the two best short documents on their subjects in existence. The Windshear Guide is the origin of the escape manoeuvre.
- **FAA Aircraft Ground Deicing pages** — the current **holdover tables** (the 2025-26 set), the general information and regression documents.
- **AC 120-XX series** on low visibility operations, TALPA/runway condition reporting, and turbulence.
- **NTSB accident reports** — free, searchable, and the primary source for `09`.

### 3.2 ICAO

- **Annex 3, Meteorological Service for International Air Navigation** — the specification. Paid, but if your operator has a copy, read Chapters 3–7 and the Appendices with the templates.
- **Doc 8896, Manual of Aeronautical Meteorological Practice** — the practical companion.
- **Doc 9691, Manual on Volcanic Ash, Radioactive Material and Toxic Chemical Clouds**.
- **Doc 9817, Manual on Low-level Wind Shear**.
- **Doc 10064, Aeroplane Performance Manual** (runway condition and performance).
- **Doc 8168 (PANS-OPS)** for the cold-temperature correction table and approach design.
- **Doc 4444 (PANS-ATM)** for the weather deviation procedure and separation.

### 3.3 WMO

- **No. 306, Manual on Codes** — the definitive METAR/SPECI/TAF code definitions. Free.
- **No. 8, CIMO Guide** — instruments and observing methods. Free.
- **No. 49, Technical Regulations Vol II** — the aeronautical volume. Free.
- **WMO training and e-learning portal**.

### 3.4 National services

- **Met Office (UK)** — the "Learn about the weather" and aviation pages, the *Forecasters' Reference Book* material, and their public training resources. WAFC London sits here.
- **NOAA/NWS** — **aviationweather.gov** documentation, the **JetStream** online school (excellent, free, well-illustrated), **SPC** (Storm Prediction Center) mesoanalysis and educational pages, **NHC** (hurricanes), **SWPC** (space weather).
- **EUMETSAT and EUMeTrain** — free satellite interpretation training and case studies; EUMeTrain's satellite manual and event weeks are outstanding and under-used.
- **ECMWF** — open charts, open data (since 2022), the model documentation, and their training courses and lecture notes, many of which are public.
- **Bureau of Meteorology (Australia)** — the *Aviation Weather Services* handbook and their excellent aviation knowledge centre.
- **Environment Canada / NAV CANADA** — the **LAKP (Local Area Knowledge and Practices)** series and the *Aviation Weather* manual (the AWS/MANAIR), which are among the best regional aviation-meteorology documents anywhere.

### 3.5 MetEd / COMET (UCAR)

**The single best free training resource in the field.** COMET (the Cooperative Program for Operational Meteorology, Education and Training) at UCAR publishes hundreds of self-paced modules with quizzes and completion certificates, at levels from introductory to forecaster-professional. Registration is free.

Topic areas relevant to a professional pilot: aviation weather hazards; icing; turbulence; convection and mesoscale meteorology; volcanic ash; dust and sand; ceiling and visibility/fog; mountain weather and downslope winds; satellite interpretation (a very large and very good series); radar meteorology; numerical weather prediction and ensembles; tropical meteorology and tropical cyclones; and dedicated aviation-forecaster courses.

> ⚠️ **Specific MetEd module titles could not be retrieved in this session** — the site requires JavaScript and returned no course list. Rather than guess at titles, search the MetEd catalogue for the topic keywords above; the aviation, satellite and mesoscale sections are the ones to work through. `needs-verification`

## 4. Online tools

| Tool | What it is | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| **aviationweather.gov** | NOAA Aviation Weather Center | The reference implementation of every aviation product: METAR/TAF (raw and decoded), PIREPs, SIGMET/G-AIRMET/CWA, satellite, MRMS radar, GFA graphical forecasts, **flight path tool with cross-sections**, prog charts, extended-range wind/temperature to 4 days. Free, no registration, and the flight path tool's vertical cross-section is the best free way to visualise turbulence and icing along a route |
| **Windy.com** | Multi-model visualiser (ECMWF, GFS, ICON, and high-resolution models) | Quick, beautiful, and genuinely useful for comparing models. The **airport overlay with METAR/TAF, the CAPE/CIN layers, the wind-at-flight-level layers and the satellite/radar overlays** are excellent. Use it for pattern recognition, not for legal planning |
| **Ventusky** | Similar visualiser, different rendering | Better for some fields (precipitation type, wave); a good cross-check against Windy |
| **earth.nullschool.net** | Animated global wind/ocean visualisation | The best intuitive picture of the general circulation ever made. Use it to *see* the jets, the ITCZ, the monsoon and the trade winds. Excellent for teaching yourself the circulation in `02` |
| **Tropical Tidbits** | Model output and tropical cyclone analysis | Fast, comprehensive, model-by-model comparison; the best free source for tropical cyclone model guidance and for looking at raw model fields |
| **University of Wyoming sounding archive** (`weather.uwyo.edu/upperair/sounding.html`) | Global radiosonde archive with plotted skew-T | The place to practise reading soundings. Pull the 00Z sounding for a station near your destination and work the LCL, LFC, CAPE, freezing level and inversion structure by hand |
| **NOAA/NCAR RAP real-time soundings** | Model and observed soundings, interactive | Forecast soundings for any point — the tool a forecaster uses for fog, convection and icing |
| **Ogimet** | Archive of raw METAR/TAF/SYNOP by station and date | **Indispensable for climatology and for post-flight analysis.** Pull every METAR for your destination for the last five Januaries and you will know its fog behaviour better than the TAF does |
| **SPC Mesoanalysis** (US only) | Real-time severe weather parameters | For learning what CAPE, shear, LI and the composite indices look like in a real event |
| **Blitzortung / lightningmaps.org, and commercial GLD360/ENTLN feeds** | Real-time lightning | Convective activity over data-sparse regions |
| **NHC, JTWC, JMA, RSMC La Réunion, BoM** | Tropical cyclone warning centres | Authoritative track and intensity forecasts by basin |
| **VAAC websites** (London, Toulouse, Washington, Anchorage, Darwin, Tokyo, Wellington, Buenos Aires, Montreal) | Volcanic ash advisories and graphics | Live ash situation on your route |
| **NOAA SWPC** | Space weather alerts, the R/S/G scales, aurora and D-region absorption forecasts | Polar and HF operations |
| **ECMWF open charts** | Free ECMWF forecast charts including ensemble products | The best free access to the best model |
| **Your operator's EFB weather app** | The one you actually use | Learn its data source, its update rate, and its limitations. Most are GFS- or WAFS-based; some are ECMWF-based. Knowing which changes how much you trust it |

## 5. A structured self-study plan

Designed for a working line pilot with roughly **3–5 hours a week**, over about **six months**. Each phase pairs reading with a practical habit, because meteorology learned without daily application evaporates.

### Phase 1 — Foundations (weeks 1–4)

**Read:** FAA-H-8083-28 chapters on the atmosphere, temperature, moisture, pressure and altimetry, and stability. Then `01` of this domain.
**Do:**
- Every day, before you leave for the airport, compute the density altitude for your departure and destination from the METAR, by hand. It takes 30 seconds and it builds the number sense that no book gives you.
- Once a week, pull a sounding from the Wyoming archive for a station near a destination you flew, and identify the inversion, the freezing level and the cloud layers.

### Phase 2 — Circulation and synoptics (weeks 5–10)

**Read:** Ahrens or Wallace & Hobbs on the general circulation, air masses and fronts. Then `02`.
**Do:**
- Spend ten minutes a week on **earth.nullschool.net** watching the jet stream and the ITCZ move.
- For every sector you fly, look at the surface analysis and the 250/300 hPa chart before departure and predict the ride. Compare afterwards.
- Learn to read the station model on a plotted surface chart.

### Phase 3 — Hazards (weeks 11–18)

**Read:** `03` of this domain, then the FAA's **AC 00-54 Pilot Windshear Guide** and **AC 91-74 Flight in Icing Conditions**, then the relevant MetEd modules (icing, turbulence, convection, dust, volcanic ash).
**Do:**
- Work the airborne radar deliberately on every convective sector: sweep the tilt, find the tops, find the cores, and afterwards compare what the radar showed with what you flew through.
- Read one NTSB or BEA weather-related accident report per fortnight (start with the list in `09`).
- On any winter sector, watch the de-icing operation and read the holdover table for the actual conditions.

### Phase 4 — Products and interpretation (weeks 19–24)

**Read:** `04`, plus the FAA Aviation Weather Handbook's products half, plus WMO No. 306's METAR/TAF sections for the code detail.
**Do:**
- Decode every METAR and TAF you receive **without** the decoder for a full month. It is tedious for a week and then it is automatic forever.
- Work the EUMeTrain satellite manual and identify, on the water vapour channel, a dry intrusion, a jet axis and a PV anomaly.
- Use the aviationweather.gov **flight path tool** to build a cross-section for a sector you are about to fly, and compare the forecast turbulence and icing with what you actually get.

### Phase 5 — Forecasting and judgement (ongoing)

**Read:** `05`, `06` and `07`; Buck's *Weather Flying*; Kalnay if you want the theory.
**Do:**
- Before each long sector, look at the ensemble meteogram for the destination and decide, from the spread, whether the TAF's `PROB` group is a real threat.
- **Keep a weather log.** One line per sector: what you expected, what you got, what you would decide differently. After a hundred sectors this is the most valuable meteorological document you own, because it is calibrated to your aircraft, your network and your judgement.
- Every six months, pull five years of Ogimet METARs for one of your regular destinations and build its personal climatology for the month you are entering.

### Recurrent and formal training

- **Type-specific**: windshear escape, upset prevention and recovery (UPRT), adverse weather and contaminated runway operations, LVO recurrent and CAT II/III currency, cold weather operations and de-icing.
- **CRM/threat and error management**: weather is the single most common external threat in TEM logs. The decision-making, not the physics, is what fails.
- **Simulator**: ask for a microburst on approach, a contaminated-runway landing at limiting weight, and a hot-and-high engine failure at V1. The simulator is the only place you get to experience these.
- **Dispatcher/flight-operations-officer courses** teach the planning side properly and are worth doing if your operator offers one.

## 6. What to do with all of it

The point of this catalogue is not completeness for its own sake. Three tiers of ambition:

- **Minimum professional standard**: read FAA-H-8083-28 once; decode raw METAR/TAF fluently; know the windshear escape manoeuvre and the volcanic ash procedure cold; work the radar properly; understand the RCAM and cold-temperature correction.
- **Good**: add Lester and Buck; work the relevant MetEd modules; read soundings; use the ensemble; keep the weather log.
- **Excellent**: add Wallace & Hobbs and Markowski & Richardson; read satellite imagery like a forecaster; understand what your model output can and cannot resolve; be the person on the flight deck whose weather judgement the rest of the fleet asks about.
