# Spanish and Mediterranean wall traditions

**Summary.** The Mediterranean wall is not a style but a set of solved problems: how to build with earth and lime where there is no good timber and little water; how to keep a courtyard cool; how to make a thick wall beautiful with almost no material. This file treats the tradition technically — Andalusian *tapia* and its **tapial calicastrado** variant, lime render and *cal* limewash, Moorish and Mudéjar wall systems, the Alhambra's stucco-and-tile grammar, *azulejo* and *zellij*, the *cortijo* and *hacienda* enclosure, Ibiza's vernacular, UNESCO-listed Mediterranean dry stone, tadelakt, and the proportions, colours and finishes that separate an authentic result from a superficial one.

## 1. *Tapia* and *tapial* — Andalusian rammed earth

**The words.** *Tapial* is the timber formwork; *tapia* is the wall of rammed earth made in it. In Spanish America the wall itself became *tapia*. Corominas argues the word is pre-Roman and onomatopoeic — *tap*, the sound of the rammer.

**The technique, precisely.** Two parallel boards, held apart by needles (*agujas*) passing through the wall and wedged, are set on the previous course. Earth is poured in **layers of 10–15 cm** and compacted with a *pisón* until it rings. When the box is full, the form is struck, moved along the wall, and the process repeats; the horizontal joint lines between boxes and the needle holes remain visible and are the signature of the technique. Openings are cut with a chisel after the wall has dried.

**Variants that matter:**
- ***Tapial calicastrado*** (also *tapia calicostrada*) — the outer 30–50 mm of each box is packed with a lime-rich mix so the wall gains a hard, weather-resistant crust integral with the core. **This is how the Alhambra's walls are built**, and it is the correct answer for exposed earth walling. It gives durability without the vapour trap of a cement render.
- ***Tapia de yeso y aljezones*** — Aragonese variant substituting gypsum paste and gypsum stone for earth, giving greater strength and durability. The Andalusi towers around Zaragoza (Alfajarín, Cadrete, Rueda de Jalón) and the Mudéjar churches of the Comunidad de Calatayud are built this way.
- ***Tapia real / tapia valenciana*** — earth with brick or stone quoins and lacing courses built into the boxes, giving the wall corners and openings a hard edge. `needs-verification` on the precise terminology.

**Pliny** recorded that in Hispania and Africa walls were moulded between boards — *parietes formacei* — and, perhaps exaggerating, that they resisted rain, wind and fire for centuries and were "stronger than any cement."

**Contemporary parameters** (from modern rammed-earth practice): lifts 100–250 mm loose compacted to ~50 %; cement-stabilised mixes cure 28 days minimum and reach 5–18 MPa; thickness 150 mm non-loadbearing to 600 mm loadbearing; a 350 mm wall has ~12-hour thermal lag.

**Two rules that decide whether an earth wall survives:**
1. **A stone or concrete plinth.** The 1714 Spanish-American ordinance required foundations of stone to *una vara* (86,6 cm) above natural ground before continuing in adobe or *tapia*. Half a metre is a sensible modern minimum.
2. **A generous coping and overhang.** Earth walls die from the top down. A wide capping — tile, stone, or a lime-rich sacrificial cap — with a real overhang and drip is not decoration.

> ⚠️ Never render an earth wall in cement. Cement render is vapour-impermeable, traps moisture behind it, and the wall rots and blows the render off in sheets. This is the single commonest way a well-built earth wall is destroyed by a well-meaning refurbishment.

## 2. Lime — *cal*, render and limewash

Lime is the Mediterranean binder and the reason the palette is what it is.

**Lime plaster.** Sand, water and lime — usually non-hydraulic hydrated lime (air lime), often with hair for reinforcement and pozzolan to shorten working time. Non-hydraulic lime sets only by **carbonatation**, absorbing CO₂ from the air, and only while kept moist. It will not set under water. Thick coats can stay soft for weeks. Setting is accelerated by using **natural hydraulic lime (NHL)** or adding pozzolan (volcanic ash historically, fly ash now) or gypsum.

Hydraulic limes were historically graded **feeble, moderate and eminent**; modern grades are **NHL 2, NHL 3,5 and NHL 5** newtons — against Portland cement plaster at **25–35 N**, up to ten times harder. That softness is the point:

- Lime plaster is **less brittle and less prone to cracking than cement**, and requires no expansion joints.
- It does not detach under shear from thermal and moisture movement.
- It **shields softer backgrounds** from shear stress instead of transmitting it.
- Practical guidance: **do not replace more than 20 % of the lime with cement** in a facade render, and it is contested whether any cement is appropriate at all.

**Application.** Traditional three-coat work: a coarse *arricio* / render coat, a floating coat, and a fine finish of lime putty (sometimes with powdered marble, the Venetian *marmorino* line of descent from the Roman *marmoratum opus*). Pigment can be added to the wet finish coat.

> ⚠️ Wet non-hydraulic lime has a **pH of 12** and burns skin and eyes. Gloves, goggles, and vinegar or lemon juice on hand for neutralising splashes. Dry lime plaster falls to about pH 8,6.

**Limewash (*cal*, *encalado*).** Pure slaked lime in water, curing by carbonatation into calcite. Two properties define how it behaves:

- **It penetrates rather than adheres.** The wall must be **damped down** before application: not damped, and the lime and pigment stay powdery on the surface; saturated, and there is no surface tension and the wash fails. Damping down is the whole skill.
- **Opacity develops on drying and curing, not on application.** Beginners over-thicken the wash because it looks transparent going on. Apply thin, apply often — four to six thin coats, wet-on-damp, is right.

Traditional additives: water glass, glue, egg white, **salt** (to prevent mould), soap, **milk (casein)**, **flour** (a strength-enhancing binder), molasses, alum, tallow and soil (as pigment). Cement makes a harder-wearing wash but shortens open time drastically and destroys breathability — inadvisable on historic or earth walls.

The refresh cycle is the tradition's other virtue: an annual or biennial re-wash before the feast day is a maintenance ritual, not a defect. A limewashed wall is *meant* to be renewed, which is why its appearance improves as it weathers.

## 3. Moorish and Mudéjar wall treatments

**Moorish (Andalusi) architecture** concentrates decoration on the inside of the building and organises everything around the **courtyard** with water at its centre. The wall grammar is a strict two-part division:

- **Lower walls (dado):** tile mosaic — ***alicatado*** in Spanish, ***zellij*** in Arabic. Single-colour glazed tiles cut into geometric shapes and assembled into geometric patterns. Wet, washable, cool to the touch, and reflective at the level a person occupies.
- **Upper walls:** carved stucco — ***yesería*** — with **vegetal arabesque (*ataurique*)**, **epigraphic**, **geometric** and ***sebka*** (interlacing net) motifs, sculpted three-dimensionally into ***muqarnas*** / ***mocárabes*** where a surface turns into a vault.
- **Ceilings:** carved and painted wood, usually pine.

The functional logic is worth stating because it transfers directly to a hot dry climate: **hard, impervious, washable material where people touch and splash; light, permeable, shaded, deeply modelled material above where only the eye reaches.**

**Mudéjar** is the continuation of these techniques by Muslim craftsmen under Christian rule, and it is the more useful precedent for a modest project because it is a *brick and plaster* tradition rather than a marble one: patterned brickwork (*ladrillo*), *sebka* panels executed in brick relief, glazed ceramic inserts in brick fields, and towers built of *tapia de yeso*. Mudéjar shows how to get richness out of a cheap unit.

## 4. The Alhambra as a wall system

The Alhambra sits on a promontory about **700–740 m long and 200–205 m at its widest**, covering roughly **142 000 m²**, enclosed by a fortified wall with **thirteen defensive towers**. Its name — *al-Qal'ah al-Ḥamrā'*, "the red fortress" — comes from the colour of its walls, which are **rammed earth**; the red is iron oxide in the local clay, and the construction is **tapial calicastrado**, a granular red aggregate held in red clay, layered and reinforced with brick and stone.

The decoration was originally **painted in bright colour** — red, blue and gold as the primaries, with other colours in the background — not the monochrome stone-and-plaster we see now. Inscriptions were painted gold or silver, or white outlined in black, against red, blue or turquoise grounds.

**Design lessons that transfer:**
- The **wall as a page.** José Miguel Puerta Vílchez compares the Alhambra's walls to the pages of a manuscript. Decoration is banded and framed in cartouches, not scattered.
- **Concentration.** The vast majority of wall surface is plain. Richness is placed where it will be seen at close range — around openings, at the dado, at the transition to a vault.
- **Water at the centre of the courtyard**, with the wall as its reflector and shade-giver.
- **Fragile materials, continuous maintenance.** The Alhambra's chronology is hard to establish precisely *because* the materials needed regular repair. Plan for it.

## 5. Azulejo and tilework

**Terminology and technique, in historical order:**

- ***Alicatado / zellij*** (13th c. onward) — tiles glazed a single colour, then **cut** into geometric shapes and assembled into mosaic. Labour-intensive, endlessly variable. Many examples in the Alhambra; continued in Mudéjar work (14th-c. Alcázar of Seville) and to this day in Morocco.
- ***Cuerda seca*** — a greasy manganese line drawn on the tile keeps coloured glazes from bleeding into each other during firing.
- ***Cuenca / arista*** (late 15th–early 16th c., Seville) — a metal or wooden mould is pressed into the unbaked tile, leaving **thin ridges of clay** that keep the glazes apart. More efficient for mass production than *cuerda seca*, and the technique that made Mudéjar-style tiling affordable. Prominent in the early-16th-century **Casa de Pilatos**, Seville. Exported widely into the 17th century.
- ***Maiolica / pisano*** — Italian potters arrived in Seville in the early 16th century; **Francisco Niculoso** settled there in 1498, and figurative polychrome panels became possible.
- **Portugal** — the techniques were introduced by **King Manuel I after a 1503 visit to Seville**; the Arab Room of the **Sintra National Palace** has *cuenca* tiles with the armillary sphere. The Portuguese adopted the Moorish *horror vacui* and covered walls **completely**.

**Practical rules for using tile on a boundary or courtyard wall:**
- Use it at the **dado** (up to 900–1 100 mm), on **bench faces**, in **niches**, around a **fountain or spout**, and as a **frieze band** — not as an all-over field unless you are consciously doing the Portuguese thing.
- Set on a **lime-based bedding** over a lime render if the substrate is earth or lime; cement adhesive over a lime background will fail.
- Leave a **generous, coloured grout joint** (3–5 mm). Machine-perfect 1 mm joints read as modern bathroom tiling and destroy the effect instantly.
- Handmade tiles are dimensionally irregular by 2–3 mm. That irregularity is what makes the light on the surface alive; do not specify a rectified tile and then wonder why it looks flat.

## 6. Courtyard and patio walls

The Andalusian *patio* is a wall-defined room with no roof. Its rules:

- **Proportion.** A patio reads as a room when the wall height is between about **0,7 and 1,2 times** the shorter plan dimension. Lower and it is a yard; higher and it is a shaft. `needs-verification` — this is a design heuristic, not a codified rule.
- **Thickness reads.** Reveals of 300–500 mm at openings are what tell you a wall is massive. A 110 mm wall with a 110 mm reveal will never look Mediterranean regardless of colour.
- **One shaded side, one lit side.** The patio works because the sun crosses it; a fully shaded courtyard is a cellar.
- **Water.** A fountain, a rill, a simple *pila* (basin) with a wall spout. Evaporative cooling plus the sound.
- **Planting against the wall**, not in the middle: citrus, jasmine, bougainvillea, vines. The wall provides the microclimate; the plants provide the humidity.
- **Level change.** One or two steps between the patio and the rooms defines the threshold without a door.

## 7. *Cortijo*, *hacienda* and rural enclosure

The Andalusian **cortijo** is a working farmstead: a group of buildings and walls forming one or more enclosed courtyards, presenting an almost blank face outward and opening entirely inward. The wall vocabulary:

- **Long, low, unbroken runs of white wall** with very few, small openings on the outer face.
- A **single monumental entrance** — an arched *portón* wide enough for a cart, often the only decorated element on the whole exterior, sometimes with a *espadaña* (bell-gable) over it.
- **Blind arcading** or shallow pilasters to break a very long run without windows.
- **Threshing floor, water tank and animal yards** as walled outdoor rooms.
- Colour: white walls with **ochre, sienna, iron-red or Naples-yellow** on the socle, the entrance surround and the openings.

For a rural Namibian plot this is the most directly transferable Mediterranean model in this file: blank outward, rich inward, one great gate, and the enclosure doing the work that a fence would otherwise do badly.

## 8. Ibiza and the Balearics

The Ibicenco vernacular (*casa payesa*) is the most stripped-back Mediterranean wall language and the one contemporary architects borrow from most:

- **Thick rubble-stone walls** rendered and limewashed to a soft, hand-modelled surface — arrises rounded, not sharp; faces subtly undulating.
- **Flat roofs** with a simple parapet, so the wall is the dominant element and there is no visible eaves line.
- **Very few openings**, small and deep, sometimes with a simple projecting hood.
- **Additive geometry** — the building is a cluster of white cubes rather than a composition.
- Terraces retained by **dry stone**, with *sabina* (juniper) beams and canes for pergolas.
- Colour: white, with **indigo blue** or **green** on shutters and doors, and the natural terracotta of pots and tile.

The look is achievable and is frequently faked badly. The difference is entirely in the **radius of the arrises**, the **irregularity of the plane**, and the **absence of trims**. See §12.

## 9. Italian, Greek and UNESCO dry stone

The **art of dry stone walling** was inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in **2018**, jointly for **Croatia, Cyprus, France, Greece, Italy, Slovenia, Spain and Switzerland**; **Ireland** was added in **2024**. In Croatia the *suhozidi* were built to clear stone for crops, mark ownership, and shelter from the *bora* wind; the island of **Baljenac** carries **23 km** of dry stone wall on **14 hectares**.

The Mediterranean forms differ from the British:
- **Terracing** dominates — dry stone retaining walls holding cultivated soil on slopes, from the Cinque Terre to the Cyclades to the Douro.
- **Larger, more irregular stones**, less coursing, more reliance on interlock than on regular beds.
- **Dry stone shelters** — the Apulian *trullo*, the Provençal *borie*, the Spanish *barraca*, the Valencian shelter with its triangular arch of two slabs pitched against each other.

Technique remains the same as file `01` describes: foundation stones bedded on subsoil, a **battered** double face diminishing in width with height, **through-stones** at intervals, hand-placed **hearting** (never poured rubble), and **copes** across the full width. In the Mediterranean the coping is often a line of stones set vertically on edge and slightly overhanging.

## 10. Portuguese and Moroccan influences

**Portuguese:** the *azulejo* wall carried to completeness; *calçada portuguesa* black-and-white stone paving at the wall's foot; and the *taipa* (rammed earth) tradition of the Alentejo, which is close cousin to Andalusian *tapia*.

**Moroccan:** the same Andalusi grammar without the Christian interruption. Two things to take from it:

- ***Zellij*** cut-tile mosaic, still practised, still hand-cut with the *menqach* hammer.
- ***Tadelakt*** — the most useful Moroccan technique for walls in a dry climate.

### Tadelakt, precisely

A waterproof lime plaster used in Moroccan architecture for baths, sinks, water vessels, interior and exterior walls, ceilings, roofs and floors. Composition:

- **Lime plaster — not Portland cement.**
- In some cases **marble or limestone sand**, and no other aggregate.
- **Natural soap**, usually black or olive-oil soap.

The chemistry: the soap reacts with the lime to form **calcium soaps**, which are insoluble in water and fairly hard — the same reaction that leaves scum in a bathtub in hard-water areas, deliberately induced.

The method:
1. Apply the lime plaster as a paste — it can form curves and is seamless, with a soft undulating character.
2. **Before the plaster sets**, compress it with a flat, smooth, hard stone; then polish with a plastic trowel.
3. Polish mechanically with stones or abrasives harder than the plaster.
4. Seal with an **olive-oil soap solution**, traditionally rubbed in with a river stone and treated with oleic acid.

Pigment can be added for any colour; **deep red is traditional**. Finish can be shiny or matte. Tadelakt is labour-intensive but durable — and, importantly for a boundary wall, it is a *lime* finish, so it remains vapour-permeable over an earth or lime substrate.

## 11. Pergolas, arches, niches, benches and integrated planting

The Mediterranean wall is rarely just a wall; it carries things.

- **Pergolas.** Timber or steel beams from the wall to a line of posts, at **2,4–2,7 m** clear height, with rafters at **400–600 mm** centres, giving roughly 50 % shade before planting and 80–90 % once a vine is established. Build a **bearer plate or corbels into the wall** as it goes up — bolting into a finished plastered wall later is both ugly and structurally poor.
- **Arches.** A semicircular arch in a boundary wall needs abutments capable of taking the thrust: a pier at each springing, at least **1,5 × the arch thickness** wide, and a mass of wall above the crown of at least **0,4 m** (the code's minimum masonry height above openings). A segmental arch is flatter and prettier but pushes harder sideways.
- **Niches (*hornacinas*).** A recess **300–450 mm wide × 500–700 mm high × 150–250 mm deep**, arched or square-headed, for a pot, a lamp or a figure. Needs a wall of at least 340 mm to be built into, or a local thickening.
- **Benches (*poyos*).** A masonry bench built off the wall: seat height **420–450 mm**, depth **400–450 mm**, faced in tile or lime plaster with a rounded front arris. The most sociable thing you can add to a courtyard wall.
- **Spouts and basins.** A wall spout at **1 100–1 300 mm** discharging into a *pila* — needs a pipe cast into the wall during construction.
- **Integrated planting.** Built-in planters at the wall foot (min. **450 mm** internal depth and width, with drainage and a waterproof membrane between planter and wall), and pot ledges or pot hooks. Espaliered citrus, fig or vine against a white wall is the classic and works especially well in a hot dry climate because the wall reflects light into the canopy.

## 12. Colour, finish and getting it right rather than superficially right

### The palette

| Role | Colours |
|---|---|
| Field | Lime white; warm off-whites tinted with a trace of yellow ochre or raw sienna |
| Socle / plinth (bottom 600–900 mm) | Ochre, iron oxide red (*almagre*), umber, charcoal |
| Openings, surrounds, quoins | The socle colour, or a stronger version of the field |
| Joinery, shutters, gates | Indigo/Prussian blue, olive/bottle green, oxblood, unpainted weathered timber |
| Accents | Terracotta (pots, tile, coping), glazed tile blue/green/white |
| Never | Bright modern white (titanium dioxide), pastel pinks, grey render, black window frames |

Pigments must be **lime-fast**: natural earth oxides and a small number of mineral pigments only. Organic pigments are destroyed by lime's alkalinity.

### Proportion rules

- **Wall thickness reads at the reveal.** Aim for reveals of **300 mm minimum**, 400–500 mm ideally. If the wall is thin, thicken it locally at every opening.
- **Openings are small and few** on the outward face; large and shaded on the courtyard face.
- **The wall meets the ground without a plinth trim** — the lime render runs down and stops on a slightly battered base or simply meets the paving.
- **The coping is minimal or absent** in the whitewashed idiom; the wall's top is rounded over by the render itself. (This is aesthetically right and technically risky — see the warning below.)
- **Long runs are broken by mass, not by pattern** — a pier, a change of height, a return, a gate.

### Authentic versus superficial

| Superficial | Authentic |
|---|---|
| Acrylic textured render over blockwork, sprayed | Lime render, applied and floated by hand, over masonry or earth |
| Uniform machine texture | Undulating plane, visible tool marks, subtle colour variation |
| Sharp 90° arrises and metal corner beads | Arrises rounded by hand to a **10–25 mm** radius; no beads anywhere |
| Brilliant white acrylic paint | Limewash in 4–6 thin coats, damped down before each |
| One thick coat | Multiple thin coats over weeks |
| Thin walls with 110 mm reveals | Real or locally-built-up thickness with deep reveals |
| Aluminium windows and doors in the boundary | Timber, or steel painted a lime-fast colour |
| Tile applied everywhere at 1 mm joints | Tile concentrated at dado, niche, bench and spout, at 3–5 mm coloured joints |
| Precast "Tuscan" balusters and pilaster mouldings | No mouldings; the interest is in mass, shadow and colour |
| Cement everything | Lime binder throughout, or cement only where structurally necessary and detailed to move |

> ⚠️ The one place where authenticity and durability genuinely conflict is the **coping**. The traditional Mediterranean rendered wall has a rounded lime top and no projecting capping, and it survives because it is re-limewashed every year or two. In a climate with intense sun and driving seasonal rain, and with a client who will not re-wash annually, that detail will fail. Compromise: keep the rounded profile but form it in a **lime-rich, well-compacted cap** over a hidden flexible membrane, with a shallow drip cut into the underside of a **25–40 mm** overhang. Do not import a heavy precast concrete coping and then complain that the wall looks like a suburb.

## 13. Applying this at Okongo

The Mediterranean tradition is a hot-dry-climate tradition, and it transfers to northern Namibia far better than a northern European one does. What is directly usable:

- **Mass and thermal lag** on the west and north boundary of a courtyard.
- **Lime finishes** — vapour-permeable, refreshable, cheap in material, and forgiving of imperfect blockwork. Lime is available in Namibia and South Africa; hydrated builder's lime is already used as a mortar plasticiser.
- **The *cortijo* model** — blank outward, one great gate, rich inward — which resolves the security requirement and the aesthetic requirement in the same move.
- **The patio with water and planting** as the actual living space.
- **Bagged blockwork + limewash** as the cheap, honest route to the finish, in place of expensive lime render on a poor substrate.

What does not transfer: terracotta pantile copings that will not survive hail; deep timber pergolas without termite protection; and any detail relying on annual maintenance that will not happen.
