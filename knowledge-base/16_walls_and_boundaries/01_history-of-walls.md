# A history of walls

**Summary.** Every major wall tradition solved the same four problems — get the stones or units to site, make them stack stably, keep water out of the joint, and stop the whole thing overturning — and each solved them with different technical knowledge. This file reads the history for what was *known*, not for chronology alone: Jericho's coursed undressed stone at c. 8000 BC, Roman facing systems that separated structure from surface, Inca polygonal fitting that made a wall seismically self-correcting, Great Zimbabwe's and Khami's mortarless granite with a batter and a decorative course grammar, English drystone's through-stones and diminishing section, Japanese *ishigaki* with its 45° curved base, and the political wall from Hadrian to Berlin to the contemporary southern African security perimeter.

## 1. Neolithic beginnings — Jericho

The wall and tower at Tell es-Sultan (Jericho) are the earliest monumental enclosure known. Kathleen Kenyon's excavations of 1952–58 established that the tower — 8,5 m tall, roughly 9 m in diameter at the base narrowing to 7 m at the top, with walls about 1,5 m thick and an internal stair of 22 steps — belongs to the Pre-Pottery Neolithic A, around 8000 BC, not to the Late Bronze Age as John Garstang had earlier proposed.

**What they knew:** that a battered (tapering) section is stable without mortar; that undressed stone can be coursed if you grade it by size; and that a wall enclosing a settlement is worth an enormous investment of collective labour before metallurgy, writing or the wheel. The tapering profile — 9 m at the base to 7 m at the top over 8,5 m of height, a batter of roughly 1 in 8 per face — is the same principle a modern drystone waller uses.

## 2. Mesopotamian and Egyptian mudbrick

Sun-dried mudbrick (adobe) is the substrate of Near Eastern and Egyptian walling. The technical knowledge is threefold: **fibre reinforcement** (chopped straw or dung reduces drying shrinkage cracking), **a stone or fired-brick plinth** to keep the earth above splash and capillary rise, and **a sacrificial render** refreshed on a cycle. Neolithic rammed-earth construction is attested at Jarmo in Mesopotamia from the late 8th millennium BC and in the Yangshao and Longshan cultures of the Yellow River valley about 5 000 years ago.

Mudbrick enclosure walls achieved great height by being enormously thick — the section is governed by slenderness, exactly as a modern boundary wall is. The Egyptian *pan-bedding* technique, laying courses in shallow concave undulations, is a deliberate accommodation of differential settlement: an early articulation joint.

## 3. The Great Wall of China

The Chinese were building walls by the Spring and Autumn period (8th–5th c. BC), "made mostly of stone or by stamping earth and gravel between board frames" — that is, rammed earth in formwork, *hangtu*. The 2012 survey by China's National Cultural Heritage Administration put the total of all Great Wall sites at **21 196,18 km** across 10 051 wall sections, of which the Ming wall alone is **8 850 km** (6 259 km wall, 359 km trench, 2 232 km natural barrier).

**What they knew:** to use local material as a rule of logistics — "stone was used in montane areas, while rammed earth was used while building in the plains." Under Qi Jiguang, between 1567 and 1570, 1 200 watchtowers were built from Shanhaiguan to Changping and rammed-earth sections were *faced with brick* — a durable skin over an economical core, the same idea as Roman *opus testaceum* and modern brick-faced blockwork. The Ming shift to brick was a productivity decision: bricks were easier to handle, quickened construction, and bore more load than rammed earth.

The failure mode is instructive. A 2012 report found 22 % of the Ming wall (1 961 km) had disappeared; western sections built of mud rather than brick and stone are far more susceptible, with heights in Gansu reduced from over 5 m to under 2 m by sandstorm erosion. Unrendered earth in an abrasive dry-wind environment is a wasting asset — directly relevant to any earth-wall proposal in northern Namibia.

## 4. Hadrian's Wall and the Roman frontier

Begun in AD 122, Hadrian's Wall ran 80 Roman miles (117 km) from Wallsend to Bowness-on-Solway. It was built by three legions — II Augusta, VI Victrix, XX Valeria Victrix, some 15 000 soldiers — in about six years, with milecastles at Roman-mile intervals and two turrets between each pair.

The most useful detail for a builder is the **change of specification mid-project**. The wall was set out to a uniform 10 Roman feet (3,0 m) thick; resources ran short and the eastern half was reduced to about 8 feet (2,4 m), producing the "Broad Wall" footings carrying "Narrow Wall" superstructure that is still visible on the ground. The western sector was built as a **turf wall**: 6 m wide at the base, about 3,66 m high, in courses of turf blocks 460 × 300 × 150 mm, with the north face battered at about 75 % and the south face starting vertical then flattening. Where the ground was boggy, wooden piles were driven. A basal cobble layer was used west of Milecastle 72.

**What they knew:** that a wide-base battered earth section will stand at 3,7 m; that ground conditions dictate foundation type on a length-by-length basis; and that a border wall is only as good as its gates and its garrison — the gates "may have been customs posts" and the structure was manned by roughly 10 000 auxiliaries.

## 5. Roman wall technology — the *opus* systems

Roman walling separated **core** from **face**, which is the single most transferable idea in this file.

- ***Opus caementicium*** — Roman concrete: aggregate (tuff, brick rubble, ceramic tile, lime clasts) in a hydraulic lime mortar. Vitruvius, writing c. 25 BC, specifies **1 part lime to 3 parts pozzolana** for building mortar and **1 : 2** for underwater work. Pozzolanic mortar has high alumina and silica; the setting chemistry is close to modern Portland cement blended with fly ash or slag. Research published in 2023 attributes much of its durability to *hot mixing* producing lime clasts that let the concrete self-heal cracks.
- ***Opus incertum*** — a facing of irregular, uneven tuff pieces pressed into the wet core. Predecessor to reticulate work; largely gone by the late 1st century.
- ***Opus quasi-reticulatum*** — the transitional, irregular form.
- ***Opus reticulatum*** — small pyramid-shaped tuff blocks set point-inward into the core to give a net-like diamond face. Its virtue was **de-skilling**: the uniform block shape made it "more uniform and accessible to unskilled labourers", shifting the workload to preparation and concrete-making. Used from about 100 BC into the early Empire. Tufo Lionato from the Aniene was the preferred stone. Its weaknesses were real: it needed **quoins** of brick or stone at every edge because it could not form its own arris, and the moderately lithified tuff absorbed moisture and eroded. Vitruvius criticised it for cracking at the joints.
- ***Opus testaceum*** — brick facing, which superseded reticulate work especially after the Great Fire of AD 64. Brick needed no quoins, offered colour variation, and worked well with the concrete core.
- ***Opus africanum*** — vertical stone piers with rubble-mortar infill, faced with unshaped stones, rectangular blocks or reticulate work; found in North Africa, e.g. Bulla Regia in modern Tunisia. This is functionally a **pier-and-panel wall** — the same structural idea as a modern precast panel-and-post boundary wall.

**What they knew:** that structure and appearance can be procured separately and by different grades of labour; that hydraulic binders let you build in wet ground; and that decorative facing is a maintenance liability if the substrate absorbs water.

## 6. Inca dry stone — Sacsayhuamán and Cusco

The three great terrace walls at Sacsayhuamán run about 400 m at the longest and stand roughly 6 m tall, containing over 6 000 m³ of stone. Weight estimates for the largest limestone block range from 128 to nearly 200 tonnes, moved some 35 km from Rumicolca. Cieza de León in 1553 recorded 4 000 labourers quarrying, 6 000 hauling with leather and *cabuya* cables, and the rest preparing foundations — under "great masters of the art of building who had been well instructed."

The technique: split hard stone along natural fracture lines or by chiselling a row of holes and wedging; dress with **hammerstones** (impact marks survive on the faces, chips are found in excavation below walls); lift with rope slings tied to lifting bosses near the block's base; adjust by eye, possibly with a compressible dust. Rowe classified fine masonry as **coursed** or **polygonal**; Agurto subdivided into cellular polygonal (small blocks), ashlar polygonal (very large stones), encased coursed, and sedimentary coursed.

**What they knew — the important part:** walls were built with an **inward inclination** and **rounded corners**, and in double-wall construction the two leaves incline into each other. The combination of tight fit, batter, rounded arrises, interlocking irregular shapes and the absence of resonant frequencies gives exceptional seismic performance; during strong earthquakes blocks have been observed to "dance" and settle back into position. Ashlar was reserved for the most sacred buildings — the Coricancha, the Sun Temple at Machu Picchu — partly because ashlar is *less* forgiving: a broken polygonal corner can be reshaped to fit, an ashlar block cannot.

## 7. Great Zimbabwe and Khami — southern Africa's own drystone science

This is the tradition most directly relevant to a wall built in southern Africa, and it deserves more than a sentence.

**Great Zimbabwe** (Masvingo Province, Zimbabwe) covers 7,22 km². Stone building began in the 11th century AD and continued for over 300 years. The **Great Enclosure**, built in the 13th and 14th centuries, has dry stone walls up to **11 m high** and about **250 m** in extent, with the Conical Tower — 5,5 m in diameter and 9 m high — standing between the two walls. Elite houses were built of thick *daga* (earthen daub) on and within the stone enclosures; the stone walls were largely enclosure and status architecture rather than roofed structure. Water was managed through *dhaka* pits capable of holding more than 18 000 m³.

The granite is naturally **exfoliating**: Zimbabwean granite weathers in sheets, producing slab-like blocks that can be dressed to a regular rectangular face with minimal tooling. That geological gift is what made high, coursed, mortarless walling possible. Walls are **battered inward**, built as two faces with a rubble hearting, and the decorative grammar — chevron, herringbone, chequer, dentelle, cord courses — is executed by varying the coursing near the top of the wall where it is most visible and least structurally critical.

> ⚠️ Note on the archaeological literature: the wall-style classifications commonly cited as **P, Q, PQ and R** (Whitty's typology, refined by Summers and others) are widely used to phase Zimbabwe-tradition walling by coursing regularity and dressing quality. The classification is real, but the specific attributions were not confirmed against a primary source in this pass. `needs-verification`

**Khami** (near Bulawayo, 15th–17th c., the Torwa/Butua successor capital) is the more instructive engineering case. The local stone is **laminar granite with dolerite** — harder to quarry and yielding shapeless blocks, with an estimated **over 60 % of quarried stone unusable** for building. The builders' response was an explicit change of structural strategy: because the stone would not make sound **freestanding** dry walls, they built **retaining walls** instead, creating terraced platforms 2–7 m high on which *dhaka* houses stood. The walls are **gravity retaining walls without mortar**, leaning inward so gravity resists collapse, with foundations of huge blocks needing at least four people to place. The precipice platform's retaining wall is **6 m high × 68 m long**, decorated with a chequerboard pattern along its entire length; excavations of 2000–2006 found chequer, herringbone, cord and variegated-stone decoration throughout the western Hill Complex. Wooden poles were set along the terrace tops, probably as handholds for guards on the high, steep walls.

**What they knew:** match the wall type to the stone you actually have; batter into the retained mass; place the largest blocks at the base; decorate in the top courses; and accept a 60 % quarry reject rate as a cost of the material rather than compromising the wall.

## 8. Medieval curtain walls and city walls

The European curtain wall is a wall as a *system*: a battered plinth (to deflect dropped missiles and to resist undermining), a rubble core between dressed faces, mural towers projecting to give flanking fire along the wall face, a wall-walk and parapet, and gates as the concentration of both defence and revenue. The technical content that matters outside castle studies is the **projecting tower/buttress at regular spacing** — precisely what a modern boundary wall achieves with piers — and the recognition that the **base** is the vulnerable zone.

## 9. English and Irish drystone field walls, and the Cornish hedge

The British drystone wall is the most codified freestanding drystone tradition. A **double wall** is built as two faces on foundation stones bedded on subsoil, with the largest, flattest stones at the bottom and the section **diminishing in width as it rises** — traditional British practice, and the reason British walls stand. **Through-stones** (tie-stones) spanning both faces are placed at intervals, bonding what would otherwise be two thin walls leaning together. The voids are packed with **hearting**, small stones placed by hand, never poured. **Copes** or capstones span the full width and lock the top; in South Wales a **coverband** of flat stones slightly wider than the wall is laid first. A **batter frame** and guidelines control the profile.

Variants encode local conditions: the **Galloway dyke** — double or boulder construction below, single above, deliberately full of holes — is used where wind is exceptional, because a porous wall sheds wind force that would topple a solid one, and the holes deter stock from attempting it. The **Cornish hedge / Welsh *clawdd*** is a stone-clad earth bank topped with turf or scrub, with a strict inward batter, where "the height is the same as the width of the base, and the top is half the base width" — a memorable and directly usable proportion rule for any battered earth or stone boundary.

The craft is now formally certified: the **Dry Stone Walling Association of Great Britain** runs a four-grade scheme from Initial to Master Craftsman (see file `07`). The **art of dry stone walling** was inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2018 for Croatia, Cyprus, France, Greece, Italy, Slovenia, Spain and Switzerland, with Ireland added in 2024. The Croatian island of Baljenac carries 23 km of *suhozidi* on 14 hectares.

## 10. Japanese castle stone bases (*ishigaki*)

Japanese castles evolved from *yamajiro* hill forts of cleared earth and wattle-and-daub to the stone-based castle of the 1570s onward, beginning with Azuchi. Stone bases were built up as a layer of fine pebbles encasing the hilltop, then larger rocks over it, **with no mortar**. The **Anōshū** — a stonemasonry guild, the Anō family of Ōmi Province — were the foremost castle builders of the late 16th century and were renowned for **45-degree stone bases** used not only for the castle mound but for keeps, gatehouses and corner towers. The steep concave-curved walls beneath Kumamoto Castle are known as *musha-gaeshi* ("warrior repeller"). Individual stones could be as much as 6 m high.

**What they knew:** a concave (*ogi-zori*, "fan curve") batter is both harder to climb and more stable seismically than a straight face, because it puts the steepest slope where the load is least; and mortarless construction lets the mass deform and re-seat in an earthquake instead of cracking.

## 11. Adobe and rammed earth worldwide

*Tapial* (Spain and the Mediterranean; *tapia* in the Americas; *pisé* in France; *opus formaceum* to the Romans) is earth compacted in movable timber formwork. The Spanish account is exact: two parallel boards, earth poured in layers (*tongadas*) of **10–15 cm**, compacted by blows with a *pisón*, then the form moved along; openings cut with a chisel after the wall is standing. Pliny recorded that in Hispania and Africa walls were moulded between boards — *parietes formacei* — and claimed they lasted centuries, "stronger than any cement."

Regional refinements matter. In Aragón, where gypsum is abundant, **tapial de yeso y aljezones** substitutes gypsum paste and gypsum stone for earth, giving greater strength and durability; the Andalusi towers and castles around Zaragoza (Alfajarín, Cadrete, Rueda de Jalón) are built this way. In Granada, the Alhambra's walls are ***tapial calicastrado*** — rammed earth with a lime-rich outer crust and brick and stone reinforcement — and it is the iron oxide in the local red clay that gives *al-Qal'ah al-Ḥamrā'*, "the red fortress", its name.

Modern technical parameters: soil is placed in lifts of **100–250 mm** and compacted to about **50 % of loose volume**; cement-stabilised rammed earth cures for a minimum of **28 days** and reaches **5–18 MPa**, with Australian CSRE practice reaching up to 40 MPa; thickness ranges from **150 mm** for non-loadbearing to **600 mm** loadbearing; a **350 mm** wall has a thermal lag of about 12 hours. Formwork must be clamped face-to-face against very large bursting forces. Walls can be wire-brushed or carved within about an hour of striking, after which they are too hard to work.

Africa has its own codified rammed-earth standard: from the late 1970s, work led by British architect Julian Keable through Tanzania, Sierra Leone, Ghana, Kenya, Uganda and Malawi produced *Rammed Earth Structures: A Code of Practice*, which became a Zimbabwean national standard, then a **SADC standard**, and finally an **African Regional Standard**. That lineage makes rammed earth a technically legitimate — not merely romantic — option in Namibia.

## 12. The Berlin Wall and the modern political wall

Construction began on 13 August 1961. The mature "Grenzmauer 75" used concrete segments **3,6 m high**, totalling **106 km** of concrete wall segment, backed by a "death strip" with anti-vehicle trenches, guard towers and obstacles. Over 100 000 people attempted to cross; more than 5 000 succeeded; estimates of those killed range from 136 to over 200. The design detail worth carrying forward is the **smooth pipe coping** — a large-diameter sewer pipe laid along the top specifically to defeat hand-grip. Every anti-climb coping since is a descendant of it.

The political wall has not disappeared; its contemporary forms are border fences and separation barriers. The design lesson is uncomfortable and worth stating: the technical vocabulary of the highest-security boundary — anti-climb coping, no footholds, clear sightlines, detection layer, response — is the same vocabulary now sold for suburban residential use.

## 13. The modern security wall in southern Africa

From roughly the 1980s, and accelerating through the 1990s, the southern African residential boundary changed from a low garden wall or hedge to a **high opaque security perimeter**. The characteristic assembly is:

- a precast concrete **panel-and-post** wall 1,8–2,4 m high, or a plastered block wall of similar height;
- a **coping or capping** — often a moulded precast capping or a chamfered *in situ* course;
- **electric fence** topping of 4–12 strands on brackets, typically 400–800 mm above the wall, energised at 1 Hz;
- **razor or barbed wire** in some contexts;
- **motorised sliding gate** on a track with a rack-and-pinion operator, plus a pedestrian gate with intercom;
- floodlighting and, increasingly, camera coverage.

Electrified **palisade** fences are commonly 2,4 m tall and pulse at 1 Hz, and are mechanically stronger than cable fences — able to resist impact from animals, small falling trees and wildfires.

Two critical observations. First, this assembly is a *product*, driven by the precast walling industry's economics — a two-person crew and a light truck can erect long runs per day with no wet trades — rather than by a considered security analysis of each site. Second, the opaque wall trades **surveillance for concealment**: it removes passive overlooking from the street, which is the cheapest security measure that exists. File `06` treats the alternatives; file `09` treats what actually makes sense in a rural Ohangwena setting, where the traditional Owambo homestead solved the same problem with a **palisade of large vertical poles linked by two horizontal poles on each side**, forming a maze with two gates.
