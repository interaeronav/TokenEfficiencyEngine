# Wall construction methods and workmanship

**Summary.** A boundary wall is built well or badly on six decisions: how accurately it is set out over its full length; how it is stepped on falling ground; whether the gauge is held; whether the mortar is right and the joints are solidly filled; whether control joints and the coping are actually built as drawn; and whether the gates, their piers and their hardware were designed before the wall was set out rather than after. This file covers each in sequence, with build rates, mortar and plaster mixes from Cement & Concrete SA, precast panel-and-post systems in detail, palisade and mesh fencing, gates and automation with real Centurion model data, electric fencing, and a programme for a typical residential run.

## 1. Setting out a long run

A boundary wall is the longest single element on a residential site and the one where cumulative error shows most.

**Establish the line first, then the levels.**

1. **Confirm the boundary.** On surveyed land, get the beacons located by a land surveyor and mark them. On communal land (**[NA]**, most of Ohangwena), the boundary is defined by the traditional authority and by neighbours' agreement; get it walked and agreed *in writing, with the neighbours present*, before a single peg goes in. Retro-fitting a wall that is 700 mm onto a neighbour's land is the single most expensive mistake available on this job.
2. **Set two end pegs** and string a line. For runs over about 60 m, do not trust a string line — it sags and blows. Use a **theodolite/total station**, or set intermediate pegs by ranging: sight from peg A through peg B and have a hand move a ranging rod until it disappears behind B.
3. **Offset the line.** Set a parallel line of **profile boards** 600–1 000 mm outside the wall face so the line survives the excavation. Nail saw-cuts into the profiles for: outside face of foundation, outside face of wall, inside face of wall.
4. **Check the diagonals** on any rectangular enclosure — the 3-4-5 rule at every corner, using 3 m / 4 m / 5 m rather than 300/400/500 mm, which is too short to be accurate over a long run.
5. **Set a datum.** One permanent benchmark on site (a concrete-set steel peg with a nail top), from which every foundation and coping level is taken. Never take levels off a previously built section.

**Tolerances to work to** (these are what to specify and what to check — see file `10`):
- Position of wall face against setting-out line: **± 10 mm**
- Verticality over full height: **± 10 mm**, and never more than **± 5 mm** in any 1 m
- Straightness in plan, measured over 5 m: **± 10 mm**
- Level of coping over 5 m: **± 5 mm**
- Coursing gauge accumulated over 8 courses: **± 5 mm**

### Curves

Set out a circular curve from a centre point with a tape or wire. For a large-radius curve where the centre is inaccessible, use **offsets from the chord**: divide the chord into equal parts and compute the offset `y = √(R² − x²) − (R − m)`, or use the approximation `y ≈ x(L − x)/(2R)` for shallow curves, where `L` is the chord and `R` the radius.

Masonry follows a curve in short chords. With a 222 mm unit and a 10 mm perpend, the maximum angular change per unit before the perpend opens beyond 12 mm on the outside is about **1,5°** for a 220 mm wall — i.e. a minimum practical radius of roughly `232 / (2 × sin 0,75°) ≈ 8,9 m` before you must cut tapered units or open the joints. Below about 3 m radius, use cut units, a curved *in situ* concrete wall, or a segmental block designed for radius work.

Serpentine ("crinkle-crankle") walls are laid out as a repeating arc set: pick an amplitude equal to the required effective depth (from SANS Table 17, e.g. 440 mm for a 110 mm wall at 2,1 m) and a wavelength equal to the permitted pier spacing (1,5 m).

## 2. Level, stepping and sloping ground

On falling ground you have three options and only one of them is usually right.

**A. Step the foundation and step the wall.** Standard practice. Rules:
- Step in whole courses. With 190 mm-high blocks and 10 mm joints the step is a multiple of **200 mm**; with 114 mm maxi units, a multiple of **124 mm**; with 73 mm imperial bricks, a multiple of **83 mm**.
- Each step in the footing must **overlap the next by at least twice the footing thickness, or 300 mm, whichever is greater**, and the step riser must be at least as thick as the footing.
- Maximum step height in one go: **the footing thickness**, i.e. 250 mm for a 250 mm strip. Larger falls need more, shorter steps.
- **Put a control joint at every step.** The code notes that control joints need not continue below ground floor level *except* at changes in level and in freestanding walls — both conditions apply here.
- Remember the code's height rule: where the ground-level difference `x` across the wall exceeds 0,3 m, reduce the permitted height by `(x − 0,3)`. On a cross-fall site this can bite hard.

**B. Rake the coping parallel to the ground.** Visually calm on a steady slope; only works if the slope is uniform, and it complicates the coping (every unit needs a raking cut) and the gate.

**C. Retain and level.** Where the fall is more than about 600 mm over the run, consider a low retaining wall with a freestanding wall above — but note the code treats a wall retaining fill as a **retaining wall**, with a different (lower) table and a mandatory drainage requirement.

> ⚠️ The commonest sloping-ground error is to build a level wall and let the ground fall away, leaving 900 mm of the foundation exposed at one end. The exposed footing is unsightly, undermines in rain, and the wall is now effectively taller than designed at that point. Step it.

## 3. Brick and block laying — gauge and rates

**Gauge.** Set a **gauge rod** — a straight batten marked with the course heights for the units in use — and use it at every profile and at every pier. With 222 × 106 × 73 mm imperial brick, the course is 83 mm and four courses are 332 mm. With 390 × 190 × 190 blocks, the course is 200 mm. Check the gauge rod against the actual delivered units: SANS 227 tolerances allow ±4 mm on individual FBS heights, so a rod set from the drawing rather than from the stock will drift.

**Bond.** Stretcher bond with a half-lap is standard for single-leaf walls. For a 220 mm wall in 110 mm units, use **English or Flemish bond** if it is fair-faced both sides, or two collar-jointed leaves with the collar joint solidly filled if it is to be plastered. Never build two leaves with an unfilled collar joint and call it a 220 mm wall — it is two 110 mm walls and it will behave like it.

**Joints.** 10 mm nominal. **Full bed joints and full perpends.** Furrowing the bed (running a trowel point down the middle to spread the mortar) halves the bonded area and is the direct cause of the base-course failures described in file `03`. On a freestanding wall, insist on solid perpends — buttering the ends properly, not slapping mortar into the joint from above.

**Wetting.** Wet burnt clay bricks before laying. **Do not wet concrete bricks and blocks.** Getting this backwards is a common site error and produces either a dry, unbonded joint (dry clay) or shrinkage cracking (wet concrete).

**Mortar working life** is about two hours. Discard stiffened mortar; do not re-temper it with water after it has begun to set.

**Lifts.** Do not build more than about **1,2–1,5 m of freestanding wall in a day** in one lift — green mortar has almost no flexural strength and a fresh wall is at its most vulnerable to wind. Brace tall lifts with raking props until the mortar has gained strength.

**Curing.** In a **BSh** climate at Okongo, cover the wall with plastic sheeting or hessian and damp it for the first 3 days, and keep the mortar damp for **at least 7 days**. Uncured mortar in 35 °C and low humidity loses a large fraction of its designed strength — and on a freestanding wall, mortar bond *is* the structure.

**Output rates.** These are planning rates for a bricklayer with one labourer, in reasonable conditions, and must be calibrated locally. `needs-verification`

| Work | Rate per bricklayer-day (8 h) |
|---|---|
| 222 × 106 × 73 imperial brick, half-brick wall, plastered | 400–550 bricks (≈ 8–11 m²) |
| 222 × 106 × 73, one-brick (220 mm) wall | 450–600 bricks (≈ 4–6 m²) |
| Face brickwork, fair-faced one side | 300–400 bricks |
| 390 × 190 × 190 blocks | 130–180 blocks (≈ 17–24 m²) |
| 440 × 220 × 220 blocks | 100–140 blocks (≈ 10–14 m²) |

For a 40 m × 1,8 m wall in 440 × 220 × 220 blocks: 720 blocks ÷ 120/day ≈ **6 bricklayer-days**, plus piers, plus 144 m² of plaster ÷ 18 m²/day ≈ **8 plasterer-days**.

## 4. Mortar mixes for exposed walls

Boundary walls are the most exposed masonry on a site: wetted on both faces, dried by wind, with no roof overhang. **Class II mortar** is what SANS 10400-K requires for freestanding, retaining, parapet and balustrade walls, and Cement & Concrete SA lists Class II specifically for "parapets, balustrades, retaining structures; freestanding and garden walls; and other walls exposed to possible severe dampness."

| Mortar | Min. strength 28 d (prelim. lab / works) | Common cement 32,5/42,5 | Masonry cement 22,5X | Per m³ (common cement) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Class I | 14,5 / 10 MPa | 130 ℓ sand per 50 kg | 80 ℓ | 10 bags + 1,25 m³ sand |
| Class II | 7 / 5 MPa | **200 ℓ sand per 50 kg** | 130 ℓ | **7 bags + 1,35 m³ sand** |

Notes that matter:
- Sand quantities assume **5 % moisture content**. Wet sand bulks; measure it as delivered and adjust.
- **Lime is optional**: max 10 kg per 50 kg common cement for Class I, 25 kg for Class II. Mix proportions do not change; yield increases about 5 %. Lime improves workability, water retention and autogenous healing of hairline cracks — worth having on a long exposed wall. **Never add lime to masonry cement.**
- **Masonry cement (SANS 50413) must not be used in concrete** — only in mortar and plaster. It is common for a site to have one cement and use it for everything; check the bag.
- Do a **field test on the sand**: fill a clear bottle with sand, top with water, shake, let it settle. A silt/clay layer above the sand of more than about 6 % of the sand depth means the sand needs washing or rejecting.

## 5. Finishing — plaster, face brick, or bagging

**Plaster and paint** is the default in southern Africa and is right where the block is ugly, the client wants colour, and there is water to cure.

- **External plaster** with common cement: **50 kg : 150 ℓ sand**, yield 120 ℓ per batch; **700 kg cement + 2,25 m³ sand per 100 m² at 15 mm**. Small batches: 1 : 4,5 by volume with common cement, 1 : 3,5 with MC 22,5X or MC 12,5. **Do not use MC 12,5X for external plaster.**
- One coat of 12–15 mm on blockwork; two coats only where the background is very uneven, with the second no richer than the first.
- **Dub out** hollows in a separate operation and let them set before the main coat — plaster thicker than about 20 mm in one pass will craze and debond.
- Cure the plaster: damp it for 3–7 days. In Okongo's climate this is the difference between a wall that holds paint for 8 years and one that crazes in 8 weeks.
- Bell-cast or stop the plaster **150 mm above ground** and leave the base bare, so rising moisture escapes without blowing the render.

**Face brick** eliminates the plaster and paint cycle entirely and is the lowest-maintenance masonry finish. It requires FBX or FBS units (SANS 227), a bricklayer who can keep perpends and a consistent joint profile, and **weather-struck or bucket-handle joints** — never a recessed joint on an exposed boundary wall, which shelves water on every course.

**Bagging (rub-up)** — a slurry of cement and fine sand rubbed over the masonry with a sponge float or hessian to fill the pores and even the colour, leaving the block texture visible. Fast (40–60 m²/day), cheap, and gives an attractively imperfect finish that suits a Mediterranean or rustic palette (file `05`). It is not waterproof and needs a good paint or limewash over it.

**Limewash** over bagged blockwork is the traditional Mediterranean and North African answer and is genuinely appropriate to a hot dry climate: vapour-permeable, cheap, refreshable annually, and antibacterial. See file `05`.

**Off-shutter and board-marked concrete**: see file `06`.

## 6. Precast concrete panel-and-post walling — the dominant southern African product

This deserves detail because it is what most of the market buys and because it is installed badly more often than not.

### The system

- **Posts** — precast reinforced concrete, typically with a groove or slot down two faces (intermediate posts) or one face (end posts) and three faces (corner posts). Set into concrete-filled holes.
- **Panels** — precast reinforced concrete slabs, typically around **300–350 mm high** and spanning the post centres (commonly **2,4–3,0 m**), around **50–75 mm** thick, with a moulded pattern on one or both faces. Stacked to height.
- **Capping** — a moulded coping panel or a separate capping unit.
- Panels may be **plain, single-sided decorative or double-sided decorative**. Country Wide Walling, for instance, offers plain and decorative patterns and single- and double-sided units.

`needs-verification` on the panel and post dimensions above: manufacturers differ and no published datasheet was obtained in this pass. Ask for the datasheet (file `08`).

### How the load path works

The **posts are cantilevers**; the panels are one-way slabs spanning between them. All of the wind moment ends up at the post base. Therefore:

- **The post hole is the structure.** For a 2,0 m wall, a hole of at least **400 × 400 mm × 700 mm deep** filled with 15–20 MPa concrete is a sensible minimum in firm ground; deepen to **900 mm** in loose Kalahari sand. Design it as a short cantilever pile: `depth ≈ 0,3 × (exposed height) + 300 mm` is a workable first estimate, but check overturning. `needs-verification`
- Cast the post footing and let it gain strength **before** loading the posts with panels. A crew that sets posts and stacks panels the same morning is building a wall that will lean.
- Set the **bottom panel below finished ground level** by 50–100 mm. Otherwise the standard method of entry is to lever out a bottom panel and crawl through — the single most exploited weakness of this system.
- Fill the groove between panel edge and post with mortar or a flexible sealant to close the gap; unsealed, the wall leaks light, dust and noise.

### Installation sequence

1. Set out the line and mark post positions at the manufacturer's exact centres (panels are cast to a fixed length; the post spacing is not adjustable).
2. Auger or dig all holes to depth. Check depth with a rod, not by eye.
3. Set corner and end posts first, plumb and braced; string a line at the top of the posts.
4. Set intermediate posts to the line, checking spacing with the actual panel, not the drawing.
5. Concrete the posts; brace and leave.
6. After 24–48 h, slide the panels in from the top, starting with the bottom panel.
7. Cap, point the grooves, and make good the ground each side.

### Where it goes wrong

Undersized/shallow post holes; posts concreted with a dry weak mix or with soil; panels forced into out-of-tolerance post spacing and cracked; bottom panel above ground; no capping; and no allowance for a gate pier, which is a masonry or steel element and must be founded quite differently from a fence post.

## 7. Palisade and welded mesh fencing

**Steel palisade.** Vertical pales (typically 40 × 40 mm square tube, or D-section/W-section rolled sections) bolted through two or three horizontal rails, between I-section or square-tube posts at 2,5–3,0 m centres, set in concrete. Splayed or spiked pale tops. Hot-dip galvanized to SANS 121 (ISO 1461) is worth the premium over painted mild steel; painted steel on a boundary needs recoating every 5–7 years.

**Concrete palisade.** Precast concrete pales in precast posts; cheaper, heavier, no corrosion, but brittle and easily broken with a hammer.

**Welded mesh.** Panel systems on posts, from light garden mesh to **anti-climb ("358") mesh** with very small apertures that admit neither fingers nor bolt-cutter jaws. Superior climb resistance to palisade at higher cost. `needs-verification` on aperture and wire dimensions.

**Detailing that matters for all of them:**
- **The ground line is the enemy.** Corrosion and rot concentrate where the post enters concrete or soil. Cast the concrete with a **domed top falling away from the post**, and paint or galvanize 150 mm above and below.
- **Founding:** post embedment of at least **1/3 of the exposed height** in concrete, minimum 600 mm.
- **Provide a plinth.** A 300–600 mm masonry or concrete plinth under a palisade or mesh fence keeps dirt, water and animals out and stops the "crawl under" defeat.
- **Straining posts** at corners, at changes of direction, and every 50–60 m on straight runs.

## 8. Gates — structure, hardware, motorisation, access control

Gates are the weakest and most expensive part of any boundary. Design them **before** setting out the wall, because the gate determines the pier positions.

### Gate structure

- **Sliding (cantilever or tracked)** — the standard for vehicle entrances in southern Africa. A **tracked** gate runs on a ground rail; cheap, but the rail fills with sand and stones (a real problem in Kalahari sand) and is a trip hazard. A **cantilever** gate is carried on rollers in a backing frame with no ground track — better in sand, more expensive, and needs a run-back of about **1,4–1,5 × the opening width**.
- **Swing (single or double leaf)** — needs swing clearance and a level, well-drained arc. Wind load on a solid swing leaf is significant; use a slatted or perforated leaf on exposed sites.
- **Frame:** typically 50 × 50 × 3 mm or 76 × 50 × 3 mm rectangular hollow section, with intermediate stiles at ≤ 600 mm and a diagonal brace running **from the bottom hinge corner up to the top latch corner** — always in this direction, so the brace works in compression.
- **Infill:** vertical bars, slats, mesh, or sheet. A sheet-clad gate is a sail; on an exposed site it is the element most likely to wreck its own hinges.
- **Mass:** a 4,0 m × 1,8 m steel gate with a light infill typically runs **150–300 kg**; sheet-clad or timber-clad, **300–600 kg**. Weigh or calculate it — the motor selection depends on it.

### Gate piers

Gate piers carry the whole gate load, static and dynamic, and they are the anchor for the lock or motor. Build them as **isolated, over-designed elements**, not as an incidental thickening of the wall:

- Minimum **440 × 440 mm** masonry with filled cores and 4 × Y12 vertical bars, cast into a **1 000 × 1 000 × 400 mm** reinforced pad.
- Or a steel post — 100 × 100 × 5 mm SHS or heavier — set 900 mm into a 400 mm concrete-filled hole and clad in masonry.
- Put an **articulation/control joint between the gate pier and the adjoining wall**, both sides. The pier will move differently from the wall and will crack it otherwise.

### Hinges

- **Swing gates:** heavy-duty adjustable hook-and-band or ball-bearing pin hinges, minimum two per leaf, three above 500 kg or 2,0 m leaf height. Specify **adjustable in three axes** — the pier will never be exactly where the drawing says.
- Fit a **bottom pin retainer** so the gate cannot be lifted off its hinges.
- Grease points on every hinge. In a dusty environment, a dry hinge seizes and the motor destroys itself trying.
- **Sliding gates:** two bottom rollers (tracked) or four cantilever rollers in a guide bracket, plus two top guide rollers on the pier. All rollers sealed-bearing type; open bearings fill with sand.

Selection and installation notes:

- **Size on real mass, not on gate width.** Add 30 % for a gate on a track that will fill with sand.
- The **12 V and 24 V DC** operators run from an internal battery that the mains trickle-charges, so they keep working through a power cut — essential in northern Namibia, where supply is not continuous.
- **Rack** is fixed to the gate frame at the manufacturer's height above the pinion; steel rack on a steel gate, bolted (not tack-welded) so it can be adjusted.
- **Origin/limit sensing** on modern operators is by encoder rather than mechanical limit switches, which removes a common failure point.
- **Safety devices** — infrared beams across the opening, and a force-limiting setting on the operator. A powered gate can kill a child. Fit beams; commission the force limits; test them.
- **Access control** — code-hopping remotes, keypads, GSM intercom, or a hard-wired intercom to the house. In a village setting with visitors on foot, a **pedestrian gate with its own intercom and lock** is more useful than any vehicle-gate gadget.
- Run **conduit under the driveway before it is paved**: at least one 32 mm sleeve for the motor supply and one 25 mm for beams and intercom. Retrofitting this costs ten times as much.

> ⚠️ Gate-operator safety requirements (force limits, edge protection, beam coverage) are governed by product standards in the IEC 60335 / EN 12453 family. The specific standard applicable in Namibia and South Africa was not confirmed in this pass. `needs-verification` — but fit beams regardless.

## 9. Electric fencing

The standard southern African security topping.

- An **energiser** converts mains or battery power into a short high-voltage pulse, typically **one pulse per second (1 Hz)**, delivered to bare wires; a separate earth rod completes the circuit through anyone touching a live wire and the ground.
- Residential wall-top installations use **4–12 strands** on insulated brackets set 300–800 mm above the coping, angled outward.
- Energisers may be mains-powered or run from a lead-acid or dry-cell battery, often solar-maintained. A fence in good condition draws little power; a battery serving several hundred metres can last weeks on a charge. In Okongo, specify **solar-maintained battery operation** as standard, not as an option.
- **Electrified palisade fences** — pulsing the palisade itself — are typically 2,4 m tall, also at 1 Hz, and are mechanically stronger than cable fences, resisting impacts from wildlife, falling branches and fire.
- Vegetation shorting the fence is the main nuisance; keep a 600 mm cleared strip both sides.

> ⚠️ Electric fences carry lethal potential. Installation, signage and commissioning must be by a person competent and, where the law requires it, certificated. **[ZA]** an Electric Fence System Certificate of Compliance is required in South Africa; the equivalent Namibian requirement was not confirmed. `needs-verification` Warning signage at intervals along the fence and at every gate is mandatory practice.

## 10. Sequence and durations for a typical residential run

Basis: **40 m of 1,8 m plastered blockwork boundary wall**, one 4,0 m sliding vehicle gate, one 1,0 m pedestrian gate, on flat ground at Okongo, with a gang of 2 bricklayers + 4 labourers, plus specialists.

| # | Activity | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Boundary confirmation, neighbour agreement, survey | 1–5 days | Elapsed, not resource. Do first. |
| 2 | Clear and grub the line, 1,5 m each side | 1 day | Remove roots — they will lift the footing |
| 3 | Set out, profiles, datum | 0,5 day | Re-check diagonals |
| 4 | Excavate trench 800 × 450 mm, 40 m | 2 days | 14,4 m³ by hand; ½ day by TLB if available |
| 5 | Trim, compact bottom, blind | 0,5 day | |
| 6 | Gate pier pads (2 no.) reinforcement + cast | 1 day | 25 MPa |
| 7 | Cast strip footing, 8 m³ 15 MPa | 1 day | Mixer on site; 47 bags cement |
| 8 | **Cure footing** | 3 days | Overlaps with 9 |
| 9 | Order/deliver blocks, coping, gate steel | Concurrent | 2–6 weeks lead time — start at step 1 |
| 10 | Build wall to 1,2 m (lift 1) | 3 days | Brickforce every 400 mm; form control joints |
| 11 | Build to full height (lift 2), piers, gate piers | 3 days | Fill pier cores with grade 10 infill |
| 12 | **Cure masonry** | 7 days | Overlaps with 13 |
| 13 | Bed coping, joint over control joints | 1 day | |
| 14 | Plaster both sides, 144 m² | 8 days | Two plasterers = 4 days |
| 15 | Cure plaster | 5 days | Damp daily |
| 16 | Gate fabrication (off site) | 5–10 days | Concurrent from step 6 |
| 17 | Hang gates, fit hardware | 1 day | |
| 18 | Sleeves, cabling, motor, beams, intercom | 1 day | Sleeves must go in before paving |
| 19 | Electric fence installation and commissioning | 1 day | Specialist |
| 20 | Paint, 2 coats | 2 days | After plaster has cured ≥ 14 days |
| 21 | Make good ground, snag, hand over | 1 day | |

**Critical path ≈ 28–32 working days** (about 6–7 weeks), of which the true constraints are material lead time (step 9), curing (steps 8, 12, 15) and gate fabrication (step 16). Compressing the cure is the false economy that ruins boundary walls.
