# Contemporary wall and boundary design

**Summary.** Contemporary wall design has converged on a small number of materially honest languages — board-marked concrete, perforated screen block, weathering steel, gabion, rammed earth, timber and aluminium screening, and living facades — each of which resolves privacy, security and appearance differently. This file covers the technique behind each, names real built projects and their architects, and then addresses the two questions that actually decide a residential boundary: how to layer for privacy versus security, and how contemporary architects treat the threshold.

## 1. Board-marked and off-shutter concrete

Concrete cast against a deliberately articulated formwork so that the shutter becomes the finish.

**Technique.**
- **Board-marked**: rough-sawn timber boards, typically **100–200 mm wide × 22–32 mm thick**, laid horizontally or vertically, with the grain raised (sandblast, wire-brush, or pre-weather the boards) so the grain prints into the concrete. Board joints must be sealed with a compressible tape or the concrete will bleed and leave fins.
- **Smooth off-shutter (*béton brut* / *fair-faced*)**: film-faced plywood, laid to a set-out pattern with the **tie-hole positions and panel joints designed as part of the elevation**. Tadao Ando's canonical version uses a 900 × 1 800 mm panel with six recessed tie plugs, but any module can be used — the point is that it is deliberate.
- **Formwork release agent** must be the same product, applied at the same rate, on every panel. Mixed release agents produce mottling that cannot be corrected.
- **One concrete supplier, one mix, one cement source, one sand source** for the entire visible pour. Concrete colour is driven by the cement and fine aggregate; changing either mid-job produces a visible line.
- **Pour continuously**, and plan every construction joint as a designed line, not an accident.
- Compact with **poker vibrators inserted at close, regular spacing** and withdrawn slowly; over-vibration segregates and under-vibration blowholes.

**Realistic expectations.** Off-shutter concrete is unforgiving and expensive to do well, and it needs a contractor who has done it before. On a residential boundary in a place without a specialist concrete subcontractor, **a bagged and limewashed blockwork wall will look better than a badly cast off-shutter one**. Do not specify board-marked concrete unless you have seen the contractor's previous work.

**Precedents:** Tadao Ando's Church of the Light (Ibaraki, 1989) and Koshino House; Peter Zumthor's Therme Vals (1996, stone rather than concrete but the same discipline of coursing); Le Corbusier's Unité d'Habitation (Marseille, 1952) as the origin of *béton brut*.

## 2. Perforated walling — breeze block, screen block, cobogó

The most useful contemporary boundary language for a hot climate, because it does three things at once: reduces wind load, admits air and filtered light, and gives privacy by pattern rather than by opacity.

**The Brazilian *cobogó*** is the canonical version: a hollow wall-filling element, named from the initials of three Recife engineers — **Amadeu Oliveira Coimbra, Ernst August Boeckmann and Antônio de Góis** — who conceived it in **1929–30**. Originally cement-only; now also clay and glass, in a wide variety of forms and patterns. It became central to Brazilian modernism, used extensively by Lúcio Costa, Oscar Niemeyer and their contemporaries, and is currently in a global revival.

**The southern African screen-block tradition** ran in parallel through the 1950s–70s — pierced concrete and clay screen blocks in schools, civic buildings and suburban walls. Much of it was demolished during the security-wall era; it is now returning.

**Real product:** **Corobrik breezeblock** — **222 × 73 × 106 mm**, **37 units per m²**, 2,2 kg each, compressive strength **>10 MPa**, 24-hour water absorption 9,1, classified **FBX** face brick (the tightest tolerance class), available in eight satin colours (Agate, Silvergrey, Titanium, Onyx, Platinum, Black Brick, Firelight, Roan), 500 per pallet, made to order.

**Design rules.**
- Perforated walling is **not a deemed-to-satisfy freestanding wall type**. Treat it as **infill between structural piers or in a reinforced concrete frame**, at close centres — 1,5–2,5 m is typical.
- Reinforce every third or fourth horizontal joint with brickforce, and grout vertical bars into piers.
- **Privacy by geometry, not by opacity.** A screen at 40 % open area gives near-total privacy at an oblique viewing angle and near-none straight on. Set the perforation axis so that the normal view direction from outside is oblique. Sloping the perforation channels (as good cobogó do) achieves this in a single unit.
- **Security:** a screen wall is climbable. Use it above a solid plinth, behind a hedge, or where security is not the primary requirement.
- **Dust:** in a windblown-sand environment, a screen wall passes sand into the courtyard. Locate it on the leeward side, or accept and design for it.

## 3. Corten / weathering steel

Weathering steel (ASTM A242, A588, A606 and the well-known trade name **COR-TEN**) develops a stable, adherent rust patina that slows further corrosion, allowing use without paint.

**What you must know before specifying it:**
- **It runs.** For the first 1–3 years the patina sheds iron oxide, staining everything below it — paving, walls, plants. Detail a **drip and a gap**, and use dark or gravel surfaces beneath, never pale concrete.
- **It needs wet–dry cycling to stabilise.** Permanently wet conditions, sheltered conditions, or ground contact prevent patina formation and it corrodes continuously.
- **It does not stop corroding.** It slows. Section loss over 30–50 years is real; do not use thin sections in the ground.
- **Chlorides destroy it.** Coastal Namibian sites (Swakopmund, Walvis Bay, Lüderitz) are a poor application; inland Okongo is a good one.
- Typical boundary application: 4–8 mm plate panels, laser-cut with a pattern, on a galvanized or stainless subframe, set clear of the ground on a plinth.

**Precedents:** Richard Serra's *Torqued Ellipses* and *Fulcrum*; the Angel of the North (Antony Gormley, 1998); the Barclays Center facade (SHoP Architects, 2012); and, in southern Africa, a growing use of laser-cut Corten screens in gates and entrance walls.

## 4. Gabion

Covered structurally in file `02`; architecturally the reference is **Dominus Estate winery, Yountville, Napa Valley**, by **Herzog & de Meuron** (1996–97, US$5 m, ~4 600 m²) — their first US project — whose entire exterior is modular wire-mesh gabions filled with locally quarried basalt. The stones are graded so the wall is dense and dark at the base and looser and more translucent higher up, admitting filtered light, allowing air movement, and moderating internal temperature between hot days and cold nights.

**Practical points for a boundary wall:**
- Specify **stone size 1,5–2 × the mesh aperture**, hard, angular and durable. Rounded river cobble settles and slumps.
- **Hand-place the face stones**; tip only the hearting. The difference between a beautiful gabion and a rubbish skip is entirely in the face placement.
- Fit **internal bracing wires** every 300 mm of height, or the baskets bulge.
- Use **PVC-coated galvanized wire** (est. 60-year life) rather than plain galvanized for anything permanent.
- Put a **geotextile** behind any gabion retaining soil.
- Gabion gives **no acoustic benefit** and is climbable; use it for retaining, for plinths, and for its appearance, not for security.

## 5. Rammed earth revival and Martin Rauch

Rammed earth's contemporary revival is largely the work of one practice: **Lehm Ton Erde Baukunst GmbH**, founded by **Martin Rauch** in Schlins, Vorarlberg, Austria.

**Key built work (all confirmed from Lehm Ton Erde's project index):**

| Project | Location | Year | Architect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rauch workshop & studio | Schlins, AT | 1994 | Robert Felber & Martin Rauch |
| **Chapel of Reconciliation** | Berlin, DE | **2000** | Rudolf Reitermann & Peter Sassenroth |
| **Rauch House** | Schlins, AT | **2008** | Martin Rauch with Roger Boltshauser |
| **Ricola Herb Center** | Laufen, CH | **2012** | **Herzog & de Meuron** |
| ERDEN House / Lehm Ton Erde workshop | Schlins, AT | 2022 | Lehm Ton Erde |
| Brickyard – The Rammed Earth House | Wiltshire, GB | 2024 | Lehm Ton Erde |
| HORTUS | Allschwil, Basel, CH | 2024 | — |
| Hospital Chapel Elisabethinen | Graz, AT | 2025 | — |

The **Chapel of Reconciliation** in Berlin is the most relevant precedent for this domain, because it stands on the site of the Berlin Wall's death strip: a rammed-earth oval inside a timber slat screen, replacing a church demolished by the GDR in 1985. It is a wall building about walls.

The **Ricola Herb Center** (Herzog & de Meuron, 2012) is the largest rammed-earth building in Europe and demonstrates the industrialised version of the technique — prefabricated rammed-earth blocks made from local quarry material, stacked and jointed, with the horizontal stratification of the lifts as the entire architectural expression.

**Rauch's technical contributions worth knowing:**
- **Erosion checks (*Erosionsbremse*)** — thin courses of trass-lime or brick built into the wall at intervals of roughly 300–600 mm, which shed water and arrest the vertical erosion channels that otherwise cut into an exposed earth face. `needs-verification` on the exact spacing.
- **Designed erosion.** Rather than trying to make earth permanent, the wall is designed to weather at a known rate, with a large roof overhang and a plinth doing most of the protection.
- **Prefabrication** of rammed-earth elements, which moves the labour off the weather-dependent site.

**For northern Namibia:** rammed earth is technically plausible — the Kalahari sands need clay added, and cement stabilisation at 5–8 % would almost certainly be required. Africa has a codified standard lineage: *Rammed Earth Structures: A Code of Practice*, developed through work in Tanzania, Sierra Leone, Ghana, Kenya, Uganda and Malawi, became a Zimbabwean national standard, then a **SADC standard**, then an **African Regional Standard**. That makes it specifiable. The honest caution is the carbon one: a 300 mm CSRE wall at 5 % cement produces slightly more CO₂ than a 100 mm concrete wall, so rammed earth is only "green" if the soil is on site and the cement is minimal.

## 6. Timber slat screens

The cheapest way to make a boundary read as contemporary rather than defensive.

- **Slats**: 38 × 38 mm to 70 × 20 mm, in a rot- and termite-resistant or preservative-treated species. Fixed to a steel or timber subframe.
- **Gap ratio** decides privacy: a **1:1** slat-to-gap ratio blocks views beyond about 45° obliquity; **2:1** gives near-full privacy from a walking-past position; **1:2** is a light screen only.
- **Angle the slats** (30–45°) to give one-way privacy — open to the view from inside, closed from outside.
- **Keep timber out of the ground.** Steel posts, galvanized shoes, and a minimum **150 mm** clearance from paving to the lowest slat.
- **Termites (Namibia).** Untreated timber in or near ground contact in Ohangwena will not last. Specify CCA or creosote treatment to the correct hazard class (H4/H5 for ground contact) or use only above a masonry plinth with a physical termite barrier.
- Expect to **re-oil or re-stain every 2–3 years** in high UV, or accept silvering.

## 7. Aluminium louvre systems

Extruded aluminium blades (elliptical, aerofoil or flat, typically 50–200 mm) in an aluminium frame, fixed or adjustable, powder-coated or anodised.

- **Advantages:** dimensionally stable, no maintenance beyond washing, no rot or termites, available with motorised adjustment, and the only screening material that genuinely lasts in coastal Namibian conditions if marine-grade anodised.
- **Disadvantages:** high cost, high embodied energy, and a hard industrial appearance that can read as commercial rather than domestic.
- Louvre pitch and blade angle determine both privacy and solar shading; a 45° blade at a pitch equal to the blade width blocks all direct view from one side.
- **Fully imported** for a Namibian project; budget lead time accordingly.

## 8. Green walls and living facades

Two distinct systems, frequently confused:

- **Green facade** — climbers rooted in the ground, growing on the wall directly (self-clinging: ivy, Ficus pumila) or on a trellis/cable system held **50–100 mm off the wall** (twining: jasmine, bougainvillea, vines). Low cost, low technology, low risk. **Use this one.**
- **Living wall** — plants growing in a modular substrate system fixed to the wall, with automatic irrigation and fertigation. High cost, high water demand, high failure rate, and a maintenance contract for life.

For a boundary wall in Ohangwena, a **green facade on a cable trellis** is the only sensible option: it uses no more water than the plants would in the ground, it shades the wall (reducing surface temperature substantially and thus reducing heat re-radiation into the courtyard), and if it dies the wall is unharmed. A living wall in a semi-arid climate with an intermittent water supply is a liability.

Detailing: keep self-clinging climbers off rendered or limewashed walls (they destroy the finish and the aerial roots hold moisture); always use a spaced trellis on a finished wall; and keep vegetation clear of any electric fence by 600 mm both sides.

## 9. Integrated lighting

Lighting turns a wall from a daytime object into a 24-hour one, and it is the cheapest single upgrade to both appearance and security.

- **Grazing.** A linear source **50–150 mm** off the wall face, aimed along it, reveals texture — the correct technique for board-marked concrete, rammed earth, gabion and coursed stone. The closer the source, the more dramatic the texture.
- **Washing.** A source further out (typically **0,3–0,5 × wall height**) gives even illumination and *suppresses* texture. Correct for smooth plastered walls.
- **Uplighting piers and trees** for rhythm; the strongest cheap effect on a long boundary.
- **Step and threshold lighting** at gates: recessed low-level, glare-free.
- **Security lighting** and **amenity lighting** are different jobs. Floodlights aimed outward blind the observer and create black shadow beyond — worse than nothing. Aim light **at the wall and the ground**, not at the approach.
- **Specify warm colour temperature (2 700–3 000 K)** for domestic work; 4 000 K and above reads institutional.
- **Conduit must be cast in.** Run at least one 25 mm sleeve along the wall at coping level and one at ground level, with draw boxes at every pier, before plastering.
- **Off-grid consideration [NA]:** a boundary lighting circuit is an easy candidate for a small dedicated PV + battery system, independent of the house supply and of grid interruptions.

## 10. Layering for privacy versus security

The single most useful idea in contemporary boundary design is that **privacy and security are different problems and often demand opposite solutions**.

| | Privacy wants | Security wants |
|---|---|---|
| Opacity | Opaque | Transparent (so intruders can be seen) |
| Height | Enough to block sightlines | Enough to resist climbing |
| Position | Close to the space being screened | At the perimeter, with clear ground inside |
| Planting | Dense, tall, close | Low and clear; no cover, no climbing aid |
| Lighting | Low, warm, inward | Even, on the wall and ground |
| Overlooking | Prevent it | Encourage it (passive surveillance from the street) |

The resolution is **layering** — solve each at a different plane:

1. **Perimeter plane (security):** transparent or semi-transparent — palisade, welded mesh, or a masonry plinth with an open fence above. Visible from the street. Clear ground both sides. Detection at this plane (electric fence, beams).
2. **Middle plane (buffer):** planting, a driveway, a service yard — space that is inside the boundary but not private. Nothing here that gives cover.
3. **Inner plane (privacy):** the courtyard wall, screen or slat wall close to the living space. This one can be solid, and being closer to the observer it needs to be *lower* to screen the same sightline.

This is the *cortijo* logic (file `05`) in modern dress, and it is markedly better than the southern African default of one tall opaque wall doing both jobs badly.

**The geometry of privacy.** To screen a viewer of eye height `h_e` at horizontal distance `d_v` from a wall, from seeing a point at height `h_t` at distance `d_t` on the other side, the required wall height is
`h_w = h_e + (h_t − h_e) × d_v / (d_v + d_t)`.
A viewer at 1,6 m eye height standing 2 m from the wall, screening a seated person (1,2 m) 4 m the other side, needs `h_w = 1,6 + (1,2 − 1,6) × 2/6 = 1,47 m`. Moving the screen from the perimeter to 1 m from the seated person drops the required height further. **Screens close to what they protect are shorter, cheaper and less oppressive than perimeter walls.**

## 11. The threshold

How contemporary architects handle the point where the boundary is crossed is the clearest signal of whether a project has been thought about.

**Approaches that work:**

- **The thickened gate.** Rather than a hole in a wall, the entrance is a piece of wall in its own right — deeper, taller, differently finished, with the gate set back into a reveal of 400–1 000 mm. Barragán's Cuadra San Cristóbal (Los Clubes, Mexico City, **1966–68**) does this with a coloured plane; the Casa Barragán (1948, **UNESCO World Heritage Site 2004**) does it with a compressed, dark entrance sequence that opens into light. Barragán received the **Pritzker Prize in 1980**, the second laureate; his colour and spatial thinking runs directly into **Ricardo Legorreta's** work, notably the Hotel Camino Real, Polanco, Mexico City.
- **The gate as the only ornament.** The *cortijo* move: a blank wall and one great door. Extremely effective and extremely cheap, because the whole decorative budget goes to 4 m of the run.
- **Compression and release.** A narrow, low, dark entry opening into a bright courtyard. The oldest trick in Islamic and Mediterranean domestic architecture and still the most reliable.
- **The bent entry.** Never see the whole courtyard from the gate. The traditional Owambo homestead does this with a maze of palisade passages (file `09`); the Andalusian house does it with a *zaguán* (entrance vestibule) turning 90°.
- **Material change at the threshold.** The paving, not the wall, announces the entrance.
- **Separating vehicle and person.** A vehicle gate is an engineering object; a pedestrian gate is a social one. Give the pedestrian gate its own place, its own light, its own bell, and make it the one people use.

**Approaches that fail:** a motorised gate as the entire architectural gesture; ornamental precast pilasters and finials; a gate wider or taller than the wall it sits in; and a threshold with nowhere to stand out of the sun while waiting.

## 12. Applying this at Okongo

The contemporary languages that genuinely suit a rural northern Namibian residential boundary, in order of practicality:

1. **Screen block / breeze block panels between masonry piers** — hot-climate appropriate, reduces wind load, locally buildable, and a direct descendant of both cobogó and southern African screen-block modernism.
2. **Bagged and limewashed blockwork with a thickened, coloured entrance** — cheapest route to a strong result.
3. **Timber slat screening on masonry plinths** for the inner privacy plane, using treated poles, echoing the traditional palisade.
4. **Gabion** for retaining and for plinths, if suitable rock is available locally; not for the security plane.
5. **Rammed earth** as a feature wall or courtyard wall, with a proper plinth, a real coping, and cement stabilisation — expensive in skill, striking in result.
6. **Corten** as a laser-cut gate panel or entrance screen — inland climate is favourable, but it is imported and it stains.
