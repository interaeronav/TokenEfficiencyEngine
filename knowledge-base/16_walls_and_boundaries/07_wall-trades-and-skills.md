# Wall trades and skills

**Summary.** Walls are built by six distinguishable trades — bricklayer/mason, drystone waller, plasterer/renderer, stonemason (banker and fixer), rammed-earth specialist, and fencing contractor — with very different training routes, certification, output rates and failure modes. The only one with a rigorous, published, internationally recognised craft-certification scheme is drystone walling, through the **DSWA** in Great Britain (four levels, Lantra-accredited, Ofqual-recognised at Levels 1–3). Southern African masonry competence is certified through national trade testing — the **Namibia Training Authority** under the Vocational Education and Training Act 2008 **[NA]**, and the QCTO/SETA system **[ZA]** — but in practice most boundary-wall work is done by informally trained builders, so this file also gives a practical method for assessing a tradesman's work before hiring.

## 1. The bricklayer / mason

The trade that builds nearly every boundary wall in southern Africa.

### Skill hierarchy

| Stage | Typical time | What they can do reliably |
|---|---|---|
| Labourer / hod carrier | 0–12 months | Mix to a ratio, load out, keep the bricklayer supplied, clean up. Cannot lay. |
| Improver / assistant bricklayer | 1–2 years | Lay a straight run to a line in stretcher bond, keep gauge with supervision. |
| Bricklayer (trade-tested) | 3–4 years | Set out from profiles, corners and returns, English/Flemish bond, piers, openings, control joints, arches, fair-faced work. |
| Chargehand / leading hand | 5–8 years | Run a gang, set out a whole boundary, resolve level and step problems, manage quality. |
| Foreman mason | 8+ years | Programme, sequence, quantities, subcontract coordination, defect diagnosis. |

### Certification

**[NA]** The **Namibia Training Authority** regulates and funds vocational education and training under the **Vocational Education and Training Act, 2008**, develops vocational qualifications, oversees assessment and certification, and registers training providers. Bricklaying and plastering are delivered through Vocational Training Centres and assessed to national standards; the exact qualification titles and NQF levels were not confirmed in this pass. `needs-verification` — ask a candidate for their **NTA certificate and level** and verify it with the NTA.

**[ZA]** Trade certification runs through the **QCTO** and the construction SETA, culminating in a **trade test** and a national trade certificate. South African trade-tested bricklayers are common in Namibia and their certificates are widely recognised.

**Neither certificate proves the person can build a boundary wall.** Trade tests concentrate on building work — corners, openings, bonding — not on the specific competences a freestanding wall demands: setting out a long run, stepping on a slope, control-joint discipline, and full solid joints in an unbraced wall.

### Output rates

Planning rates for a bricklayer with one labourer, 8-hour day, reasonable conditions. `needs-verification` — calibrate locally.

| Work | Rate/day |
|---|---|
| Half-brick (110 mm) wall, common brick, to be plastered | 400–550 bricks (8–11 m²) |
| One-brick (220 mm) wall | 450–600 bricks (4–6 m²) |
| Face brickwork, fair one side | 300–400 bricks |
| 390 × 190 × 190 hollow blocks | 130–180 blocks (17–24 m²) |
| 440 × 220 × 220 solid blocks | 100–140 blocks (10–14 m²) |
| Piers, returns, cut work | Deduct 25–35 % |

A good measure of a bricklayer's real quality is how the rate *changes* with the work. A poor bricklayer is fast on a straight run and collapses at the first pier. A good one is only 30 % slower at the piers.

### Common skill failures

1. **Furrowed bed joints** — running the trowel down the middle of the bed to spread mortar, halving the bonded area. Fatal in a freestanding wall.
2. **Slapped perpends** — mortar dropped in from above rather than buttered onto the unit end. The perpends are hollow, the wall leaks and is weak in horizontal bending.
3. **Re-tempering stiff mortar** with water after it has begun to set. The wall looks fine and has half the bond strength.
4. **Wetting concrete blocks / not wetting clay bricks.** Backwards from what Cement & Concrete SA specifies.
5. **Omitting control joints** because they look wrong. Result: cracks at 5–8 m centres.
6. **Losing gauge** by not using a gauge rod, so the coping course has to be cut or the joints opened.
7. **Building too high in a day** on a freestanding wall, then blaming wind for the collapse.
8. **Building over roots and topsoil** because the trench was not cleaned out.
9. **Piers stopped short of the top**, or reduced in size at height — explicitly forbidden by SANS 10400-K 4.2.4.2.
10. **No curing.** In a hot dry climate this alone can halve the delivered mortar strength.

### How to assess before hiring

Walk two of their completed walls, ideally 3+ years old, and check:
- **Sight along the wall** at eye level for straightness in plan and for bulges.
- **Plumb** with a 1,2 m level at five random points and at every pier.
- **Look at the perpends** — hollow perpends show as dark shadow lines and pinholes on an unplastered wall; on a plastered wall, tap for hollowness.
- **Look for cracks at 5–8 m intervals** and ask where the control joints are. If they cannot show you one, they do not build them.
- **Look at the coping** — overhang, drip, joints over the control joints.
- **Look at the base** — is the wall 150 mm clear of ground? Is the footing exposed anywhere?
- **Ask them to set out a right angle** with a tape in front of you. If they reach for 3-4-5 without prompting, they are a bricklayer. If they use a builder's square on a 40 m run, they are not.

## 2. The drystone waller

The one wall trade with a rigorous, independently assessed craft certification. Worth knowing about even if you will never build a drystone wall, because it is the benchmark against which every other wall trade's "certification" should be judged.

### The DSWA Craft Certification Scheme

Operated by the **Dry Stone Walling Association of Great Britain** (registered charity 289678, Lane Farm, Crooklands, Milnthorpe, Cumbria). **Accredited by Lantra; recognised by Ofqual for Levels 1–3.** Four levels:

| Level | Requirement | Prerequisite |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Build **2,5 m²** of wall, **minimum 1 m high below the copes**. Judged on **sound and solid construction rather than finished quality**. | None — entry level |
| **2** | **2,5 m²**, min 1 m below copes, **must include a vertical cheekend**. Judged on straightness, batter and cheekend quality. This is the qualification required to **register as a Professional Dry Stone Waller**. | **≥ 12 months'** dry stone walling experience |
| **3** | Two-part assessment: **features** plus a **timed test element**. Higher craftsmanship standard. | Level 2 |
| **Master Craftsman** | **Part A:** two features, including a mandatory **5 m² of wall on sloping ground of at least 30°**. **Part B:** three features with one timed test element. | Level 3 |

The scheme's structure is instructive: the entry test explicitly grades **soundness over appearance**, and the top test explicitly requires **sloping ground** — because that is where the craft is actually hard. Any wall-trade assessment that tests only a flat, straight, finished-looking sample is testing the wrong thing.

DSWA also runs beginner courses, 4-day intensive training, qualification courses, walling competitions (including a British Grand Prix), and maintains a "Wallers Near Me" register of professional wallers.

### Equivalents elsewhere

- **Dry Stone Walling Association of Canada** and other international groups affiliated with DSWA.
- **UNESCO** inscribed the *art of dry stone walling, knowledge and techniques* on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in **2018**, for Croatia, Cyprus, France, Greece, Italy, Slovenia, Spain and Switzerland; **Ireland** added **2024**. Inscription is recognition of the craft, not a certification of individuals.
- **Southern Africa has no equivalent scheme** for drystone or for boundary walling generally. The Zimbabwe-tradition walling skills that built Great Zimbabwe and Khami (file `01`) survive only through heritage conservation programmes such as the National Museums and Monuments of Zimbabwe restoration work at Khami from the early 2000s.

### What competence looks like

- **Foundation stones bedded on subsoil**, largest and flattest at the bottom, set into the ground.
- A consistent **batter**, controlled by a batter frame and lines, with the wall **diminishing in width as it rises**.
- **Through-stones (tie-stones)** at regular intervals spanning both faces.
- **Hearting hand-placed**, never poured or shovelled in — this is the single clearest tell of a good waller.
- **Every stone touching its neighbours at at least two points**, and no stone rocking.
- **Copes across the full width**, tightly set, over a coverband where the tradition uses one.
- **Cheekends** (wall ends) vertical, tight, and built with the longest stones available.

### Common failures

Poured hearting; running joints (vertical joints continuing through more than one course); no through-stones; a face built plumb instead of battered; small stones used as face and large as hearting (exactly backwards); and building "two thin walls leaning together" without ties.

## 3. The plasterer and renderer

Distinct from the bricklayer and, in southern Africa, often a separate gang.

**Competence looks like:** working to screeds and dots; a single 12–15 mm coat that is flat, without hollows, without crazing; correct mix (**external plaster: 50 kg cement : 150 ℓ sand** with common cement, or 1 : 4,5 by volume; 1 : 3,5 with MC 22,5X or MC 12,5, **never MC 12,5X externally**); dubbing out separately and letting it set; correct bell-cast and stop details; and **curing the plaster for 3–7 days**.

**Output:** 15–22 m² per plasterer-day at 15 mm on masonry. `needs-verification`

**Common failures:**
1. **Crazing and map cracking** — mix too rich in cement, applied too thick, or not cured. The single commonest plaster defect.
2. **Debonding / hollowness** — dusty or dry background, no key, or a second coat richer than the first.
3. **Applying more than ~20 mm in one pass.**
4. **Using masonry cement class MC 12,5X externally.**
5. **Carrying the plaster down to the ground**, so rising moisture blows it off at the base.
6. **No bell-cast or drip** at the base and at the coping.
7. **Over-trowelling**, bringing fines to the surface and producing a dense, crazed skin.

**Lime plastering** is a distinct and much rarer competence (file `05`): it requires understanding carbonatation, keeping the work damp for days, working with a caustic pH 12 material, and never adding more than about 20 % cement. Very few plasterers in southern Africa have it; assume you will have to train the gang and supervise closely.

**How to assess:** run a straight edge over a finished wall of theirs (2 m rule, expect **± 4 mm**); tap the surface across a whole elevation listening for hollowness; look at the base detail and the arrises; and look at a 3-year-old wall for crazing.

## 4. The stonemason — banker and fixer

Two distinct trades sharing a name:

- **Banker mason** — works at a bench (the "banker"), cutting and carving stone to templates: mouldings, copings, cills, quoins, tracery, lettering. Tools: mallet and chisels, points, claws, punches, and now saws and CNC. Competence is measured by accuracy to a template and by the quality of a worked face.
- **Fixer mason** — sets the stone on the building: setting out, lifting, bedding, jointing, cramping and dowelling, pointing. Competence is measured by line, level, joint consistency, and by whether the fixings will outlast the stone.

For a boundary wall, the fixer mason matters most: coping units, gate pier caps, plinth courses and stone facing are all fixing work. Specify **stainless steel cramps and dowels**, not galvanized, for anything intended to last — a corroding cramp splits the stone it holds.

**Assessment:** look at joint width consistency (should be within ± 1 mm on a specified joint), at whether the courses are level over 5 m, at how the coping joints are treated over movement joints, and at any weathering staining pattern, which reveals whether the mason understood which way water would run.

**Historical benchmark:** the **Anōshū**, the stonemasonry group from Ōmi Province, were the foremost Japanese castle builders of the late 16th century and were renowned for the 45° stone bases used for keeps, gatehouses and corner towers. The lesson is organisational as much as technical — walling at that quality was done by an identifiable, named, hereditary group with a transmitted method.

## 5. The rammed earth specialist

An emerging, mostly self-taught or Europe-trained trade, dominated internationally by **Lehm Ton Erde** (Martin Rauch) and its alumni.

**Competence looks like:**
- **Soil science first.** Sieve analysis, Atterberg limits, and a proctor-type compaction test to find optimum moisture content. A specialist who does not test the soil is a labourer with a rammer.
- The **drop test** and the **ribbon test** as field checks on clay content and moisture.
- **Formwork engineering** — plywood or steel, heavily braced and clamped face-to-face, because the bursting forces during compaction are very large.
- **Consistent lift depth** (100–250 mm loose, compacted to ~50 %) and consistent compaction energy across the whole lift, including into corners.
- Knowing that the wall can be **wire-brushed, carved or mould-impressed within about an hour** of striking the form, and not after.
- Detailing the **plinth**, the **coping**, and any **erosion checks**.
- For stabilised work: correct cement dosing (typically 5–10 %), and a **minimum 28-day cure**.

**Common failures:** wrong soil (too much clay → shrinkage cracking; too little → no cohesion); inconsistent moisture between batches, producing colour banding and weak lifts; under-compaction at the base of each lift, giving a visible weak line; formwork deflection producing a bulged wall; and cement render applied afterwards, which destroys the wall.

**Certification:** none formal. Assess on **built work only** — go and look at a wall they built at least three years ago and inspect the base, the top, and the lift lines for erosion.

## 6. The fencing contractor

The trade for palisade, mesh, precast panel walling, and gates.

**Competence looks like:**
- Setting out a long run accurately, including ranging over undulating ground.
- **Correct post-hole depth and diameter, and correct concrete.** This is the whole job — everything else is bolting.
- Straining posts at corners, changes of direction and at intervals on straight runs.
- Concrete domed and falling away from the post.
- Hot-dip galvanized components (SANS 121 / ISO 1461), or a documented paint system with a stated maintenance interval.
- For precast walling: setting posts to the manufacturer's exact centres, letting the concrete gain strength before loading panels, and setting the bottom panel below ground.
- For gates: welding to a recognised standard, correct hinge selection and adjustment, and correct motor sizing for the actual gate mass.

**Common failures:** shallow post holes; posts concreted and loaded the same day; panels forced into wrong spacings; no plinth so animals and people go under; a gate that drags because the pier moved; and a motor sized on gate width instead of mass.

**How to assess:** push hard on the top of a post on one of their finished fences and watch the base. Any movement means the hole was too small or too shallow. Look at the ground line for corrosion. Open and close one of their motorised gates ten times and listen.

## 7. Cross-trade: what "competent" means for a boundary wall

Regardless of trade, a competent boundary-wall builder demonstrates six things:

1. **They set out from a datum and profiles**, not from the neighbour's wall.
2. **They ask about the boundary** before they ask about the wall.
3. **They ask what the ground is** and want to dig a trial hole.
4. **They know where the control joints go** and can say why.
5. **They cure**, and they build curing into their price and programme.
6. **They can explain the coping detail** — overhang, drip, bedding, joints.

If a quotation does not mention foundations, control joints, curing or the coping detail, it is not a price for a boundary wall; it is a price for a stack of blocks.

## 8. Building capacity locally **[NA]**

At Okongo the realistic labour position is a mixed pool: some NTA trade-tested artisans, many informally trained builders, and a strong local blockmaking capability. Practical responses:

- **Pay for a chargehand, not just bricklayers.** The difference between a good and a bad boundary wall is supervision, and it costs one person's wages.
- **Build a sample panel** — 2 m of wall, full height, with a pier, a control joint and the coping — before the main run starts. Approve it, photograph it, and make it the standard for the work. This is worth more than any specification clause.
- **Train on the joint.** Spend an hour demonstrating a full bed and buttered perpends, and check it daily for the first week.
- **Make curing someone's job**, by name, with a bucket and a schedule.
- **Use the NTA route** for any young worker on the job — the wall becomes a training opportunity with a certificate at the end, which is worth more locally than the wall is.
