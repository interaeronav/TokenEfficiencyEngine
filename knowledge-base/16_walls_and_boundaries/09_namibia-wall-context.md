# Walls and boundaries in the Namibian context

**Summary.** Northern Namibia already had a fully worked-out boundary technology before cement arrived: the Owambo homestead enclosure, a stockade of large vertical poles linked by two horizontal poles on each side, forming a maze with two gates. It solved security, livestock control, privacy and social hierarchy with a locally harvested, termite-resistant timber. The modern boundary wall — precast panel, concrete block, or steel palisade — replaced it for reasons that are only partly technical. This file records what is verifiable about the traditional enclosure, what walling materials are actually available in Ohangwena, what the ground and climate demand, and what makes sense for a village project as opposed to what is simply imported urban practice.

## 1. The traditional Owambo homestead enclosure

### What is verified

The traditional Ovambo home is **a complex of huts surrounded by a fence of large vertical poles linked by two horizontal poles on each side**. The complex is **a maze with two gates**, and "it is easy to get lost within the homestead." Each hut has a distinct purpose — the ***ondjugo***, the hut of the woman of the homestead, and the ***epata***, the kitchen area, are named examples.

That short description already contains four significant pieces of technical and social design:

1. **A palisade, not a wall.** Vertical poles set into the ground, so the boundary carries no mass and needs no foundation in the masonry sense. Structurally it is a fence of cantilevered posts — the same load path as a modern precast panel wall (file `02`, §11), and the same vulnerable point: the ground line.
2. **Two horizontal rails on each side** of the vertical poles. This is a genuinely good detail. Rails on both faces sandwich the verticals, so the poles are held in position without being notched or drilled — no weakening cuts, and the assembly can be tightened or repaired by replacing a rail rather than a post. It is the same principle as a modern palisade fence's rails.
3. **A maze plan with only two gates.** Security by disorientation rather than by height. This is a fundamentally different security strategy from the modern high opaque wall: it does not try to make entry physically impossible, it makes an intruder's *route* impossible. It is also the *bent entry* device that Mediterranean and Islamic domestic architecture uses (file `06`, §11) — you never see the whole homestead from the gate.
4. **Programmatic enclosure.** The boundary defines not one space but a sequence of named spaces with different users and different rules. The wall is a social diagram, not just a perimeter.

### The material

The regionally dominant construction timber is **mopane** (*Colophospermum mopane*), recorded in Ovambo as ***omufiadi***. Its properties explain the whole tradition:

- **Density 0,990–1,230 g/cm³ at 12 % moisture content** — among the heaviest woods in southern Africa. Comparable to or heavier than water.
- **Hard and dense, and difficult to work** — which is why the tradition uses whole poles held by rails rather than jointed or cut members.
- **Termite-resistant.** This is the decisive property. In Ohangwena, *Macrotermes* termites destroy untreated softwood in the ground in a season or two. Mopane is "one of the most preferred local timbers for construction" for exactly this reason, and "has long been used for building houses and fences, as railway sleepers and as pit props."
- Mopane grows as tall single-stemmed trees of **10–15 m** on sandy alluvial soils and as multi-stemmed shrub of **1–3 m** on clayey or impermeable alkaline soils. The tall form gives poles; the shrub form does not.

> ⚠️ **Do not romanticise and do not appropriate.** The homestead enclosure is living cultural property and is bound up with lineage, gender roles and traditional authority. If any element of it is to be referenced in a new boundary wall — the pole rhythm, the double-rail detail, the bent entry — do it after a conversation with the client's family and the local traditional authority, and do it as a deliberate reference rather than as a pastiche.

## 2. The transition to modern walling

The sequence in northern Namibia broadly runs:

1. **Pole palisade** — pre-colonial to mid-20th century and still present.
2. **Wire fencing** — diamond mesh, barbed wire and later welded mesh, arriving with colonial administration, veterinary cordon fencing and cash income. Cheap, fast, requires no local material, and demarcates without enclosing.
3. **Concrete blockwork** — following cement availability, cash remittances from the mines and the south, and the shift from round pole-and-thatch houses to rectangular block-and-sheet houses.
4. **Precast panel walling and steel palisade** — arriving from the South African urban market via Oshakati, Ondangwa and Windhoek, carrying the whole security-wall aesthetic with it (file `01`, §13).

The drivers of the shift were, in rough order: **timber scarcity and the labour of harvesting poles**; **termites and fire**; **status** — a block wall signals a cash income and permanence; **land tenure formalisation** — a permanent wall is a claim; and **imported norms** about what a modern house looks like.

The engineering point worth making is that **step 4 is not obviously an improvement on step 1** for a rural plot. A pole palisade costs almost nothing in cash, is repairable by the household, and provides ventilation and sightlines. A 2,1 m precast wall costs a great deal of imported cash, cannot be repaired locally when a post rotates, provides concealment for anyone who gets inside it, and — because most of the money leaves the district — does nothing for local employment.

## 3. What is actually available in Ohangwena

### Verified

- **Cement.** Ohorongo Cement, with a **depot at Ondangwa** (Oshikango main road, TransNamib yard, +264 65 242 076), roughly 200 km west of Okongo, and the **Sargberg plant at North Otavi** near Tsumeb. Products: CEM II B-LL 32.5N, CEM II A-LL 42.5N, CEM II B-V 42.5N, CEM I 52.5N, CEM I 42.5R. All NSI, SABS, BOBS and ISOQAR certified. Project pricing available on volume.
- **Road access.** Okongo is on a **tarred road**, about **120 km east of Eenhana** on the route to Nkurenkuru, with an airstrip for small aircraft. Deliveries by truck are practical.
- **Local timber.** Mopane, where the woodland supports it — Okongo has a **Community Forest and Conservancy** immediately north of the village, which means pole harvesting is regulated. `needs-verification` on current harvesting permits and quotas.

### Very likely, but not verified in this pass

- **Locally made concrete blocks.** Small blockmaking yards using Ohorongo cement and local pit or river sand are the normal source of masonry units in northern Namibia. Machine-made 390 × 190 × 140 and 390 × 190 × 190 hollow blocks and 220 × 110 × 90 solid units are the usual products. `needs-verification`
- **Sand and aggregate.** Fine aeolian Kalahari sand is everywhere; **coarse concrete sand and crushed stone are the scarce items** and are typically trucked from further afield. This is the biggest hidden cost in any concrete work at Okongo. `needs-verification`
- **Steel, mesh, gate hardware, motors.** Available from merchants in Oshakati, Ondangwa and Eenhana, or ordered from Windhoek; almost all originating in South Africa.
- **Retail chains.** Pupkewitz Megabuild and Build It have northern Namibian branches. `needs-verification` on which branches and what they stock.

### Not available locally

- Precast panel-and-post walling manufactured within economic transport distance. `needs-verification` — check Oshakati and Ondangwa; if the nearest manufacturer is in Windhoek or South Africa, the delivered cost of panel walling at Okongo will be dominated by freight and breakage, and block masonry becomes the cheaper option.
- Face brick, natural stone, corten steel, aluminium louvre, natural hydraulic lime, and any specialist finish. All imported with 2–6 week lead times.
- Specialist trades: off-shutter concrete, rammed earth, drystone, lime plastering. None should be assumed available.

## 4. Ground, climate and what they demand

**Ground.** Deep aeolian Kalahari sands — the Kalahari basin covers 900 000 km² and is characterised by "vast areas covered by red sand without any permanent surface water" — locally over calcrete, with the *oshana* system of shallow seasonally inundated depressions crossing the landscape. Consequences for a wall:

- Sands are **free-draining and non-expansive** — good, because there is no heave.
- Sands are **loose and can be collapsible on wetting** — bad, because a shallow footing rotates. See file `03`, §3: found at **450 mm minimum**, 600 mm through loose or made ground, and size the footing on **overturning and eccentricity**, not on bearing.
- **Do not found in an *oshana*** or in its margin without a proper investigation. Seasonal saturation plus loose sand is the worst combination available.
- **Calcrete**, where it occurs, is stiff. Found entirely on it or entirely off it, never partly on — and step the foundation at the transition with a control joint above.
- **Dig trial holes.** Three along a 40 m run, to 1,0 m, costs a morning and prevents the commonest failure.

**Climate.** Köppen **BSh** — hot semi-arid. Summer maxima routinely above 35 °C, very low relative humidity, steady wind, and a short intense wet season. Consequences:

- **Curing is the single largest determinant of wall durability.** Mortar and plaster lose strength irrecoverably if they dry in the first hours. Budget water, cover the work, and make curing someone's named responsibility (file `04`, §10).
- **Thermal movement is large.** Control joints at the code spacing are not optional (file `03`, §4).
- **Wind on open flat terrain** gives higher design pressures at 2 m than a suburban site does. The SANS 10400-K tables assume the wall is not on an exposed crest; flat open ground is within their scope, but do not assume a large margin.
- **Windblown sand** abrades finishes at the base and fills gate tracks. Favour a cantilever sliding gate over a tracked one; detail the wall base robustly.
- **Efundja** — the seasonal flood in the *oshana* system — can cut roads. Programme deliveries outside the wet season.

**Termites.** Assume any untreated softwood in ground contact will be destroyed. Timber elements must be mopane, CCA/creosote-treated to a ground-contact hazard class, or held clear of the ground on steel or masonry.

## 5. Security norms

Namibia's rural security context is not Johannesburg's, and importing Johannesburg's wall is a category error.

- The realistic threats on a rural Ohangwena plot are **opportunistic theft of tools, materials, livestock and moveables**, and stock straying in or out — not armed home invasion.
- The traditional response — enclosure, a bent entry, dogs, and the fact that everyone in a village knows who is where — remains effective and is nearly free.
- The imported response — a 2,1 m opaque wall with electric fence — **removes passive surveillance** from the road, which in a village is the strongest security asset the plot has.
- **Livestock control** is a genuine, continuous requirement and is usually the honest reason a rural boundary is built. It needs about **1,2–1,5 m** of stock-proof barrier, not 2,1 m of masonry.
- The **materials stockpile during construction** is the highest-risk period on the whole project, and it is better solved with a locked container and a night watchman than with a wall built early.

A defensible position for the Okongo project: **build to 1,8 m in masonry on the road frontage and at the gates** — where appearance, dust, privacy and status all matter — and use a **stock-proof fence or a pole-and-rail palisade** on the remaining boundaries, at a fraction of the cost. Layer it as file `06`, §10 describes: transparent at the perimeter, solid close to the courtyard.

## 6. Costs

**No verified price data was obtained for Namibian materials or labour in this research pass.** Every figure must come from a live quotation. `needs-verification`

What can be said about **cost structure** rather than cost level:

| Component | Where the money goes | Local content |
|---|---|---|
| Concrete block masonry wall | Cement (Ohorongo, Ondangwa), local sand, local blocks, local labour | **High — 60–80 %** |
| Precast panel-and-post | Manufacturer + freight + specialist crew | **Low — 20–35 %** |
| Steel palisade | Imported steel + fabrication (Oshakati/Windhoek) + galvanizing | **Low — 20–30 %** |
| Diamond mesh fence | Imported wire + local posts + local labour | Medium |
| Pole palisade | Permit + harvesting + local labour | **Very high — 90 %+** |
| Gate + automation | Imported hardware and motor + specialist installation | **Very low — 10–20 %** |

Local content estimates are judgement, not measurement. `needs-verification`

The practical implications:

1. **Block masonry is the option that circulates the most money in Ohangwena** and creates the most local employment and skills. On a project where the client lives in the district, that is a substantive argument, not a sentimental one.
2. **Freight dominates any imported product.** Before pricing precast walling, get a **delivered-to-Okongo** price including breakage, not an ex-works price.
3. **Coarse aggregate and concrete sand are the hidden cost.** Get a delivered rate per m³ before designing anything with a lot of concrete in it.
4. **Curing water has a price.** If water is trucked, put it in the bill of quantities as a line item (file `10`), or it will be skipped.
5. **The gate is disproportionately expensive.** A 4 m automated vehicle gate with beams, intercom and a battery can easily cost more than 20 m of wall. Design the wall around one good gate rather than two mediocre ones.

## 7. Local blockmaking

Blockmaking on or near site is normal in northern Namibia and is worth taking seriously rather than treating as a fallback.

**What it needs:**
- A **block machine** — manual egg-layer or a semi-automatic vibrating press.
- **Cement** — Ohorongo CEM II B-LL 32.5N is the appropriate class for blockmaking.
- **Sand** — this is the constraint. Blockmaking needs a graded sand with coarse particles up to about 5 mm; fine aeolian dune sand alone makes weak, high-shrinkage blocks. Blend with pit sand or crushed fines.
- **Stone** — maximum particle size **13 mm** for concrete bricks and solid blocks, **10 mm** for hollow blocks (SANS 1215 practice).
- **Water and a curing area.** Blocks must be **cured for at least 7 days** and should be **at least 28 days old** before being built into a wall, or their shrinkage will crack it.
- **Quality control.** Test cubes or crushed blocks. SANS 10400-K requires **≥ 3,0 MPa hollow / 5,0 MPa solid** for freestanding walls — a locally made block that does not reach this is not fit for a boundary wall, whatever it is fit for.

Cement & Concrete SA publishes *How to make concrete bricks and blocks*, which is the right reference. **[NA]** Certification of locally made units would be through the **Namibian Standards Institution (NSI)**; in practice, small yards are uncertified, so **test them yourself** — crush ten blocks and measure ten for dimension before ordering ten thousand.

## 8. What makes sense at Okongo — and what does not

### Makes sense

1. **Locally made concrete block masonry**, 220 mm at 1,8 m or 220 mm with 660 × 440 piers at 3,2 m for 2,1 m, on a properly sized footing, with real control joints, brickforce, and a real coping (file `03`).
2. **Bagged and painted, or bagged and limewashed**, rather than full plaster — cheaper, less water, more forgiving of imperfect blockwork, and better-looking than a mediocre plaster job.
3. **The wall concentrated where it does work** — road frontage, gates, courtyard — with fence elsewhere.
4. **One good gate**, well founded, well hinged, with a battery-backed Centurion operator sized on real mass, beams fitted, and conduit laid before the driveway.
5. **Screen-block or timber-slat panels** between piers on the courtyard side, for shade, air and privacy without mass (file `06`).
6. **Solar-maintained** lighting and gate operation, independent of the grid.
7. **A sample panel** built and approved before the main run, and **an NTA trainee** on the job.
8. **A written boundary agreement** with neighbours and the traditional authority before setting out.

### Does not make sense

1. **A 2,4 m opaque wall with electric fence around the whole plot.** Imported urban practice, expensive, removes passive surveillance, and disproportionate to the actual threat.
2. **Precast panel walling** unless a manufacturer within economic freight distance can be found and a delivered price obtained.
3. **Face brick**, natural stone cladding, corten, aluminium louvre — all imported, all with long lead times, none justified on a village plot.
4. **A rammed-earth wall** without a specialist, without soil testing, and without accepting a 5–8 % cement content that undermines the environmental argument.
5. **Any wall element that depends on annual maintenance** that will not happen — including the traditional rounded lime coping of file `05`.
6. **A gate motor from a brand with no service presence north of Windhoek.**
7. **Founding at 300 mm in loose sand** because "that's what everyone does here." It is also why the walls lean.
