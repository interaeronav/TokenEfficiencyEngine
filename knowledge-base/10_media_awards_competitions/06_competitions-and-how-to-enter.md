# Architectural competitions and how to enter

**Summary.** Competitions are the only mechanism in architecture by which an unknown practice can win a real commission on the strength of an idea alone. They come in three structural types — open, invited, and two-stage — and two economic types: **procurement competitions** run by clients who intend to build, and **commercial ideas competitions** run by platforms that make their money from entry fees. Both are worth entering, but for entirely different reasons. The rulebook that protects fair competition is the **UNESCO-UIA regulations**, created by the UIA in 1956 and subsequently ratified by UNESCO; UIA endorsement functions as a quality label confirming a brief complies with it.

## How competitions work

### The three structural types

**1. Open competition.** Anyone meeting the eligibility criteria may enter. Entries are **anonymous** — identified by a registration number only, with no names, logos or identifying marks anywhere on the boards. A jury assesses all entries and selects winners. This is the classic, meritocratic form and the only one where an unknown can win.

**2. Invited (limited/restricted) competition.** The client selects a shortlist of practices — usually 3 to 8 — and pays each an **honorarium** to develop a scheme. Selection into the shortlist is by portfolio and prequalification. Entries are usually not anonymous. This is how most significant public buildings are actually procured in Europe.

**3. Two-stage competition.** Stage 1 is open and anonymous, producing a shortlist on the strength of a concept presented on one or two boards. Stage 2 pays the shortlisted teams to develop the design further, often with a jury interview. **This is the best format for a small practice**: the barrier at Stage 1 is a single good idea well drawn, and Stage 2 is paid.

A fourth, non-structural distinction: **project competitions** (the client intends to build) versus **ideas competitions** (no client, no site rights, no intention to build). Ideas competitions produce portfolio material and publication, never a building.

### The UNESCO-UIA framework

The **UIA created standardised competition regulations in 1956**, subsequently ratified by UNESCO. The framework rests on three principles: **design quality, transparency, and impartial treatment of all competitors**, with anonymity and professional jury evaluation as the operative mechanisms.

UIA endorsement means the UIA has reviewed the brief and confirmed it provides fair conditions and equitable treatment. It functions, in the UIA's own framing, as a **quality label**. Organisers submit competitions to the UIA for validation.

**Practical test before you enter anything:**

| Question | Bad answer |
|---|---|
| Is the jury named, with credentials, before the deadline? | "Jury to be announced" |
| Who owns the intellectual property of submitted entries? | The organiser owns all entries |
| Is the prize fund guaranteed, or contingent on entry numbers? | "Prize fund subject to sufficient registrations" |
| Is there a real client and a real site? | Neither |
| Are results published with jury comments? | Winners announced with no reasoning |
| Is anonymity enforced? | Names required on boards |

Failing two or more of these means the competition is an entry-fee business, not a competition. That can still be worth entering — for the deadline discipline and the portfolio piece — but price it accordingly.

## The commercial platform economics

Be clear-eyed about the arithmetic. A platform charging, say, €100 per team across 400 teams collects €40 000 and pays out a €20 000 prize fund. That is a viable business and a **negative expected value for the individual entrant**. Entering a commercial ideas competition to make money is irrational. Entering for these reasons is rational:

1. **Forced completion.** A hard external deadline is the only reliable way to finish speculative work.
2. **Portfolio material** that is not client work — essential for a new practice with nothing built.
3. **Staff development.** A three-week internal competition is the cheapest design-training exercise available.
4. **Publication.** Winners and honourable mentions are published by the platform and often picked up by ArchDaily, Dezeen and — in YAC's case — *Casabella* and *The Plan*.
5. **Discipline in graphic communication**, which transfers directly to client presentations and award entries.

Set an annual competition budget, treat it as marketing and training spend, and stop when it is exhausted.

## Preparing a winning entry

### The single most important fact about competition judging

A jury of six people assessing 400 entries over two days spends, on average, **well under a minute per entry in the first pass**. Most entries are eliminated in that first pass. Everything below follows from that.

### Board composition

**Structure the board as a decision tree, not as a document.** The jury reads in this order and stops as soon as it loses interest:

1. **The hero image** — one large, unambiguous, atmospheric image that states the idea. Top of the board, occupying 30–50% of the area. If the jury understands the project from this image alone, you have survived the first pass.
2. **The diagram** — one diagram, not six, showing the single generative move. If your idea needs five diagrams to explain, your idea is not yet one idea.
3. **The site plan** — orientation, north point, scale bar, context. Juries need to know *where* before they can assess *what*.
4. **Plan and section** — the plan that proves the idea works, and the section that proves it stands up and is inhabitable.
5. **Supporting images and detail** — the last thing read, if at all.

Hard rules:

- **One idea per entry.** Entries that hedge between two concepts lose to entries that commit to one.
- **Text is read last and briefly.** A 200-word statement, a title, and labels. Nothing else. Long text on a board is not read.
- **Legibility at A3.** Juries frequently review printed A3 reductions of A1/A0 boards, and increasingly review on screen. Test your board printed at A3 and viewed at 25% on a monitor. If line weights vanish or text is unreadable, redo it.
- **Line weight hierarchy.** Cut lines heaviest, then edges, then surface, then hatch. This one discipline separates professional from student boards more visibly than anything else.
- **Consistent scale bars and north points** on every orthographic drawing.
- **Restrained colour.** A limited palette reads as deliberate; a full-spectrum board reads as noise.

### Narrative

The written statement should answer four questions in this order, in about 200 words:

1. **What is the situation?** (the site/problem, one sentence)
2. **What is the move?** (the single idea, one sentence — this is the sentence the jury will quote when arguing for you)
3. **How does it work?** (organisation, structure, materials, environment — three or four sentences)
4. **What does it change?** (the consequence, one sentence)

Avoid: "the project seeks to explore", "a dialogue between", "the interstitial condition", "weaving together". Juries have read these thousands of times. Write plainly. A jury argues *for* an entry using its own words; give them usable sentences.

### Drawing selection

| Include | Reason |
|---|---|
| One site plan | Establishes context and orientation |
| One or two plans at a legible scale | Proves the organisation |
| **One long section** through the most important spatial condition | Proves the section idea — the most under-used and most persuasive drawing |
| One diagram of the generative idea | Lets the jury restate your idea to each other |
| One or two atmospheric visualisations | Emotional persuasion |
| One construction/detail fragment (if the brief is technical) | Signals competence |

| Omit | Reason |
|---|---|
| Every plan of every floor | Nobody reads floors 3–7 |
| Elevations of all four sides | Rarely persuade; the section does the work |
| Programme bubble diagrams | Read as unresolved |
| Aerial renders with no people | Read as unconsidered |
| Extensive precedent analysis | The jury knows the precedents |

### Model photography

Physical models photograph well and are under-used in an era of rendered images — a well-photographed model reads as *considered* in a way a render does not.

- **Single light source**, raking, to give a clear shadow direction. Diffuse it through tracing paper or a white sheet.
- **Neutral seamless background** — white or mid-grey paper curved up behind the model to eliminate the horizon line.
- **Shoot at eye level of the model's occupant**, not from above. A model photographed from a standing person's eye height looks like a model; photographed from its own scale it looks like a building.
- **Deep depth of field** — small aperture, tripod, long exposure. Focus stacking if available.
- **No visible base edges, cutting mats, desks or hands.**
- Shoot **RAW**, correct perspective in post, and keep verticals vertical.

### Common disqualification traps

> ⚠️ Disqualification is almost always automatic and non-appealable. Most disqualifications are administrative, not qualitative.

| Trap | How to avoid |
|---|---|
| **Any identifying mark on the boards** — practice name, logo, author name, university name, even in file metadata or a title block | Strip all names. **Check PDF metadata** — exported file properties frequently carry the author's name |
| **Registration number missing or in the wrong position** | Most briefs specify the exact corner and size. Follow it literally |
| **Wrong file format, size, resolution or naming convention** | Read the submission section of the brief twice; name files exactly as specified |
| **Exceeding the board count or changing orientation** | Two A1 landscape means two, A1, landscape |
| **File size over the upload limit** | Test the upload well before the deadline; compress raster images, not vectors |
| **Late submission** | Deadlines are in a named timezone (frequently CET/CEST or GMT). Convert to **[ZA]**/**[NA]** local time and submit 24 hours early. Servers fail at deadlines |
| **Registering but not submitting, or submitting without registering** | These are separate steps on almost every platform |
| **Ineligible team composition** | Some competitions require a registered architect on the team; some are students-only; some exclude nationals of the host country |
| **Using copyrighted images or unlicensed entourage** | Use licensed or self-made assets |
| **Missing the required written statement or exceeding its word limit** | Word limits are enforced by juries reading the first N words and stopping |

### A working timeline for a three-month open competition

| Week | Activity |
|---|---|
| 1 | Read the brief twice. Build a **compliance checklist** from the submission requirements. Register (lowest fee tier) |
| 2 | Site and context research; precedent scan; decide the single idea |
| 3–4 | Test the idea in section and plan; sketch models |
| 5–6 | Resolve organisation; freeze the design. **Design freeze must happen at the halfway point** |
| 7–8 | Produce drawings at final line weights; build and photograph the physical model |
| 9–10 | Visualisation |
| 11 | Board layout; write the 200-word statement; internal review by someone who has not seen the project |
| 12 | **Compliance check against the week-1 checklist**; strip metadata; test-print at A3; submit **at least 48 hours early** |

The two non-negotiable items are the **week-1 compliance checklist** and the **halfway design freeze**. Entries fail far more often from running out of time than from having a weak idea.
