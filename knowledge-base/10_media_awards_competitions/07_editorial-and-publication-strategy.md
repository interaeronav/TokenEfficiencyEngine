# Editorial and publication strategy for built work

**Summary.** Getting a building published is an operational problem, not a creative one. Editors are understaffed and receive far more submissions than they can use; they publish the projects that arrive **complete, correct and easy to process**. The three decisive factors, in order, are: professional photography, a complete and accurate press kit, and a project description written the way editors actually write. A practice that gets these right will be published repeatedly; a practice with better buildings and worse packaging will not.

## Key facts

| Requirement | Standard expectation |
|---|---|
| Photography | **Professional, commissioned, high-resolution.** This is the hard gate. Phone photographs are rejected regardless of building quality |
| Image count | 10–25 photographs for a full feature; a hero image that works cropped to both landscape and square |
| Image resolution | Minimum ~2 000 px on the long edge for web; 300 dpi at print size for magazines. Supply the largest available |
| Drawings | Site plan, floor plans, at least one section, sometimes elevations and a detail. **Blackline on white, cleaned of title blocks, dimensions and layer clutter, exported as high-resolution PNG or vector PDF** |
| Text | 300–800 words for digital; 150–250 words as a short version for news items |
| Credits | Full project credit list including photographer, with correct spelling |
| Eligibility (ArchDaily) | **Built projects completed after 2014**; region-specific submission requirements |
| Cost | **Free** to submit to ArchDaily, Dezeen, Designboom, Architizer. Divisare operates a reader subscription model |
| Lead time | Digital: days to weeks. Print magazines: 2–6 months. Annuals: up to a year |

## The photography requirement — the single decisive investment

Nothing else in this file matters if the photography is not professional. Every publication and every award in files `01`–`06` is judged from images.

**Budget it into the project.** For a small practice the rational move is to treat architectural photography as a line item on every project worth publishing, agreed with the client at handover stage — not as a discretionary marketing spend afterwards, by which time the building is occupied, furnished badly and no longer photographable.

### What to commission

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Shot list | Agree in advance: exterior context, exterior detail, the primary interior space, the sequence/approach, at least one detail of craft, and one image with a person in it for scale and life |
| Time of day | Two sessions minimum — morning and late afternoon/dusk. Dusk exteriors with interior lighting on are the images that get run as heroes |
| Verticals | Must be corrected. Converging verticals read as amateur and are the most common reason an otherwise good image is rejected |
| People | Editors increasingly want inhabited buildings. One or two images with people, used naturally |
| Styling | Remove clutter, cables, bins, signage. Agree with the client beforehand — this takes time on the day |
| Deliverables | High-resolution TIFF/JPEG masters, plus web-sized JPEGs, plus a caption/credit sheet |

### Licensing — the trap that ends publications

> ⚠️ Architectural photographers usually license images for specific uses, not outright. A licence limited to "the practice's own website and social media" **does not permit a magazine to publish**, and a magazine that discovers the licence is inadequate will drop the story.

Negotiate, in writing, at commissioning:

- The right to **supply images to press free of charge for editorial use**, with the photographer credited.
- The right to use images in **award and competition submissions**.
- Whether the photographer retains the right to license the images separately (they usually do, and that is normal).
- Whether the client has any approval right over publication.

Always credit the photographer. Failing to credit is the fastest way to lose access to a photographer and to be blacklisted informally within a small professional community.

## Press-kit contents

A complete press kit is a single, clearly named, downloadable folder (Dropbox, Google Drive or WeTransfer link — ArchDaily specifically asks for Dropbox or Google Drive). It contains:

```
ProjectName_PracticeName_PressKit/
├── 01_Text/
│   ├── project-description-long.docx      (400–800 words)
│   ├── project-description-short.docx     (150–250 words)
│   └── credits-and-factsheet.docx
├── 02_Photographs/
│   ├── 01_hero.jpg                        (highest resolution)
│   ├── 02...25_*.jpg                       (numbered in reading order)
│   └── photo-credits.txt
├── 03_Drawings/
│   ├── site-plan.png / .pdf
│   ├── ground-floor-plan.png
│   ├── upper-floor-plan.png
│   ├── section-aa.png
│   ├── elevation.png                       (optional)
│   └── detail.png                          (optional)
└── 04_Practice/
    ├── practice-profile-100-words.txt
    └── practice-logo.png
```

Rules that matter:

- **Number the images in the order you want them read.** Editors will usually keep your sequence. The first image is the hero.
- **Drawings must be cleaned.** Strip title blocks, revision clouds, dimension strings, grid bubbles you do not need, and any CAD layer noise. Add a scale bar and a north point. Black lines on transparent or white background. Poor drawing preparation is the second most common reason a project is passed over after poor photography.
- **The factsheet is not optional.** Editors need: project name, location (city and country), architect, completion year, gross floor area in m², client (if named), and the full consultant and contractor list.
- **No ZIP files bigger than necessary**, and no folders with spaces or special characters in the names.

## Writing the project description

Editors rewrite very little. What you send is close to what runs. Write it the way the publication writes.

**Structure — 400 to 800 words:**

1. **One-sentence opening that states what the building is, where, and what is unusual about it.** This sentence often becomes the standfirst. "A 240 m² clinic in Otjiwarongo built from stabilised earth blocks made on site" is publishable. "The project explores the notion of threshold" is not.
2. **The brief and the constraint** — what the client needed and what made it difficult. Constraint is the engine of an interesting story: budget, climate, site, materials availability, remoteness.
3. **The move** — the design response, in plain language.
4. **Organisation** — how the plan works, in two or three sentences.
5. **Materials and construction** — what it is made of and how. Name the materials specifically; editors and readers want "burnt clay brick from a kiln 40 km away", not "locally sourced materials".
6. **Environmental strategy** — passive measures first, then systems. Give numbers where you have them.
7. **Closing** — what the building does for its users or its place.

**Rules:**

- **Third person, past tense for construction, present tense for the building.**
- **No jargon.** Strike "interrogate", "articulate", "dialogue", "porosity", "liminal", "programmatic", "the architecture seeks to".
- **Numbers are content.** Area, span, budget, U-values, thermal mass, number of users, construction duration, distance materials travelled. Numbers are what make a description feel real.
- **Name the people.** Contractor, joiner, mason, engineer. Editors like specificity and it is generous to your collaborators.
- **Include one quotable sentence from the architect.** Many outlets will run it as a pull quote.

**[NA]**/**[ZA]** A specifically regional note: international editors are actively looking for well-documented southern African work and there is far less of it in their inboxes than European or Asian work. **The competitive field is thinner than practices assume.** What kills southern African submissions is almost never the building — it is amateur photography, unclean drawings and a description written in student language.

## Embargoes and exclusives

**Embargo.** You send the material in advance on the condition that nothing is published before a stated date and time. Use an embargo when you want several outlets to publish simultaneously — for a competition win, a practice launch, or a high-profile completion.

- State it unambiguously in the first line of the email: `EMBARGOED UNTIL 09:00 SAST, Tuesday 14 October 2026`.
- Give at least 3–5 working days' notice.
- Embargoes are honoured by professional outlets and routinely broken by aggregators. Do not embargo anything whose early release would cause real harm.

**Exclusive.** One outlet gets first publication, usually in exchange for better treatment — a longer piece, a better slot, an interview.

| Give an exclusive when | Do not give an exclusive when |
|---|---|
| The outlet is high-value (Dezeen, *AR*, *Domus*) and has said it will run a substantial piece | You have not confirmed the outlet will actually publish |
| The project is strong enough that one good feature beats five thin ones | The project is ordinary — take the volume instead |
| You can wait; exclusives slow everything down | You have an award deadline that needs prior publication |

> ⚠️ Never offer the same project as an "exclusive" to two outlets. Editorial communities are small and this ends relationships permanently.

**Practical middle path:** offer an exclusive to one high-value outlet with a deadline attached ("if you would like it exclusively, I can hold it until 30 September"). If they decline or do not reply by that date, release to everyone simultaneously.

## Building a publication record over time

Publication compounds. Editors publish practices they have published before, because the material arrives correctly formatted and the previous piece performed. Awards juries recognise names they have seen. The mechanism is cumulative, so treat it as a standing operational routine rather than a campaign.

**A practice publication routine:**

| Frequency | Action |
|---|---|
| At every project handover | Book photography. Agree licensing and client consent in writing |
| Within 4 weeks of handover | Build the press kit while the drawings are still current and the team remembers the details |
| Immediately | Submit to ArchDaily and the relevant local trade title |
| Within 3 months | Submit to two international digital outlets and one local professional title |
| Annually | Review the year's completions against open award entries; enter the cheap local ones first (file `05`) |
| Continuously | Maintain a **master project archive**: photographs, final drawings, descriptions, credits, published links. This is the raw material for every future award, competition, portfolio and PQQ |
| Continuously | Keep a **media contact list** with editor names, publications, what each ran of yours, and when |

**Three things that accelerate a record disproportionately:**

1. **Write for the trade press.** A 700-word technical opinion piece on, say, thermal performance of alternative walling in Namibian conditions is easier to place than a project feature, positions you as a source, and gets you an editor relationship. Editors then come to you for comment — and comment leads to project features.
2. **Photograph small projects properly.** A small, cheap, well-photographed building is far more publishable than a large, badly photographed one. Scale is not the variable editors optimise for.
3. **Be reliably responsive.** An editor on deadline who gets a high-resolution image within an hour will come back. One who waits three days will not.

## Common failure modes

| Failure | Fix |
|---|---|
| Phone photographs | Commission a professional; there is no workaround |
| Drawings straight out of CAD with title blocks and dimensions | Prepare clean publication drawings as a standard step at project close-out |
| No section supplied | Always supply at least one section — it is the drawing that most distinguishes serious submissions |
| Description written in academic language | Rewrite plainly, with numbers |
| Photographer not credited | Credit in every caption, every time |
| Images too small | Supply masters, not web exports |
| Submitting a 12-year-old project | Check completion-date eligibility (ArchDaily: after 2014) |
| Chasing editors repeatedly | One follow-up after two weeks, then move on |
| No client consent for a private house | Obtain written consent at handover |
| Submitting to everyone with a mail-merge | Personalise at least the first sentence; editors can tell |
