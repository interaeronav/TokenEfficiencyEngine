# The medical field — domain map and disclaimer

**Summary.** This domain is a reference on *how the medical profession trains, what it reads, and how medicine is made*. It maps the training pathway from school-leaver to independent specialist across the US, UK, South Africa, Namibia, Europe and Australia; the basic and clinical sciences a doctor actually studies and the books they study them from; the machinery of evidence — trial design, statistics, appraisal tools, journals, guidelines; the industrial pipeline that turns a molecular target into a licensed medicine; the practice of doing research; and the health-system context in which all of it operates. It is an educational and bibliographic resource, not a clinical decision tool.

> ⚠️ **Disclaimer — read this first.** Nothing in this domain is medical advice. No file here diagnoses, treats, or recommends treatment for any person. Diagnostic and therapeutic frameworks are described at the level of a textbook chapter or a curriculum outline, so that a reader understands *how clinicians are taught to think*, not so that a reader can act as one. There are deliberately no dosing protocols, no self-treatment instructions, and no personalised guidance. Any clinical decision — for yourself or anyone else — requires a qualified, registered clinician who has examined the patient and has access to the full history. If you are unwell, contact a doctor or emergency service. If a statement here conflicts with your clinician, your clinician is right and this file is wrong.

## The structure of the profession

Medicine is unusual among knowledge professions in that entry, progression and continued practice are all separately gatekept, by different bodies, using different instruments.

**1. The university** confers the primary medical qualification — MD or DO in the United States, MBBS/MBChB in the British-derived systems (UK, Ireland, South Africa, Namibia, India, Australia, most of the Commonwealth), and various national equivalents in Europe. This degree alone almost never permits independent practice anywhere.

**2. The licensing authority** grants the right to practise. In the US this is a state medical board, with the USMLE (for MDs) or COMLEX-USA (for DOs) as the national examination sequence and the FSMB and NBME as its co-sponsors. In the UK it is the **General Medical Council (GMC)**, which grants provisional registration on graduation and full registration after the first Foundation year. **[ZA]** In South Africa it is the **Health Professions Council of South Africa (HPCSA)**, established under the Health Professions Act No. 56 of 1974. **[NA]** In Namibia it is the **Health Professions Councils of Namibia (HPCNA)** — an umbrella of five boards, of which the **Medical and Dental Board** registers doctors.

**3. The postgraduate training body** controls specialty formation. In the US, the **ACGME** accredits residency and fellowship programmes and the **ABMS** member boards certify individuals. In the UK, the Royal Colleges (RCP, RCS, RCPsych, RCOG, RCPCH, RCGP and others) set and run the postgraduate examinations while the GMC awards the Certificate of Completion of Training (CCT) and holds the Specialist Register. **[ZA]** In South Africa the **Colleges of Medicine of South Africa (CMSA)**, founded 1954, is the national examining body across all specialties, and universities award a parallel MMed; HPCSA then registers the specialist.

**4. The maintenance regime** keeps the licence alive. The US uses **Maintenance of Certification (MOC)** run by the individual boards, now largely delivered as longitudinal assessment; the UK uses **revalidation**, a five-yearly GMC process built on annual appraisal, supporting information and a recommendation from a Responsible Officer; South Africa uses **HPCSA Continuing Professional Development (CPD)** points.

Layered across all of this is a fifth actor that no one elects: the **evidence system** — journals, systematic reviewers, guideline committees, regulators and the pharmaceutical industry — which determines what the trained doctor is supposed to *do*. Files 05 to 08 are about that system.

## Three intellectual traditions inside one profession

It helps to see modern medicine as the uneasy fusion of three distinct traditions, because the curriculum, the literature and the specialty map all bear their fingerprints.

- **The anatomico-pathological tradition** — from Vesalius (1543) through Morgagni, Bichat and Virchow — holds that disease is a lesion in a place. It produced surgery, pathology, radiology, and the habit of asking "where is it?" before "what is it?"
- **The physiological-experimental tradition** — Harvey, Bernard, Starling, Cannon — holds that disease is a derangement of function that can be measured and modelled. It produced internal medicine, endocrinology, intensive care, and the habit of reasoning from mechanism.
- **The statistical-populational tradition** — Louis's *méthode numérique*, Snow, Fisher, Bradford Hill, Cochrane, Sackett — holds that what matters is what happens to groups of patients under one policy versus another. It produced the randomised controlled trial, epidemiology, public health, and evidence-based medicine.

These traditions frequently disagree. Mechanistic reasoning said antiarrhythmics should reduce mortality after myocardial infarction; the CAST trial showed the opposite. Anatomical reasoning said arthroscopic debridement should help osteoarthritic knees; sham-controlled trials disagreed. A large part of what a modern doctor is taught in file 05 is *how to know which tradition to trust in a given case*.

## What this domain deliberately does not do

It does not contain: drug doses, titration schedules, resuscitation algorithms presented as usable protocols, diagnostic criteria presented as a checklist for self-diagnosis, or any advice framed to a particular person's situation. Where a framework is described — the SIRS/qSOFA idea, illness scripts, the differential for chest pain — it is described to illustrate *how clinicians are trained to structure a problem*, at the abstraction level of an educational overview, and it is not sufficient or intended for use in care.

It also does not pretend to be current on clinical guidelines. Guidelines change on a 2–5 year cycle and diverge between countries (see `06`). The correct move for any actual clinical question is the point-of-care resources listed in `04` — UpToDate, BMJ Best Practice, DynaMed, the BNF — used by someone licensed to act on them.

## The pipeline of knowledge, end to end

It is worth seeing the whole chain in one view, because each file in this domain sits at one link of it and the failure modes at each link explain most of what goes wrong downstream.

1. **A biological question** is asked — usually because a disease has no good treatment, or because a genetic observation suggests a mechanism (`07`, target identification).
2. **Basic research** in laboratories, mostly publicly funded, produces a candidate mechanism. This is the stage with the worst reproducibility record in the whole chain: industry attempts to rebuild on published preclinical oncology found only about one paper in ten confirmable (`05`).
3. **Translation** turns a mechanism into a molecule, a biologic, a cell or a gene edit, through screening and optimisation (`07`).
4. **Human trials** test it, at escalating scale and cost, under a regulatory and ethical regime built out of the 20th century's abuses (`07`, `08`).
5. **Regulators** license it, or do not, on a benefit–risk judgement that is partly scientific and partly political (`07`).
6. **Payers and HTA bodies** decide whether it is affordable, which is a different question and is answered differently in England, Germany, the United States and South Africa (`09`).
7. **Journals** publish the evidence — selectively, with a bias toward positive and novel results, and with a spin problem in the abstracts (`05`, `06`).
8. **Systematic reviewers and guideline committees** synthesise it, grade it, and turn it into recommendations that are then contested (`05`, `06`).
9. **Educators** compress it into a curriculum and a set of textbooks whose editions lag the literature by years (`02`, `03`, `04`).
10. **Clinicians** apply it, under time pressure, to individual patients who resemble the trial population imperfectly or not at all (`03`).
11. **Pharmacovigilance** catches the harms that trials were too small and too short to see, sometimes decades later (`07`).

Every one of those eleven links has a documented, characteristic failure mode, and a reader who understands the chain can usually locate a claim's weakest point without knowing the subject matter.

## Numbers that recur across this domain

A small set of quantities appear again and again, and are worth holding:

- **A decade and a billion dollars, at minimum,** to produce one licensed medicine — and roughly nine failures for every success once human dosing begins.
- **Ten to seventeen years** from school-leaving to independent specialist practice, in every system described here.
- **Absolute versus relative risk.** A "50% reduction" is compatible with a number-needed-to-treat of 10 or of 200, depending entirely on baseline risk. This single distinction resolves more misleading medical claims than any other piece of arithmetic (`05`).
- **Bias does not shrink with sample size.** A larger biased study is a more confidently wrong study.
- **The income gradient in disease.** In low-income countries eight of the ten leading causes of death are communicable; in high-income countries nine of ten are not. A curriculum written for one is partly wrong for the other (`09`).
- **Maldistribution beats absolute shortage.** **[ZA]** 79% of doctors serve 16% of the population; **[NA]** 38% of health professionals serve 80% of it (`09`, `10`).

## What makes medicine different from the other technical domains here

Readers arriving from the engineering, software or aviation domains of this knowledge base will find several structural differences worth naming explicitly.

**The system under study cannot be specified.** An aircraft has a design; a human being has an evolutionary history. There is no schematic, the variation between instances is large and mostly unmeasured, and the mechanism is only ever partially known. Consequently medicine relies far more heavily on statistical evidence about populations, and far less on reasoning from first principles, than any comparably technical discipline.

**Experiments on the system are ethically constrained.** The strongest inference — randomise and observe — is unavailable for most harms, for most rare outcomes, and for anything where withholding a known benefit would be wrong. Much of `05` is a toolkit for extracting causal claims from data that could not be generated by experiment.

**Individual decisions must be made before the evidence is adequate.** An engineer can decline to sign off. A clinician facing a patient at 03:00 must act on the evidence that exists, which is usually thin, often derived from a different population, and sometimes contradictory. The professional skill being trained in `03` is decision-making under irreducible uncertainty, not the elimination of uncertainty.

**The literature is enormous, adversarially incentivised and partly wrong.** Roughly a million and a half new records a year, a publication system that rewards positive novelty, an industry with a legitimate commercial interest in particular conclusions, and a documented replication problem. `06` is about navigating that, and the appropriate stance is neither credulity nor cynicism but calibrated appraisal.

**Regulation is a first-class object.** In most technical fields standards constrain the artefact. In medicine, regulation constrains the *evidence* — what must be shown, to whom, before a thing may be sold or done — and the regulator's decisions are themselves contested scientific judgements (`07`).

**Access is the binding constraint far more often than knowledge.** A great deal of avoidable death worldwide is caused not by the absence of effective treatment but by its absence *here, for this person, at a price they can pay*. That is a health-systems fact rather than a medical one, which is why `09` exists in a domain otherwise about training and evidence.
