# The medical specialties map — what each does, training, and the workforce crisis

**Summary.** A register of the medical specialties and subspecialties: what each actually does day to day, how long training takes, how competitive entry is, what the working life looks like, and where each sits on the procedural–cognitive spectrum. It covers the emerging fields — interventional radiology, clinical informatics, genomic medicine, sports and exercise medicine, aerospace medicine, occupational medicine — and closes with an honest account of burnout and the workforce crisis.

> ⚠️ Nothing here is medical advice, and none of it is career advice for a specific person. Competitiveness and lifestyle characterisations are generalisations that vary enormously by country, institution, subspecialty and individual.

## 1. How specialties are classified

Four axes, all of them used simultaneously:

- **Surgical versus internal-medicine** — surgical specialties involve major operative technique; internal-medicine specialties diagnose and treat non-operatively.
- **Age range** — paediatrics, adult medicine, geriatrics, neonatology.
- **Diagnostic versus therapeutic** — pathology and radiology principally diagnose; most others principally treat.
- **Organ-based versus technique-based** — cardiology is defined by an organ, anaesthesia and radiology by a technique.

A fifth axis matters more for daily life than any of these: **the procedural–cognitive spectrum**.

**Procedural specialties** (surgery and its subspecialties, interventional cardiology and radiology, gastroenterology, anaesthesia, obstetrics, ophthalmology, dermatology's surgical component) are paid for discrete billable acts, have shorter and more concrete patient encounters, produce visible immediate results, and carry manual-skill acquisition and maintenance requirements. They generally earn more, in every system that pays fee-for-service, because procedures are reimbursed better than thinking. They also carry more acute stress, more physical demand, more litigation exposure, and a hard end to a career when hands or eyes fail.

**Cognitive specialties** (general internal medicine, family medicine, paediatrics, psychiatry, geriatrics, infectious disease, endocrinology, rheumatology, palliative care) are paid for time and are systematically undervalued by fee-for-service schedules. They deal in ambiguity, longitudinal relationships and complexity; the work is harder to measure and therefore harder to defend at budget time. They report lower income and, in most surveys, lower satisfaction — driven less by the work than by the administrative burden and the volume expectations placed on it.

**Geographic distribution** follows the same logic: small towns get primary care, mid-sized cities secondary care, and metropolitan centres tertiary and quaternary care, with distribution determined by population income and demographics as much as by need.

## 2. The specialties

Training lengths given are **[US]** residency (including intern year) unless noted, with **[UK]** and **[ZA]** equivalents where they differ materially. "Competitiveness" is a relative characterisation, not a measured statistic.

### Primary care and generalist

**Family medicine / general practice** — 3 years **[US]**; 3 years **[UK]** (12–18 months hospital, 18–24 months primary care, ending in MRCGP); **[ZA]** FCFP(SA) via 4-year registrar training. The broadest scope in medicine: undifferentiated presentation, prevention, chronic disease, mental health, minor procedures, family and community context, and management of uncertainty over time. **[ZA]/[NA]** In district hospitals the family physician also does caesarean sections, anaesthesia, resuscitation and inpatient care — a far wider scope than the urban office practice the name suggests. Competitiveness: low in the US and UK, and in both systems this is a structural failure rather than a reflection of the work's difficulty. Lifestyle: predictable hours in office-based practice; heavy administrative load; reported satisfaction among the lowest, chiefly because of that load and short appointment times.

**General internal medicine** — 3 years **[US]**; **[UK]** Internal Medicine Training then higher specialty training; **[ZA]** FCP(SA), 4 years. Complex multi-morbid adults, diagnostic reasoning at its most demanding, and the acute medical take. Serves both as an endpoint (hospitalist, general physician) and as the gateway to the medical subspecialties. **The hospitalist** is a US-originated model — a physician who works exclusively in the inpatient setting — now the largest single career destination for US internal medicine graduates and increasingly present in the UK as the acute physician.

**Paediatrics** — 3 years **[US]**; **[UK]** 8 years run-through with MRCPCH; **[ZA]** FCPaed(SA). Neonate to adolescent, with development, safeguarding and family communication as core competencies. Lower income than adult equivalents almost everywhere. High reported meaning, high emotional load.

**Geriatric medicine** — subspecialty of internal medicine (1–2 years **[US]** fellowship; **[UK]** a full specialty and one of the largest). Frailty, multimorbidity, polypharmacy and deprescribing, falls, delirium and dementia, and goals-of-care conversations. Chronically undersupplied against a demographic wave, and one of the lowest-paid subspecialties despite requiring extra training — a structural absurdity every workforce report identifies and none fixes.

### Medical subspecialties (after internal medicine)

**Cardiology** (+3 years) — the largest and among the most competitive. Splits into **general/imaging**, **interventional** (+1–2 years: percutaneous coronary intervention, structural interventions such as TAVI), **electrophysiology** (+1–2 years: ablation, device implantation), **heart failure and transplant**, and **adult congenital**. High procedural income, high call burden, intense.

**Gastroenterology** (+3 years) — endoscopy-heavy, high procedural volume and income, competitive; hepatology as a further subspecialty.

**Pulmonology / respiratory medicine** (+2–3 years, usually combined with critical care in the US as **Pulm/CC**) — airways disease, interstitial lung disease, lung cancer, sleep, bronchoscopy and interventional pulmonology; ICU responsibility.

**Nephrology** (+2 years) — dialysis, transplant medicine, electrolyte and acid–base problems. Intellectually demanding, chronically under-recruited in the US and UK, high patient-mortality load.

**Endocrinology** (+2 years) — diabetes, thyroid, pituitary, adrenal, bone, gender-affirming care. Almost entirely cognitive; low procedural income; currently at the centre of the metabolic-medicine revolution described in `07`.

**Rheumatology** (+2 years) — inflammatory arthritis, connective tissue disease, vasculitis; a specialty transformed by biologics; cognitive, longitudinal, undersupplied.

**Infectious disease** (+2 years) — the lowest-paid US medical subspecialty relative to training length, which is why fellowship positions go unfilled, and simultaneously the specialty central to HIV, TB, AMR stewardship and outbreak response. **[ZA]/[NA]** In southern Africa this is not a niche but the core of adult medicine.

**Haematology and medical oncology** (+2–3 years, often combined in the US) — malignant and non-malignant haematology, solid tumour oncology; the fastest-moving therapeutic area in medicine; heavy emotional load; increasingly subspecialised by tumour type. **[UK]** haematology and medical oncology are separate specialties, and clinical oncology (which includes radiotherapy) is separate again.

**Allergy and immunology**, **clinical pharmacology and therapeutics**, **medical genetics**, **palliative medicine**, **sleep medicine**, **obesity medicine** and **addiction medicine** complete the list; several are also accessible from other base specialties.

### Surgical specialties

**General surgery** — 5 years **[US]**; **[UK]** 2 years core + 6 years ST3–8 with FRCS; **[ZA]** FCS(SA), 5 years. Abdominal, endocrine, breast, trauma and emergency general surgery, increasingly laparoscopic and robotic. Long hours, steep hierarchy, high attrition from the training programme. Almost all US graduates now subspecialise via fellowship: colorectal, hepatobiliary, surgical oncology, breast, endocrine, minimally invasive, transplant, trauma/acute care surgery, paediatric surgery.

**Orthopaedic surgery** — 5 years. Among the most competitive specialties in the US and UK. Subspecialties: arthroplasty, spine, sports, hand, foot and ankle, trauma, paediatric, oncology. High income, physically demanding, strongly procedural.

**Neurosurgery** — 7 years **[US]**, the longest residency; **[UK]** 8 years run-through. Cranial, spinal, vascular, functional, paediatric, skull base. Extreme hours and stakes, very high income, small workforce, and the highest reported burnout in several surveys.

**Cardiothoracic surgery** — 6 years integrated **[US]** or 2–3 years after general surgery. Coronary, valve, aortic, transplant, thoracic oncology. Contracted in volume as percutaneous intervention took over coronary work, and re-expanding through structural and transplant work.

**Vascular surgery** — 5–6 years integrated. Increasingly endovascular, so overlapping heavily with interventional radiology and cardiology.

**Otolaryngology (ENT) — head and neck surgery** — 5 years. Highly competitive. Broad: airway, oncology, otology and cochlear implantation, rhinology, paediatric ENT, facial plastics.

**Urology** — 5 years, own early match **[US]**. Competitive; oncology, stones, endourology, reconstruction, andrology; heavily robotic.

**Plastic and reconstructive surgery** — 6 years integrated. Very competitive. Reconstruction after cancer or trauma, burns, hand, craniofacial, microsurgery, and aesthetic practice.

**Ophthalmology** — 4 years including intern year, own early match **[US]**. Very competitive. Microsurgical, largely day-case, excellent lifestyle relative to other surgical fields, high satisfaction. Subspecialties: cataract and refractive, retina (medical and surgical), glaucoma, cornea, oculoplastics, paediatric, neuro-ophthalmology.

**Obstetrics and gynaecology** — 4 years. Uniquely both surgical and medical, with two patients and unpredictable hours. Subspecialties: maternal-fetal medicine, gynaecological oncology, reproductive endocrinology and infertility, urogynaecology, minimally invasive gynaecological surgery, family planning. High litigation exposure in the US, which drives insurance costs and practice patterns.

### Hospital-based and diagnostic

**Anaesthesiology** — 4 years **[US]**; **[UK]** 7 years with FRCA; **[ZA]** FCA(SA). Perioperative medicine, airway management, applied pharmacology and physiology in real time. Subspecialties: cardiac, paediatric, obstetric, neuro, regional, **critical care**, and **pain medicine**. Shift-based with defined handover, which many find preferable to on-call ownership.

**Emergency medicine** — 3–4 years **[US]**; **[UK]** 6 years with FRCEM; **[ZA]** FCEM(SA), a young specialty in the region. Undifferentiated acute presentation, resuscitation, procedures, and disposition. Shift work with no continuity, which is both the attraction and the problem: circadian disruption, boarding and crowding, violence against staff, and among the highest burnout rates measured. **[US]** After years of very high competitiveness, EM saw a sharp fall in application numbers in the 2022–2023 cycles following workforce projections of oversupply and the growth of private-equity-owned staffing groups — a case study in how quickly a specialty's desirability can invert.

**Radiology (diagnostic)** — 5 years including intern year **[US]**; **[UK]** 5 years with FRCR. Competitive; high income; reported satisfaction among the highest; heavy volume pressure and increasing subspecialisation (neuroradiology, musculoskeletal, body, chest, breast, paediatric, nuclear medicine, emergency radiology). The specialty most discussed in relation to AI — where the honest position is that image-interpretation algorithms have proliferated, a growing number are regulator-cleared, and radiologist demand has continued to rise rather than fall, because imaging volume has risen faster than automation has absorbed it, and because the job is far more than pattern detection.

**Pathology** — 3–4 years **[US]** (anatomic and clinical pathology combined); **[UK]** histopathology 5 years with FRCPath; **[ZA]** FCPath(SA). Anatomic (surgical pathology, cytopathology, autopsy) and clinical/laboratory (chemistry, haematology, microbiology, immunology, blood banking, molecular). Increasingly digital and computational. Little or no direct patient contact, which is either the appeal or the disqualifier.

**Nuclear medicine** — separate or via radiology; expanding rapidly with **theranostics** (paired diagnostic and therapeutic radioligands such as Lu-177 PSMA and DOTATATE).

**Radiation oncology** — 5 years **[US]**. Technically sophisticated, cognitively rich, well remunerated; US application numbers fell sharply in the late 2010s on workforce-supply concerns and have partly recovered.

### Neurological and psychiatric

**Neurology** — 4 years including intern year. Localisation-driven diagnosis; transformed from a diagnostic to a therapeutic specialty by disease-modifying therapy in multiple sclerosis, thrombectomy in stroke, migraine biologics and gene therapy in neuromuscular disease. Subspecialties: stroke and neurocritical care, epilepsy, movement disorders, neuromuscular, neuro-immunology, headache, behavioural neurology, neuro-oncology, interventional neurology.

**Psychiatry** — 4 years **[US]**; **[UK]** 6 years with MRCPsych; **[ZA]** FCPsych(SA). Subspecialties: child and adolescent, old age, forensic, addiction, liaison/consultation, intellectual disability. Demand vastly exceeds supply everywhere. **[US]** Competitiveness has risen steeply over the last decade — a genuine shift in the specialty's standing. Emotional load and risk management are the main stressors; telepsychiatry has changed access and working patterns more than in any other specialty.

**Rehabilitation medicine (PM&R / physiatry)** — 4 years. Function rather than cure: spinal cord and brain injury, amputation, stroke rehabilitation, spasticity, musculoskeletal and interventional pain.

### Others

**Dermatology** — 4 years including intern year. **The most competitive US specialty in most years**, and the specialty reporting the **highest job satisfaction**. Medical dermatology, dermatopathology, dermatologic surgery (Mohs), paediatric dermatology, and aesthetics. Predictable hours, high income, largely outpatient.

**Public health and preventive medicine** — 2–3 years plus an MPH **[US]**; **[UK]** 5 years public health training. Population health, epidemiology, health protection, screening programmes, policy. Small workforce, disproportionate influence.

**Medical genetics and genomics** — see emerging fields below.

**Clinical pharmacology**, **immunology**, **transfusion medicine**, **medical microbiology and virology** (**[UK]** and **[ZA]** distinct specialties; in the US these sit within pathology) complete the map.

## 3. Emerging and boundary fields

**Interventional radiology (IR).** Image-guided minimally invasive therapy: angioplasty and stenting, embolisation (trauma, postpartum haemorrhage, uterine fibroids, prostate artery), thrombectomy, tumour ablation, transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt, biliary and urinary drainage, venous access, and increasingly a clinical service with its own clinics and inpatients. **[US]** It became a **primary specialty with its own integrated residency (IR/DR)** rather than a fellowship — a formal recognition that the field had become a clinical rather than a consultative discipline. Fast-moving, procedurally intense, and in territorial tension with vascular surgery and cardiology over overlapping procedures.

**Clinical informatics.** **[US]** An ABMS-recognised subspecialty (first certification 2013), open from any primary specialty. The work: electronic health record configuration and optimisation, clinical decision support design, data governance, interoperability standards (**HL7 FHIR**, SNOMED CT, LOINC), analytics, and increasingly the governance and evaluation of clinical AI. **[UK]** The equivalent roles are CCIO/CMIO positions and the NHS Digital Academy rather than a certified specialty. It is the field with the most direct leverage over clinician workload, because the documentation burden described below is largely an artefact of systems designed for billing rather than care.

**Genomic medicine.** Clinical genetics has existed for decades; what is new is scale and integration. **[UK]** The NHS Genomic Medicine Service and the 100,000 Genomes Project made whole-genome sequencing a routine NHS diagnostic pathway for rare disease and cancer. Content: rare disease diagnosis, cancer predisposition and somatic tumour profiling, pharmacogenomics, prenatal and newborn screening (with active debate about newborn genomic screening), and variant interpretation using the ACMG framework. The workforce bottleneck is genetic counsellors and clinical scientists rather than doctors. **[ZA]** Medical genetics exists as a small specialty; **[NA]** genomic services are largely referred to South Africa.

**Sports and exercise medicine.** **[UK]** A recognised specialty since 2005; **[US]** a fellowship subspecialty from family medicine, EM, PM&R, paediatrics or IM, distinct from orthopaedic sports surgery. Content: musculoskeletal injury, concussion management, exercise prescription as therapy for chronic disease, athlete health, anti-doping, and physical activity in public health. Its cross-over with `35_health_and_fitness` is direct.

**Aerospace medicine.** **[US]** A subspecialty of preventive medicine with residency programmes, historically military and NASA-linked. Content: aviation physiology (hypoxia, decompression, acceleration, spatial disorientation), pilot and cabin crew fitness-to-fly certification under aviation-authority medical standards, fatigue and circadian management, passenger fitness to fly and in-flight medical events, aeromedical evacuation, and — a genuinely expanding area — commercial spaceflight participant medicine, radiation exposure, microgravity physiology and countermeasures. Related to the aviation domains elsewhere in this knowledge base.

**Occupational medicine.** **[UK]** A distinct specialty with the Faculty of Occupational Medicine; **[US]** a preventive medicine subspecialty; **[ZA]** an established discipline with particular weight because of the mining industry. Content: fitness for work assessment, workplace hazard control, occupational lung disease (silicosis and coal workers' pneumoconiosis, asbestos-related disease), noise-induced hearing loss, occupational cancer, ergonomics and musculoskeletal disorders, chemical and biological exposure, mental health at work, and medico-legal compensation systems. **[ZA]** The occupational health legacy of gold and platinum mining — silicosis, silico-tuberculosis, and the multi-billion-rand class-action settlement reached in 2019 — makes this one of the most consequential specialties in the country. **[NA]** Uranium and diamond mining generate the equivalent workload.

**Others worth naming:** **critical care medicine** (entered from anaesthesia, medicine, surgery, EM or paediatrics; a specialty in its own right in the UK and much of Europe); **palliative medicine** (a full specialty in the UK, a subspecialty in the US); **pain medicine**; **addiction medicine**; **transplant medicine**; **hyperbaric and diving medicine**; **wilderness and expedition medicine**; **disaster and humanitarian medicine**; **prison and secure-environment medicine**; **lifestyle medicine**; and **telemedicine/virtual care**, which is less a specialty than a delivery mode reshaping all of them.

## 4. Competitiveness, income and lifestyle — an honest treatment

**Competitiveness is not difficulty.** It is the ratio of applicants to posts, which is set by perceived income, lifestyle, prestige and the number of training places a government or accreditor funds — not by how hard the work is or how much it matters. Geriatrics is harder than dermatology and less competitive. Infectious disease is intellectually demanding and goes unfilled. Any statement that "the best students go into X" is a statement about incentives.

**The consistently most competitive [US] specialties** in recent cycles have included dermatology, plastic surgery (integrated), orthopaedic surgery, otolaryngology, neurosurgery, thoracic surgery (integrated), vascular surgery (integrated), radiation oncology (historically), ophthalmology and urology (both in their own earlier matches). The consistently least competitive by fill rate include family medicine, internal medicine (categorical), paediatrics, psychiatry (until its recent rise) and pathology.

**Income.** Medscape's 2022 physician compensation report put average earnings at approximately **US$339,000** overall — **US$260,000** for primary care and **US$368,000** for specialists. The procedural specialties sit at the top and the cognitive and paediatric specialties at the bottom, and the gap has widened rather than narrowed. In salaried systems (**[UK]** NHS consultant pay scales, **[ZA]** public-sector Occupation Specific Dispensation) the compression is far greater and the private/public differential does the same work instead.

**Satisfaction.** Dermatologists report the highest job satisfaction, followed by radiologists and oncologists; **primary care physicians report the lowest**. It is worth being precise about why: primary care satisfaction is depressed by appointment length, panel size, documentation and the sense of being the system's shock absorber — not by the intellectual content, which practitioners consistently rate highly.

**Lifestyle** is a poor single variable. What actually varies is: predictability of hours (dermatology, pathology, radiology high; obstetrics, neurosurgery, transplant low); shift versus ownership (emergency medicine and anaesthesia hand over; general surgery and obstetrics do not); acuity and night work; physical demand; litigation exposure (obstetrics and neurosurgery highest in the US); and portability of the career between countries and settings.

**[ZA]/[NA]** In southern Africa the competitiveness calculus is different again: the constraint is **funded registrar posts**, which are allocated to departments by provincial health budgets, so a specialty can be uncompetitive by interest and impossible to enter by arithmetic. Emigration pressure, the public/private differential, and the possibility of practising a broader scope than the equivalent European specialist all reshape the choice.

## 5. Burnout and the workforce crisis

### The scale

Occupational burnout among physicians **exceeds 50% in the United States** in many surveys and affects practising physicians and trainees alike. Medical students report burnout at roughly **43–45%**; one study found the rate rising through training — **21% in first year, 41% second, 43% third, 31% fourth**. Burnout is defined by three components: **emotional exhaustion**, **cynicism and depersonalisation**, and **a low sense of personal accomplishment**.

### The drivers, in order of evidential support

1. **Administrative and documentation burden.** US physicians spend approximately **two hours on clerical work for every hour of patient-facing time**, plus about **2.6 hours a week** on external quality measures. This is the most consistently identified driver.
2. **The electronic health record.** Documented as significantly associated with burnout, through usability problems, redundant documentation, alert fatigue and inbox volume. The EHR's design constituency was billing and compliance, not clinicians, and it shows.
3. **Hours.** The average US physician works **51 hours a week**, and about **one in four works more than 60**; weekend and holiday expectations are routine.
4. **Loss of autonomy and control**, consolidation into large employed groups, and productivity targets.
5. **Moral injury** — the term increasingly preferred to burnout by clinicians themselves, because it locates the harm correctly: the distress of knowing what a patient needs and being prevented by the system from providing it. Framing the problem as individual resilience deficit is, on this account, a category error and an insult.
6. **Workforce shortage itself**, which is self-reinforcing: shortage → workload → burnout → attrition → shortage.

### The consequences

- **Medical error.** Physicians with burnout had **more than twice the odds** of self-reported medical error.
- **Patient experience** — exhausted clinicians become detached and less empathetic; patient satisfaction falls.
- **Cost** — malpractice claims, unnecessary testing, and turnover, which is expensive per replaced physician.
- **Productivity** — inversely correlated with burnout.
- **Personal harm** — depression, substance use, relationship breakdown, and suicide. Physician suicide risk is elevated, and the elevation is greatest among women physicians; the profession's culture of concealment, and licensing questions that penalise disclosure of mental illness, are documented barriers to help-seeking that several jurisdictions have begun to reform.

### The workforce crisis

**Shortage.** Projections in the US, UK and across Africa forecast substantial physician shortfalls, worst in primary care, psychiatry, geriatrics and rural areas. **Maldistribution matters more than absolute numbers** in most systems: **[ZA]** 79% of doctors work in the private sector serving 16% of the population, and only 3% of newly qualified physicians work in rural areas although half the population lives there. **[NA]** Only 38% of health professionals serve the 80% dependent on public care.

**Pipeline mismatch.** The number of medical school places, the number of funded training posts, and the specialties those posts are in are set by three different bodies on three different timescales, none of which is well coupled to projected need. **[US]** Medicare's cap on funded residency positions, essentially frozen since 1997 and only marginally increased since, is the binding constraint on US physician supply regardless of how many medical schools open. **[ZA]** Internship and community service post funding is the equivalent chokepoint, and has repeatedly left qualified graduates unplaced.

**Ageing** of both the population served and the physician workforce, with a substantial fraction of practising doctors approaching retirement in most high-income countries.

**Exit.** Early retirement, reduced sessions, and departure to industry, consulting, administration or non-clinical roles — a flow that accelerated after 2020 and is under-counted because these doctors remain on the register.

**Industrial action.** **[UK]** Junior doctors (now "resident doctors") took repeated strike action from 2022 over pay erosion in real terms, working conditions and training; consultants and specialty doctors followed. **[ZA]** Public-sector doctors' organisations have repeatedly raised unfilled post freezes and equipment shortages. These are symptoms of the same structural problem, not separate events.

### What actually helps

The evidence is clearer about what does **not** work than what does. **Individual-level interventions** — mindfulness training, resilience courses, wellness apps, pizza in the doctors' mess — produce small effects and, deployed alone, are widely experienced as insulting because they locate the fault in the person. **Organisational interventions** have larger effects: reducing documentation burden (scribes, ambient documentation, sensible note requirements), workload and panel-size limits, schedule control and flexibility, adequate staffing, team-based care that moves non-physician work to non-physicians, leadership behaviour of immediate supervisors (measurably associated with the burnout of their reports), and giving clinicians genuine influence over how their work is organised.

Systemic changes with the most leverage: **payment reform** that stops rewarding volume and procedures over time and thinking; **EHR regulation** aimed at usability and documentation minimisation (**[US]** the CMS "Patients over Paperwork" initiative was an attempt); expansion of funded training posts in undersupplied specialties; licensing and credentialing reform so that seeking mental health care is not career-threatening; and honest workforce planning on a fifteen-year horizon, which is the minimum lead time for producing a specialist.

## Open questions

- **Competitiveness rankings** are characterisations from general knowledge of recent NRMP cycles, not measured fill-rate data; the 2026 NRMP specialty-level results were not retrievable in this pass. Verify at nrmp.org before citing any specialty as "most competitive".
- **[US]** The IR/DR integrated residency start date, the clinical informatics first certification year (2013), the Medicare residency cap (Balanced Budget Act 1997) and the emergency medicine application decline of 2022–2023 are stated from general reference knowledge and were **not** verified here.
- **[UK]** Sports and exercise medicine recognition (2005), the NHS Genomic Medicine Service, and the resident-doctor industrial action timeline were not verified in this pass.
- **[ZA]** The 2019 silicosis class-action settlement is stated from general knowledge — verify the settlement date, value and parties before citing.
- Medscape 2022 compensation figures are the most recent verified in this pass and are now several years old; refresh from the current report.
- Physician suicide-rate claims are widely repeated with variable methodology; treat as `needs-verification` and consult a current systematic review before quoting a figure.
