# The preclinical curriculum — the basic sciences of medicine

**Summary.** The first 12–24 months of medical school teach the substrate on which all clinical reasoning rests: the structure of the body, the function of its systems, the chemistry that powers them, the genetic and molecular machinery underneath, the immune and microbial world acting on them, the ways they fail, and the drugs that intervene. Modern curricula rarely teach these as separate disciplines any more; they teach them as **organ-system blocks** with each discipline contributing a thread. This file describes what is actually learned in each discipline, the standard textbook, and how the material is examined.

> ⚠️ This file is a curriculum description. It contains no diagnostic criteria intended for use, no drug doses and no treatment guidance. Nothing here is medical advice.

## 1. Anatomy

### Gross anatomy

The oldest and most physically demanding part of the curriculum. Students learn the body region by region — back, thorax, abdomen, pelvis and perineum, upper limb, lower limb, head and neck — as a system of **relationships**: what lies deep to what, what a structure is tethered by, which vessel and nerve enter which compartment and where they can be injured. The organising units are the fascial compartments, the neurovascular bundles, the dermatomes and myotomes, and the surface landmarks that let a clinician find something without imaging.

What is actually learned, concretely: the brachial plexus and the deficits produced by lesions at each level; the course of the recurrent laryngeal nerves and why the left one loops under the aortic arch; the peritoneal reflections and which organs are retroperitoneal; the inguinal canal and the difference between direct and indirect herniation; the coronary arterial distribution and which territory each supplies; the lumbar plexus and the safe zones for injection; the triangles of the neck; the anatomy of the pelvic floor.

Teaching mode has bifurcated. Full **cadaveric dissection** remains standard in most UK, South African, Namibian, German and Australian schools and in many US schools; a growing number of US and some Australian schools have moved to **prosection** (pre-dissected specimens), plastination, ultrasound-based living anatomy, and 3D digital tables (Anatomage, Complete Anatomy). The pedagogical argument is about time cost versus the irreplaceable experience of variation, texture and the first encounter with a dead human body — the latter widely described as a formative professional event rather than merely a technical one.

**Standard texts.** *Moore's Clinically Oriented Anatomy* is the dominant course text in the Commonwealth and much of the US; *Gray's Anatomy for Students* is its main competitor, a student-facing derivative of *Gray's Anatomy*, first published in **1858** by Henry Gray with illustrations by Henry Vandyke Carter, whose full reference edition — currently the **42nd**, edited by Susan Standring, with a 43rd announced for December 2025 — is a reference for surgeons and anatomists, not a student book. *Netter's Atlas of Human Anatomy* is the atlas of record for its painted plates; *Rohen's Color Atlas* uses photographs of real dissections and is better preparation for what a cadaver actually looks like. *Snell's Clinical Anatomy* and *Last's Anatomy* survive as regional favourites.

**Examination.** The **"spotter"** or steeplechase: numbered tags on cadaveric specimens or images, 30–60 seconds per station, identify the structure or answer a one-line clinical question about it. Plus written MCQ, increasingly clinically framed ("a stab wound here damages which nerve").

### Histology

The microscopic counterpart: epithelia and their classifications, connective tissue and its matrix, the four basic tissue types, then organ by organ — the layered wall of the gut, the nephron, the alveolus and its type I and II pneumocytes, the hepatic lobule and its zones, the lymph node with its cortex and paracortex and germinal centres. The point is that pathology later becomes a story about *deviation from this baseline*, so the baseline must be seen thousands of times.

Almost universally taught now on **virtual slides** rather than glass and microscopes. *Junqueira's Basic Histology* and *Ross & Pawlina* are the standards; *Wheater's Functional Histology* is the atlas most students actually use.

### Embryology

Fertilisation, cleavage, gastrulation and the three germ layers; folding; the pharyngeal arches and their nerves and derivatives; the septation of the heart; the rotation of the gut; the descent of the gonads and the kidneys; neural tube closure. Embryology earns its curriculum time because congenital anomalies are only intelligible as interrupted developmental sequences — a Meckel's diverticulum, a persistent truncus arteriosus, a cleft palate, a horseshoe kidney are each a specific developmental event that failed.

*Langman's Medical Embryology* (Sadler) is the compact standard; *Moore's The Developing Human* is fuller.

### Neuroanatomy

Usually taught as its own block. Content: the meninges and CSF circulation; the cerebral arterial supply and the circle of Willis; cortical localisation; the internal capsule; the basal ganglia loops; the cerebellum and its peduncles; the brainstem in cross-section at each level, with the cranial nerve nuclei; the long tracts — corticospinal, spinothalamic, dorsal column–medial lemniscus — and where each decussates; the visual pathway and the field defect produced by a lesion at each point; the autonomic outflow.

Neuroanatomy is the purest exercise in **localisation**: given a pattern of deficits, name the single lesion that explains all of them. *Blumenfeld's Neuroanatomy through Clinical Cases* is the text that teaches it that way and is widely preferred to purely descriptive atlases; *Snell's Clinical Neuroanatomy* and *Netter's Atlas of Neuroscience* are the alternatives.

## 2. Physiology

The quantitative core. Taught system by system, usually as the spine around which the other disciplines are draped.

- **Cell and membrane physiology** — the Nernst and Goldman equations, resting potential, the action potential, ion channels and their gating, transporters and pumps, synaptic transmission.
- **Cardiovascular** — cardiac cycle and pressure–volume loops; Frank–Starling; preload, afterload, contractility; the arterial pressure waveform; baroreceptor and chemoreceptor reflexes; regional circulations; the determinants of myocardial oxygen supply and demand; haemodynamics and Poiseuille's relationship.
- **Respiratory** — lung volumes and the spirometry loop; compliance and surfactant; ventilation–perfusion matching and the causes of hypoxaemia (V/Q mismatch, shunt, diffusion limitation, hypoventilation, low inspired FiO₂); the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve and what shifts it; carbon dioxide transport; control of breathing.
- **Renal** — glomerular filtration and its determinants; clearance; tubular handling segment by segment; the counter-current multiplier and the medullary gradient; the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system; ADH and osmoregulation; potassium handling; acid–base physiology including the Henderson–Hasselbalch relationship, the anion gap and compensation rules.
- **Gastrointestinal** — motility and its neural control; secretion (gastric acid and its regulation, pancreatic, biliary); digestion and absorption of each macronutrient; the enterohepatic circulation; hepatic function.
- **Endocrine and reproductive** — hypothalamic–pituitary axes and their feedback loops; thyroid; adrenal cortex and medulla; calcium and phosphate homeostasis; insulin, glucagon and fuel homeostasis; the menstrual cycle; pregnancy and lactation physiology.
- **Neurophysiology, muscle and exercise** — sensory transduction, motor units, excitation–contraction coupling, reflex arcs, thermoregulation, the integrated exercise response.

Physiology is where students first meet the idea that medicine is a **control-systems** discipline: almost every mechanism is a negative-feedback loop, and almost every disease is a loop that has been broken, saturated, or hijacked.

**Standard texts.** *Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology* — first published **1956**, written single-handedly by Arthur Guyton through eight editions to 1991, co-authored by John E. Hall from the ninth, and now in its **15th edition (2025)** — is the best-selling medical physiology text in the world, prized for explanation over concision. *Boron and Boulpaep, Medical Physiology* is the more molecular and rigorous alternative. *Ganong's Review of Medical Physiology* is the compact reference. *Costanzo* and the *BRS Physiology* series are the exam-facing condensations.

**Examination.** Heavy on interpretation: given this pressure–volume loop, this spirometry trace, this set of arterial blood gas numbers, this set of hormone levels — what is the lesion? Written MCQ in single-best-answer clinical-vignette format.

## 3. Biochemistry and metabolism

Often the least loved and most examined block. Content divides into:

- **Molecular building blocks** — amino acids and protein structure, enzyme kinetics (Michaelis–Menten, Lineweaver–Burk, inhibition types), allosteric regulation, coenzymes and the vitamins they derive from.
- **Bioenergetics and central metabolism** — glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, oxidative phosphorylation and the electron transport chain, the malate–aspartate and glycerol phosphate shuttles, uncouplers, gluconeogenesis, glycogen synthesis and breakdown, the pentose phosphate pathway.
- **Lipid metabolism** — fatty acid synthesis and β-oxidation, ketogenesis, cholesterol synthesis and its rate-limiting step, lipoprotein classes and their trafficking.
- **Nitrogen metabolism** — transamination, the urea cycle, amino acid catabolism, purine and pyrimidine synthesis and salvage, haem synthesis and degradation.
- **Integration and fuel switching** — the fed state, the post-absorptive state, prolonged starvation, exercise, and the hormonal signals that switch between them.

Medical biochemistry is taught through its **failure modes**: the inborn errors of metabolism are the pedagogical device that makes each pathway memorable. Phenylketonuria, the glycogen storage diseases, the urea cycle disorders, the lysosomal storage diseases, the mitochondrial disorders, the porphyrias, G6PD deficiency, familial hypercholesterolaemia — each names an enzyme and thereby a step.

**Standard texts.** *Lippincott Illustrated Reviews: Biochemistry* is the student workhorse. *Harper's Illustrated Biochemistry* is the traditional medical text. *Lehninger Principles of Biochemistry* is the rigorous science text — more than most medical curricula require, and the right book for MD-PhD students or anyone who wants the chemistry to be real rather than memorised. *Marks' Basic Medical Biochemistry* is the best at clinical integration.

**Examination.** Vignette-based: a patient presents with X, which enzyme is deficient; or a metabolite accumulates, which step is blocked. Pure recall of structures has largely disappeared from examinations.

## 4. Molecular and cell biology, and genetics

**Cell and molecular biology** covers DNA replication, transcription, RNA processing, translation, protein folding and trafficking, the secretory pathway, the cytoskeleton, cell-cycle control and its checkpoints, apoptosis and other regulated cell-death programmes, signal transduction families (receptor tyrosine kinases, GPCRs, nuclear receptors, JAK-STAT), and the molecular basis of oncogenesis — oncogenes, tumour suppressors, the two-hit hypothesis, DNA repair pathways and their failure.

**Genetics** covers Mendelian inheritance patterns and pedigree analysis; penetrance, expressivity, anticipation, imprinting, mosaicism; chromosomal disorders (trisomies, deletion syndromes, sex-chromosome aneuploidies); population genetics and Hardy–Weinberg; mitochondrial inheritance; multifactorial and polygenic disease and polygenic risk scores; the technologies — karyotype, FISH, microarray, Sanger, next-generation sequencing, whole-exome and whole-genome sequencing — and how variants are classified (the ACMG pathogenicity framework); pharmacogenomics; and the ethics of predictive testing, incidental findings and prenatal screening.

**Standard texts.** *Molecular Biology of the Cell* (Alberts et al.) is the reference; *Thompson & Thompson Genetics and Genomics in Medicine* (Nussbaum) is the medical genetics standard; *Emery and Rimoin* is the specialist reference.

## 5. Immunology

Innate immunity — barriers, complement and its three pathways, pattern recognition receptors, phagocytes, NK cells, the inflammatory cascade and its mediators. Adaptive immunity — antigen processing and presentation, MHC class I and II, T cell development and selection in the thymus, B cell development, V(D)J recombination and class switching, affinity maturation, the helper T cell subsets (Th1, Th2, Th17, Tfh, Treg) and their signature cytokines, immunological memory.

Then the clinical face: hypersensitivity types I–IV; autoimmunity and tolerance failure; transplantation immunology and rejection types; tumour immunology and immune evasion; primary immunodeficiencies mapped onto the pathways they interrupt; secondary immunodeficiency; vaccination; and the biology behind the modern immunotherapies — checkpoint inhibition, cytokine blockade, B-cell depletion, CAR-T.

**Standard texts.** *Janeway's Immunobiology* is the intellectual reference and the one that explains *why*. *Abbas, Cellular and Molecular Immunology* is the medical-school standard. *Basic Immunology* (Abbas) is its short form. *Roitt's Essential Immunology* remains a good compact alternative.

## 6. Microbiology and infectious disease

Organised four ways at once and students must hold all four:

- **By organism class** — bacteria (Gram-positive cocci, Gram-negative rods, anaerobes, spirochaetes, mycobacteria, atypicals), viruses (DNA and RNA families, replication strategies), fungi (yeasts, moulds, dimorphics), parasites (protozoa, helminths, ectoparasites), and prions.
- **By mechanism** — virulence factors, exotoxins and endotoxin, adhesion, immune evasion, biofilms, resistance mechanisms (β-lactamases including ESBL and carbapenemase, efflux, target modification, porin loss).
- **By syndrome** — what causes pneumonia, meningitis, endocarditis, urinary tract infection, diarrhoea, sexually transmitted infection, in which host and which setting.
- **By laboratory approach** — Gram stain, culture media, biochemical identification, serology, antigen detection, nucleic acid amplification, and susceptibility testing.

**[ZA]/[NA]** Southern African curricula weight tuberculosis, HIV and its opportunistic infections, malaria, and the enteric and vaccine-preventable diseases far more heavily than northern-hemisphere curricula do, and teach them with local epidemiology and local diagnostic algorithms.

**Standard texts.** *Jawetz, Melnick & Adelberg's Medical Microbiology* and *Levinson's Review of Medical Microbiology and Immunology* are the standards; *Murray's Medical Microbiology* is fuller; *Mandell, Douglas, and Bennett's* is the specialist reference for later.

## 7. Pharmacology

**General principles** — pharmacokinetics (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion; volume of distribution, clearance, half-life, first-order versus zero-order kinetics, bioavailability, first-pass effect, loading and maintenance dosing as concepts); pharmacodynamics (agonists, partial agonists, inverse agonists, antagonists competitive and non-competitive, dose–response curves, EC50, efficacy versus potency, therapeutic index); receptor families; drug metabolism and the cytochrome P450 system with its inducers and inhibitors; pharmacogenetics.

**Systems pharmacology** — autonomic drugs first, because they discipline the student in receptor logic; then cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, endocrine, gastrointestinal, CNS (anaesthetics, analgesics including opioids, anticonvulsants, antidepressants, antipsychotics), antimicrobials, antineoplastics, immunomodulators, and drugs affecting haemostasis.

**Toxicology and adverse effects** — the classification of adverse drug reactions, drug interactions, the major toxidromes as a conceptual framework, and the principle of the antidote.

> ⚠️ Pharmacology as taught in a curriculum is not a prescribing manual. Real prescribing is done against a formulary — the BNF, Lexicomp, Micromedex — with the patient's renal function, weight, allergies, pregnancy status and concurrent drugs in front of the prescriber. Nothing in a textbook substitutes for that, and nothing in this knowledge base does either.

**Standard texts.** *Katzung's Basic and Clinical Pharmacology* and *Rang & Dale's Pharmacology* are the two dominant course texts (Rang & Dale more mechanistic, Katzung more clinical). *Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics* is the reference of last resort — encyclopaedic, not a study book. *Lippincott Illustrated Reviews: Pharmacology* is the condensed exam text.

## 8. Pathology

The hinge discipline: the point at which basic science becomes disease.

**General pathology** — cell injury (reversible and irreversible), adaptations (hypertrophy, hyperplasia, atrophy, metaplasia), cell death (necrosis subtypes, apoptosis, and the newer regulated forms), acute and chronic inflammation and their mediators, tissue repair and fibrosis, haemodynamic disorders (oedema, thrombosis, embolism, infarction, shock), genetic and immune-mediated disease, neoplasia (nomenclature, carcinogenesis, invasion and metastasis, grading and staging, tumour markers, paraneoplastic syndromes), environmental and nutritional pathology.

**Systemic pathology** — organ by organ, the diseases and their morphology, correlated with the physiology already learned.

Pathology is also where students learn to read a **gross specimen** and a **stained section**, and where the vocabulary of clinical medicine is set: "granulomatous", "caseating", "dysplastic", "in situ".

**Standard text.** *Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease* — the "big Robbins", **10th edition, published 18 May 2020**, edited by Vinay Kumar, Abul K. Abbas and Jon C. Aster (Elsevier, ISBN 9780323531139). *Robbins Basic Pathology* ("baby Robbins") is the abridged student version and is what most students actually read; *Robbins Essential Pathology* is shorter again. Check the publisher for a newer edition before purchase.

## 9. Integration: the organ-system block

Systems-based curricula have become increasingly common since the mid-2000s and are now the majority model in the US and widespread in the UK, Australia and southern Africa. In a cardiovascular block the student learns, in one sequence: the anatomy of the heart and great vessels and the coronary distribution; the histology of the vessel wall; the embryology of cardiac septation; the physiology of the cardiac cycle and the pressure–volume loop; the biochemistry of myocardial energetics; the pathology of atherosclerosis, infarction and cardiomyopathy; the microbiology of endocarditis and rheumatic fever; the pharmacology of antihypertensives, antianginals, antiarrhythmics, anticoagulants and lipid-lowering agents — and, in parallel, the clinical skills thread teaches cardiovascular history and examination, ECG interpretation, and heart sounds.

The claimed advantages are retention through interleaving, earlier clinical relevance, and reduced duplication. The costs are real and worth naming: **loss of disciplinary coherence** (a student may never see the whole of biochemistry as a subject), **weaker anatomy** in schools that cut dissection to fit, dependence on faculty coordination that not every institution manages, and difficulty for students who learn better by discipline. Some influential schools have moved back towards discipline-based teaching for anatomy and pathology specifically.

Other structural variants in wide use:
- **Problem-based learning (PBL)** — small-group work on a paper case, with the group defining its own learning objectives; pioneered at McMaster, adopted widely, and the subject of a long and inconclusive evidence base on whether it improves outcomes.
- **Case-based collaborative learning / team-based learning (TBL)** — more structured than PBL, with readiness-assurance tests and facilitated application exercises; increasingly preferred for scalability.
- **The flipped classroom** — recorded lectures consumed at speed (students routinely watch at 1.5–2×), classroom time used for application. This has effectively decoupled attendance from performance in many US schools and shifted study onto third-party resources (see `04`).
- **Longitudinal clinical placement from year 1** — a half-day a week in a GP practice or clinic, universal in the UK and increasingly common elsewhere.

## 10. How the preclinical years are examined

- **In-block assessment** — single-best-answer MCQs in clinical-vignette format, extended matching questions, short answers, practical spotters, laboratory and imaging interpretation.
- **Progress testing** — used by several UK and Dutch schools: the same full-curriculum test administered to every year group repeatedly, so that progress is measured as a trajectory rather than a threshold.
- **[US] USMLE Step 1** — the national summative examination on the basic sciences, **pass/fail only since 26 January 2022**. Its content spans all the disciplines above plus behavioural science, biostatistics, ethics and communication. The move to pass/fail was intended to defuse the "Step 1 climate" that had turned the pre-clerkship years into a year-long test-preparation exercise. The effect has been partial: schools report less score-chasing but comparable total study effort, with pressure displaced to Step 2 CK.
- **[UK]/[ZA]/[NA]** — internal university examinations govern progression; there is no national basic-science examination. The functional equivalent of Step 1 in the Commonwealth systems arrives later, as the Part I of the Royal College or CMSA fellowship examination taken during specialty training.
- **Professionalism and academic integrity** are assessed continuously and separately, and failure here is treated more seriously than knowledge failure in every system described.
