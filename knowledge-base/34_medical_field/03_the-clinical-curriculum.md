# The clinical curriculum — rotations, reasoning and assessment

**Summary.** The clinical years convert a person who knows facts into a person who can take a history, examine a patient, generate and prune a differential, and act under uncertainty and time pressure. This file covers the clinical-skills foundation, the core rotations and what each actually teaches, the cognitive science of clinical reasoning and where it fails, the assessment instruments (OSCE, workplace-based assessment, entrustable professional activities), the hidden curriculum, and the transition to independent practice.

> ⚠️ Diagnostic frameworks are described here at textbook level, to explain how clinicians are trained to structure a problem. They are not diagnostic criteria for use, and nothing here is medical advice. Any real clinical question requires a qualified clinician who has examined the patient.

## 1. Clinical skills: the physical examination

Physical examination is taught as a set of **regional and system routines** that must become automatic, so that attention is free for interpretation rather than sequence.

- **General inspection and vital signs** — the "end of the bed" assessment, level of consciousness, work of breathing, colour, hydration; pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature, oxygen saturation, and increasingly capillary glucose. Students are drilled that inspection is the most information-dense and most skipped step.
- **Cardiovascular** — peripheral signs, jugular venous pressure and waveform, apex beat character and displacement, heaves and thrills, the four auscultation areas, the timing and character of murmurs and the manoeuvres that change them, peripheral pulses and oedema.
- **Respiratory** — chest shape and expansion, tracheal position, percussion, breath sounds, added sounds (crackles, wheeze, pleural rub), vocal resonance.
- **Abdominal** — inspection, palpation superficial then deep, organomegaly, masses, percussion for ascites, auscultation for bowel sounds and bruits, hernial orifices, and the digital rectal examination where indicated.
- **Neurological** — the cranial nerves, then tone, power (MRC grading 0–5), reflexes, coordination, sensation in each modality, and gait. Neurological examination is explicitly taught as a **localisation instrument**, not a checklist.
- **Musculoskeletal** — the "look, feel, move, special tests" structure, and the GALS screen.
- **Others** — thyroid, breast, lymphoreticular, dermatological description (the vocabulary of macule, papule, plaque, vesicle, bulla, pustule, nodule), ophthalmoscopy and otoscopy, obstetric abdominal examination, and the mental state examination.

Two honest caveats are taught alongside the routines. First, **the diagnostic performance of many classical signs is poor** — the evidence base compiled in works like *The Rational Clinical Examination* (JAMA) and Steven McGee's *Evidence-Based Physical Diagnosis* shows likelihood ratios for many textbook signs close to 1. Second, **point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS)** has displaced parts of the traditional examination in emergency, critical care and increasingly general medicine, and its teaching is now embedded in many undergraduate curricula.

**Standard texts.** *Macleod's Clinical Examination* (Commonwealth) and *Bates' Guide to Physical Examination and History Taking* (US) are the two canonical books; *Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination* is the Australasian standard and a favourite in South Africa; *Hutchison's Clinical Methods* is the older British reference.

## 2. History taking

The history is where most diagnoses are made — the frequently repeated teaching claim is that the history yields the diagnosis in something like 70–80% of cases, a figure derived from a small number of old studies and worth treating as a pedagogical heuristic rather than a measured constant.

The taught structure: presenting complaint; history of presenting complaint (with a symptom-analysis framework such as SOCRATES for pain — site, onset, character, radiation, associations, time course, exacerbating/relieving factors, severity); systems review; past medical and surgical history; drug history and allergies; family history; social history (occupation, housing, alcohol, tobacco, other substances, travel, sexual history where relevant, functional status); and **ideas, concerns and expectations** — the ICE framework, which is the point at which the modern curriculum insists the patient's own model of their illness be elicited.

Layered on top are the **communication skills** frameworks: **Calgary–Cambridge** (the dominant consultation model in UK and Commonwealth teaching), **SPIKES** for breaking bad news, **SBAR** for handover, motivational interviewing for behaviour change, and structured approaches to interpreter-mediated, low-literacy and cross-cultural consultations. **[ZA]/[NA]** Multilingual consultation — often through a nurse or family member interpreting between English/Afrikaans and isiZulu, isiXhosa, Oshiwambo, Khoekhoegowab or Otjiherero — is a taught competency in southern African schools and a live source of clinical risk.

## 3. The core rotations

### Internal medicine

The longest and usually the formative rotation (8–12 weeks). Students clerk acute medical admissions, present on ward rounds, follow patients through investigation and treatment, and learn the multi-system, multi-morbid patient who does not fit one textbook chapter. Core content: cardiology, respiratory, gastroenterology and hepatology, nephrology, endocrinology, neurology, rheumatology, haematology, oncology, infectious disease and geriatrics. Skills: venepuncture and cannulation, arterial blood gas sampling, ECG interpretation, chest radiograph interpretation, fluid and electrolyte reasoning, and the written record — the admission clerking, the problem list, the daily progress note, the discharge summary.

### Surgery

6–12 weeks, often split between general surgery and subspecialties (orthopaedics, urology, ENT, ophthalmology, vascular, cardiothoracic, neurosurgery, plastics). Students learn the surgical workflow — the pre-operative assessment and risk stratification, consent, the operating theatre and its sterile discipline, scrubbing, assisting, suturing and knot-tying, and the post-operative course with its complications ordered by day (bleeding, atelectasis, infection, thromboembolism, anastomotic leak, ileus). Conceptually the rotation teaches the **acute abdomen** as a reasoning exercise and the distinction between a problem that surgery can fix and one it cannot.

### Paediatrics

4–8 weeks. Distinct because the patient is a moving developmental target and the history is usually taken from someone else. Content: normal growth and development and the growth chart; neonatology and the newborn examination; immunisation schedules; the common acute presentations (fever, respiratory distress, dehydration, seizure, the limping child); congenital and genetic disease; child protection and safeguarding, which is a formal and examinable duty. **[ZA]/[NA]** Malnutrition, gastroenteritis, TB, HIV-exposed infants and vaccine-preventable disease are far more prominent than in northern curricula, and the IMCI (Integrated Management of Childhood Illness) framework is taught explicitly.

### Obstetrics and gynaecology

4–8 weeks. Antenatal care and screening; normal labour and its stages; the partogram; operative delivery; the obstetric emergencies (haemorrhage, pre-eclampsia and eclampsia, shoulder dystocia, cord prolapse, sepsis); the puerperium. Gynaecology: menstrual disorders, contraception, subfertility, early pregnancy problems, gynaecological oncology, urogynaecology. It is usually a student's first exposure to a specialty where two patients' interests must be weighed simultaneously, and to intimate examination with a chaperone, consent and dignity as explicit competencies.

### Psychiatry

4–8 weeks. The **mental state examination** as a formal instrument; risk assessment; the major diagnostic groupings — mood disorders, psychotic disorders, anxiety and obsessive-compulsive and trauma-related disorders, substance use, personality disorder, eating disorders, neurodevelopmental conditions, delirium and dementia; the legal frameworks for detention and treatment (the Mental Health Act in England and Wales, the **[ZA]** Mental Health Care Act 17 of 2002, state-level involuntary commitment law in the US); and the therapeutic modalities. Psychiatry is also where the curriculum's stigma problem is most visible, and where students most often report a change of attitude.

### Family medicine / general practice

4–8 weeks, usually in the community. This is where undifferentiated presentation is met for the first time: patients arrive with symptoms, not diagnoses, at very low prior probability of serious disease, and the skill being taught is **safe management of uncertainty over time** — safety-netting, the use of time as a diagnostic test, continuity, multimorbidity, polypharmacy review, and prevention. **[ZA]/[NA]** District-hospital and clinic placements serve this role and are also where students meet the reality of resource constraint.

### Emergency medicine

2–6 weeks. Undifferentiated acute presentation, resuscitation as a team activity with defined roles, triage, the primary survey (ABCDE) as an organising discipline, procedural skills, and disposition decisions. Emergency medicine teaches **rule-out reasoning** — the deliberate prioritisation of the dangerous diagnosis over the likely one — which is a genuinely different cognitive stance from the rest of medicine.

### Anaesthesia and critical care

Often a 2–4 week attachment. Airway management, monitoring, pharmacology in real time, and the ICU as a place where physiology is manipulated directly. Widely reported by students as the single best place to learn applied physiology.

### Electives and subspecialty selectives

Radiology, pathology, dermatology, ophthalmology, ENT, genetics, palliative care, public health, sports medicine, and the international elective. **[US]** The "away rotation" or audition elective at a target residency programme is a significant strategic exercise in competitive specialties. Palliative care deserves special mention: it is the rotation that most reliably teaches students that the goal is not always cure, and its formal teaching of symptom control, advance care planning and communication near the end of life is now a required competency in most curricula.

## 4. Clinical reasoning

### Illness scripts

The core cognitive construct of modern clinical-reasoning teaching. An **illness script** is a structured memory representation of a disease with three slots:

1. **Predisposing conditions / epidemiology** — who gets it, in what context.
2. **Pathophysiological insult** — the mechanism.
3. **Clinical consequences** — the temporal pattern of symptoms, signs and results.

Expertise, on this account, is not more facts but **better-organised, more numerous and more richly indexed scripts**, retrieved by cues from the case. Novices reason forward from pathophysiology because they lack scripts; experts recognise the script and reason backwards to check it. This is why students are taught to write a **problem representation** — a one-sentence abstraction of the case ("a middle-aged patient with subacute progressive exertional dyspnoea, orthopnoea and bilateral crackles") — because the abstraction is what retrieves the script.

### Pattern recognition versus analytical reasoning, and dual-process theory

**Dual-process theory** frames clinical cognition as two interacting systems:

- **System 1** — fast, automatic, pattern-driven, low effort, emotionally coloured, "based on formed habits and very difficult to change or manipulate". This is what a consultant uses for most decisions most days, and it is usually right.
- **System 2** — slow, deliberate, analytical, effortful, "subject to conscious judgments and attitudes". This is what a student uses for everything, and what an expert falls back on when a case does not fit.

The taught synthesis is that neither is superior: expertise consists in **calibrated switching** — knowing when the pattern is trustworthy and when to force the slow system on. Practical devices include the **diagnostic time-out**, the deliberate generation of a "must-not-miss" list, and asking "what else could this be?" and "what does not fit?" before committing.

### Frameworks students are given

- **VITAMIN C / VINDICATE** and similar mnemonic scaffolds for generating a broad differential by aetiological category (vascular, infectious, traumatic, autoimmune, metabolic, iatrogenic, neoplastic, congenital) — crude, but they prevent premature narrowing.
- **Anatomical framing** — for a symptom localised to a region, work outward through the structures present.
- **Semantic qualifiers** — converting the patient's words into paired opposing descriptors (acute/chronic, unilateral/bilateral, constant/intermittent, progressive/relapsing), which is one of the more strongly evidenced correlates of diagnostic skill.
- **Bayesian framing** — pre-test probability, likelihood ratios, post-test probability; test and treatment thresholds. This is the point at which `05_evidence-based-medicine.md` becomes clinically operational.

### Sources of diagnostic error

Diagnostic error is now treated as a first-class patient-safety problem rather than an individual failing. The literature divides causes into:

**Cognitive** — the recognised failure modes include:
- **Premature closure** — accepting a diagnosis before it is verified; the single most cited contributor.
- **Anchoring** — over-weighting the first piece of information, then insufficiently adjusting.
- **Availability** — over-estimating probability because a case is recent or memorable.
- **Confirmation bias** — seeking data that support the working diagnosis and discounting data that do not.
- **Search satisficing** — stopping after the first abnormality is found (the classic second fracture on the radiograph).
- **Framing effects** — the handover's wording determining the receiver's differential.
- **Diagnosis momentum** — a provisional label hardening into fact as it is passed along.
- **Base-rate neglect** and **representativeness** — ignoring prior probability in favour of resemblance.
- **Affective bias** — the difficult, intoxicated, or frequently attending patient receiving less diagnostic effort.

**System** — handover discontinuity, time pressure, interruption, absent or delayed results, poor test follow-up loops, understaffing, fatigue, EHR design that buries the relevant datum in copied-forward text, and lack of feedback (most clinicians never learn whether their diagnosis was right).

**Knowledge** — genuine ignorance of the entity, which is less common than assumed in the error literature but not negligible.

The debiasing evidence is sobering: teaching clinicians to name biases does not reliably reduce error. What has better support is **systems change** — structured handover, result-tracking loops, checklists in specific settings, second reads in radiology, diagnostic decision support — plus **feedback loops** that tell clinicians what actually happened to their patients.

## 5. Assessment in the clinical years

### The OSCE

The **Objective Structured Clinical Examination**, created by Ronald Harden and first described in the *BMJ* in **1975**, is now the dominant clinical assessment worldwide. Structure: a circuit of short stations, **usually 5–10 minutes, sometimes up to 15**; every candidate rotates through the same stations with the same instructions, standardised marking schemes and, at each station, a different examiner. Stations use real or simulated ("standardised") patients and set a defined task — take a focused history, perform a named examination, explain a diagnosis or procedure, interpret a result, obtain consent, perform a clinical skill on a manikin, prescribe safely.

Scoring combines **itemised checklists** (marks for discrete correct actions) with a **global rating** by the examiner, and modern practice weights the global rating heavily because checklists reward thoroughness over judgement — a known pathology in which weaker candidates score well by performing every item mechanically. Standard setting is usually by **borderline regression** or the borderline-group method, in which the global ratings are used to derive the pass mark empirically from the cohort, rather than by a fixed percentage.

Criticisms, taken seriously in the literature: OSCE stations "can never be truly standardised and objective in the same way as a written exam"; simulated patients vary in the cues they give and the help they offer; examiners apply criteria inconsistently (examiner stringency is a measurable and non-trivial variance component); station length forces artificial fragmentation of a consultation; and the format can reward performance of communication rather than communication itself.

### Workplace-based assessment (WPBA)

Because the OSCE samples a simulation, it is complemented by assessment of real work:

- **Mini-CEX** (mini-Clinical Evaluation Exercise) — an observed real patient encounter with structured feedback.
- **DOPS** (Direct Observation of Procedural Skills).
- **CbD** (Case-based Discussion) — probing reasoning behind a real case the trainee managed.
- **MSF / 360° feedback** — multi-source feedback from colleagues across professions.
- **Patient feedback questionnaires**.
- **Logbooks and portfolios**, reviewed at the **[UK]** ARCP or the **[US]** Clinical Competency Committee.

The stated intent is formative — a low-stakes observation that generates feedback. The observed reality in many systems is **"tick-box" WPBA**, completed retrospectively to satisfy a portfolio quota, which is a well-documented failure of implementation rather than of the instrument.

### Competency frameworks and EPAs

Two frameworks dominate the vocabulary:

- **ACGME core competencies** (patient care, medical knowledge, practice-based learning and improvement, interpersonal and communication skills, professionalism, systems-based practice), operationalised through **Milestones** — narrative developmental levels for each specialty, reported twice yearly.
- **CanMEDS** (Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada) — the Medical Expert at the centre, surrounded by Communicator, Collaborator, Leader, Health Advocate, Scholar and Professional. CanMEDS has been adopted or adapted well beyond Canada, including in Dutch, Danish and several African curricula.

**Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs)** were introduced to make competency assessment concrete: rather than rating an abstract competency, a supervisor judges the level of supervision a trainee needs for a *unit of professional work* ("obtain informed consent for a common procedure", "give a handover", "recognise a patient requiring urgent care"). The AAMC defined **13 Core EPAs for Entering Residency**. The appeal is that entrustment is the judgement supervisors actually make; the difficulty is that entrustment decisions are strongly influenced by familiarity and risk tolerance, and are not straightforwardly comparable across supervisors.

### **[UK]** The Medical Licensing Assessment

The GMC has introduced a **Medical Licensing Assessment (MLA)** — an applied-knowledge test plus a clinical/professional skills assessment delivered by the medical schools — to create a common standard for UK graduates and PLAB candidates. Its phase-in dates and the exact interaction with university finals should be confirmed directly with the GMC before being relied on.

## 6. The hidden curriculum

The term (Hafferty) names everything students learn that is not in the syllabus: the informal norms, role-modelling, and institutional signals that shape professional identity. It is transmitted by what is rewarded and tolerated, not by what is taught.

Documented content of the hidden curriculum includes: the informal hierarchy of specialties and the disparagement of general practice, psychiatry and geriatrics ("badmouthing"); the norm of concealing uncertainty and of not admitting ignorance on a ward round; the tolerance of working while sick; the reflex of describing patients by disease or bed number; the erosion of empathy measured repeatedly across the clinical years; the normalisation of dismissive language about "difficult" patients; and the learned reluctance to speak up across a hierarchy gradient, which is a direct patient-safety hazard and the reason simulation now explicitly trains graded assertiveness.

Counter-measures that have some evidence behind them: explicit reflective practice and Balint-style groups; longitudinal integrated clerkships that give students continuity with patients and one supervisor rather than a series of five-week strangers; named faculty accountability for the learning environment; and reporting mechanisms for mistreatment, which US schools are required to survey through the AAMC Graduation Questionnaire.

Mistreatment is not a marginal issue. Surveys consistently report substantial fractions of students experiencing public humiliation, and smaller but persistent fractions reporting discrimination or harassment, most often on surgical rotations. This connects directly to the burnout literature: student burnout has been measured at roughly **43–45%**, with one study finding it rising through the course (21% in first year, 41% second, 43% third, 31% fourth).

## 7. The transition to independent practice

The step from final-year student to first-year doctor is the sharpest discontinuity in the career, and is now designed for explicitly.

**What changes.** Legal responsibility, prescribing authority, being the person bleeped, being the person who must decide at 03:00 whether to escalate, and the volume of administrative work — discharge summaries, referrals, results follow-up — which dominates the junior year in every system and is a major driver of dissatisfaction.

**Preparation devices.** The **[US]** sub-internship, the **[UK]** student assistantship and shadowing period before F1 starts, boot camps and simulation-based "prepare for practice" courses, prescribing safety assessments (the UK PSA is a compulsory summative examination), and structured induction. Evidence that these reduce error is modest but the face validity is high enough that they are universal.

**Supervision architecture.** Every system defines who the new doctor escalates to and how: the **[UK]** registrar-then-consultant ladder, the **[US]** senior resident then attending, **[ZA]** the medical officer and consultant on call. The recurring safety problem is not the absence of a ladder but reluctance to use it, which is a hidden-curriculum artefact.

**Known risk periods.** The changeover date — the first Wednesday in August in the UK, 1 July in the US — has been studied for an associated mortality signal (the so-called "killing season" or "July effect"). Findings are mixed and effect sizes, where present, are small; the honest summary is that the evidence is contested, but the phenomenon is taken seriously enough that both systems have restructured induction around it.

**Then it continues.** Residency and specialty training are themselves a graded transfer of responsibility, capped in the US at **80 duty hours per week averaged over four weeks** (with permitted exceptions to 88), and shaped by the European Working Time Directive in the EU and UK. The endpoint — the consultant or attending who is the final decision-maker — arrives 7 to 15 years after graduation depending on system and specialty (see `01` and `10`).
