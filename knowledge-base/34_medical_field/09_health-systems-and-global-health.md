# Health systems and global health — models, economics and the southern African picture

**Summary.** A doctor's clinical decisions are bounded by a financing system they usually did not choose and rarely understand. This file describes the four canonical health-system models with country examples, the economics of rationing (QALYs, thresholds, health technology assessment), the WHO's role, the global burden of disease with dated figures, and then the southern African picture in detail: the HIV and TB burden and the programmes built around it, South Africa's public/private split and the NHI Act, and Namibia's system and its constraints. It closes with primary care and universal health coverage, antimicrobial resistance, pandemic preparedness and health workforce migration.

> ⚠️ Nothing here is medical advice. Figures decay quickly; every number is dated.

## 1. The four models

The taxonomy popularised by T. R. Reid is a simplification, and every real country is a hybrid, but it remains the most useful first cut.

### Beveridge — tax-financed, publicly provided

Named for William Beveridge, whose 1942 report shaped the post-war British welfare state. Financing from **general taxation**; most providers publicly employed or contracted; the state is both payer and provider; access is a right of residence rather than of contribution.

Examples: the **UK NHS** (founded **1948**), Spain, Italy, the Nordic countries, Cuba, Hong Kong, and the **[US]** Veterans Health Administration as a domestic exception.

Characteristics: strong cost control (a global budget is a hard constraint), low administrative overhead, equitable access by design; and the corresponding failure modes — waiting lists as the rationing mechanism, capital underinvestment when budgets are squeezed, and political vulnerability because the health budget competes directly with everything else in the fiscal envelope.

### Bismarck — social health insurance

Named for Otto von Bismarck, whose 1883 sickness insurance law was the first modern system. Financing from **compulsory payroll contributions** shared between employers and employees, pooled in non-profit **sickness funds**; providers are largely private; the funds are heavily regulated and generally must accept all applicants at a community rate.

Examples: **Germany**, France, Japan (universal since **1961**), Belgium, the Netherlands, Switzerland (mandatory purchase from regulated private insurers), Austria, and to a degree South Korea and Taiwan's predecessor arrangements.

Characteristics: high patient choice, short waits, generous provision; and the corresponding weaknesses — higher administrative cost, weaker overall cost control, and coverage historically tied to employment, which requires explicit mechanisms for the unemployed and informal sector.

### National health insurance — single payer, private provision

A hybrid: financing is public and single-payer (as in Beveridge), delivery is private (as in Bismarck). The single payer's monopsony power is the cost-control instrument.

Examples: **Canada** (provincial single-payer plans), **Taiwan** (since 1995, often cited as the best-designed modern system), South Korea, Australia's **Medicare** (established as Medibank in **1975**), and **[US] Medicare** for those over 65 as a domestic instance.

### Out-of-pocket

No meaningful pooling. Care is bought when affordable and foregone when not. This is the default for a large share of the world's population and the mechanism by which illness causes poverty: over **1 billion** people face catastrophic out-of-pocket health expenditure and **2 billion** experience financial difficulty from health costs (WHO/World Bank, 2023).

Most low-income countries operate a mixture: a nominally free but under-resourced public system, a private sector for those who can pay, out-of-pocket payment for medicines and diagnostics even within the public system, and donor-funded vertical programmes for specific diseases.

### The United States as a category error

The US operates all four simultaneously: Beveridge for veterans and active military; Bismarck-like for employer-sponsored insurance covering roughly half the population; national health insurance for Medicare beneficiaries; Medicaid as a means-tested public programme; and out-of-pocket for the uninsured. It spends roughly 17–18% of GDP on health — far more than any other country — with worse life expectancy than peer nations, and it is the only high-income country without universal coverage.

## 2. Health economics for clinicians

### Why rationing is unavoidable

Health needs are effectively unbounded; budgets are not. Every system rations. The only variable is whether it does so **explicitly** (NICE publishing a threshold and a decision), **implicitly** (waiting lists, geographic availability, clinician discretion), or **by price** (ability to pay). Explicit rationing is politically painful and analytically honest; the alternatives are politically comfortable and usually less equitable.

### The QALY

A **quality-adjusted life year** combines length and quality of life into a single unit: one year in full health = 1 QALY; one year at a utility weight of 0.5 = 0.5 QALYs. Utility weights are derived from preference-elicitation instruments — **EQ-5D** (the most used, with country-specific value sets), SF-6D, HUI — valued by the general public using time trade-off or standard gamble methods.

**The DALY** (disability-adjusted life year) is the burden-of-disease counterpart: **years of life lost** plus **years lived with disability**. "One DALY can be thought of as one year of healthy life lost." QALYs are gained by interventions; DALYs are averted.

**Criticisms of the QALY, taken seriously:** it discriminates against people with disabilities and the elderly, because they have fewer or lower-weighted years to gain; public preference weights may not reflect the preferences of people who actually have the condition (who systematically rate their state higher); it aggregates across people, so a large benefit to one and a small benefit to many are treated as equivalent; and it cannot capture process utility, dignity, carer burden, or the special weight societies place on rescue and on children. The **EuroQol** community, NICE and others have added modifiers — severity weighting, end-of-life uplifts — rather than abandoning the metric.

### Cost-effectiveness analysis

The **incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER)**:

**ICER = (Cost_new − Cost_comparator) ÷ (Effect_new − Effect_comparator)**

expressed as cost per QALY gained. A worked illustration: a new therapy costs £8,000 more per patient than standard care and yields 0.4 additional QALYs. ICER = £8,000 ÷ 0.4 = **£20,000 per QALY** — exactly at the conventional English threshold, and therefore a genuinely marginal decision.

**Thresholds.** **[UK]** NICE treats below **£20,000/QALY** as cost-effective and requires "increasingly strong reasons" above **£30,000**, with a higher threshold permitted for end-of-life treatments since 2008. A 2015 University of York analysis argued the threshold should be **more than halved to around £13,000**, on the argument that spending above the true marginal productivity of the NHS displaces more health than it creates. The WHO's older heuristic of 1–3× GDP per capita per DALY averted has been formally repudiated by its own authors as arbitrary and too permissive, though it is still widely quoted.

Related concepts: **cost-utility** (the QALY form), **cost-effectiveness** (natural units — cost per case detected, per life saved), **cost-benefit** (everything monetised), **cost-minimisation** (when outcomes are equivalent); the **budget impact** analysis, which asks whether the system can afford the total, not just whether each unit is good value; **opportunity cost**, which is what the threshold is really about; and **discounting** of future costs and benefits (commonly 3–3.5% annually), which systematically disadvantages prevention.

### Health technology assessment (HTA)

The institutional apparatus for making these judgements: **NICE** (England), the **Scottish Medicines Consortium**, **IQWiG** and the G-BA (Germany), **HAS** (France), **CADTH/Canada's Drug Agency**, **PBAC** (Australia), **ICER** (US, non-governmental and non-binding), and the **[EU] Health Technology Assessment Regulation (EU) 2021/2282**, which since January 2025 requires joint clinical assessments at EU level for new oncology medicines and advanced therapy medicinal products, with phased extension. **[ZA]** South Africa's National Essential Medicines List Committee performs an HTA-like function for the public sector, and formal HTA is a stated component of NHI design. **[NA]** Namibia relies substantially on WHO Essential Medicines List processes and South African precedent.

## 3. The WHO and global health governance

The **World Health Organization**, founded 1948, is a UN specialised agency with 194 member states, governed by the World Health Assembly, headquartered in Geneva with six regional offices — for southern Africa, **AFRO** in Brazzaville — and country offices.

Its functions: normative guidance (guidelines, the **Essential Medicines List**, the **International Classification of Diseases**, prequalification of medicines and vaccines); the **International Health Regulations (2005)**, under which states must detect, assess, report and respond to public health events and the Director-General may declare a **Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC)**; surveillance and outbreak response; technical assistance; and convening (the Framework Convention on Tobacco Control is its major treaty success).

Its structural constraints are as important as its powers: it has **no enforcement authority** over sovereign states; roughly 80% of its budget is **voluntary, earmarked contributions** from a small number of donors and foundations, which means donors set priorities rather than the Assembly; and it has been criticised for slowness in the 2014 West African Ebola outbreak and for both over- and under-reaction during COVID-19. The **[US]** announcement of withdrawal in January 2025 removed its largest single assessed contributor and precipitated significant budget and programme reductions — the most consequential change to global health financing in a generation, and one whose downstream effects on HIV, TB and malaria programmes in Africa are still unfolding.

**Other significant actors:** the **Global Fund to Fight AIDS, Tuberculosis and Malaria**; **Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance**; **PEPFAR** (the US President's Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief, which since 2003 has been the single largest funder of HIV treatment in southern Africa); the **Gates Foundation**; **Unitaid**; the **World Bank**; **UNAIDS**; **Africa CDC** (established 2017, with a mandate strengthened after COVID-19); and **CEPI** for epidemic vaccine development.

## 4. The global burden of disease

The **Global Burden of Disease Study**, coordinated by the **Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME)** at the University of Washington, quantifies mortality, morbidity and risk factors by cause, age, sex, geography and year, using the **DALY** as its unit. The first study (1990) covered over 100 diseases across eight world regions and introduced the DALY.

**The headline structural finding** across three decades: the **epidemiological transition**. As countries grow richer, deaths shift from communicable, maternal, neonatal and nutritional causes toward non-communicable diseases and injuries; and, because people survive longer with disease, the burden shifts from **mortality** toward **disability**. Low back pain has been the leading cause of years lived with disability since at least 2010; depressive disorders are consistently near the top.

**Current mortality picture (WHO, 2021):** the top ten causes account for **39 million of 68 million** deaths. Ischaemic heart disease leads at **9.1 million (13%)**; COVID-19 was second at **8.8 million**; stroke, COPD and lower respiratory infections follow. **Seven of the top ten are non-communicable**, and those seven account for 68% of deaths within the top ten.

**The income gradient is the central fact.** In **low-income countries, eight of the top ten causes are communicable** — malaria, tuberculosis and HIV/AIDS remain in the top ten. In **lower-middle-income countries** the split is five and five. In **high-income countries, nine of the top ten are non-communicable**, and Alzheimer's disease deaths have risen nearly four-fold since 2000. A doctor trained on a European or North American curriculum, working in a district hospital in Namibia or the Eastern Cape, is working against a different top-ten list.

## 5. The southern African picture

### HIV

**[Global]** At the end of **2025** there were an estimated **41.0 million people living with HIV, 64% of them in the WHO African Region**; **1.2 million** acquired HIV and **570,000** died of HIV-related causes in 2025; **44.2 million** lives have been claimed to date. The **95-95-95** cascade stood at **88%** knowing their status, **89%** of those on antiretroviral therapy, and **95%** of those treated virally suppressed — close to the second and third targets, short on the first.

**[ZA]** South Africa runs the largest antiretroviral treatment programme in the world. The historical arc matters: the Mbeki-era denialism of 1999–2008, the Treatment Action Campaign's litigation (the 2002 Constitutional Court judgment compelling provision of nevirapine for prevention of mother-to-child transmission), the 1998–2001 litigation by 39 pharmaceutical companies against the Medicines Act that ended in withdrawal under international pressure, and the subsequent scale-up from 2004. The programme is now the country's largest public health undertaking and the principal reason life expectancy recovered from its early-2000s collapse. As of 2009 an estimated 5.6 million South Africans were living with HIV with 37% of those infected on treatment; both numbers have changed substantially since and should be re-verified against current UNAIDS and Statistics South Africa data.

**[NA]** Namibia's adult (15–49) HIV prevalence was approximately **9.7%** in 2023 — **12.7% among women and 6.6% among men**. Treatment performance is strong: **93%** of people living with HIV were aware of their status and **95%** of those diagnosed were on antiretroviral therapy. Namibia is among the countries closest to the 95-95-95 targets.

**The current risk** across the region is financing, not science: PEPFAR and Global Fund flows have supported a large share of treatment, prevention, laboratory and human-resource costs, and the 2025 disruption to US global health funding poses a direct threat to continuity of treatment — which, for antiretroviral therapy, means resistance and mortality, not merely inconvenience.

### Tuberculosis

**[Global]** In **2024**, **10.7 million** people fell ill with TB (5.8 million men, 3.7 million women, 1.2 million children) and **1.23 million** died, including **150,000** among people with HIV. TB is again the world's leading cause of death from a single infectious agent. **Africa accounted for 25%** of new cases; eight high-burden countries carry two-thirds of the global total. Drug-resistant TB remains the sharpest failure: only about **two in five** people with drug-resistant TB accessed treatment in 2024, though approximately **34,000** started the shorter **six-month** regimens (BPaL/BPaLM), which are a genuine advance over the previous 18–24 month, injectable-containing, poorly tolerated courses.

**[ZA]/[NA]** Southern Africa carries a TB burden driven by HIV co-infection, mining-related silicosis, crowded housing and incomplete treatment. Namibia's TB incidence is in the range of **450–460 per 100,000** — among the highest in the world. South Africa has among the highest absolute burdens of drug-resistant TB. GeneXpert MTB/RIF molecular testing, decentralised drug-resistant TB care, and shorter all-oral regimens are the operative programme changes of the last decade.

### **[ZA]** South Africa's public/private split and the NHI

**The split.** Approximately **84% of the population relies on the public sector** and roughly **16% have private cover**, yet about **79% of doctors work in the private sector**. Health spending was **8.1% of GDP** in 2017 (US$499.2 per capita), with government expenditure about 42% of the total — but per-capita spending diverges by an order of magnitude: roughly **US$1,500 per capita in the private sector versus US$150 in the public sector**. Only 3% of newly qualified physicians work in rural areas although about half the population lives there. About 10% of medical staff qualified abroad. Medical student enrolment rose 34% between 2000 and 2012.

This is, in international comparison, one of the most unequal health systems in the world, and it is a direct structural inheritance of apartheid-era arrangements: between 1945 and 1960 the proportion of white South Africans with health insurance rose from 48% to 80% while 95% of non-white South Africans remained dependent on the public sector.

**The NHI Act.** The **National Health Insurance Act (Act No. 20 of 2023)** was passed by the National Assembly on **13 June 2023**, by the National Council of Provinces on **6 December 2023**, and signed into law by President Cyril Ramaphosa on **15 May 2024**. Commencement is by presidential proclamation in the *Government Gazette* and is pending.

Design: a **National Health Insurance Fund** as **single purchaser and single payer**, with services **free at the point of care** for eligible users — citizens, permanent residents, refugees, inmates, certain foreign nationals, and all children. Implementation is phased and resource-dependent. Primary care is the mandatory entry point, with referral required before specialist access. **Contracting Units for Primary Health Care** are to integrate public and private provision at district level. Revenue is to come from general taxation, payroll taxes, an income-tax surcharge, and reallocation of the existing medical scheme tax credits.

**Opposition and support.** The South African Medical Association's petition against the legislation gathered approximately **78,000 signatures**. The Democratic Alliance, Economic Freedom Fighters, ACDP and IFP opposed the Bill for differing reasons. Business raised implementation capacity, cost and the absence of public–private partnership provisions. Labour unions and public-sector users largely favour it: survey support ran at **79.2% among public-sector users versus 48.9% among private scheme members**.

**Litigation.** On **24 July 2024** the Gauteng Division of the High Court ruled that certain provisions of the National Health Act 61 of 2003 — specifically the "certificate of need" requirements for health facilities — were unconstitutional. Legal commentators have argued the reasoning bears on the NHI Act's centralised planning framework. Further constitutional challenges to the NHI Act itself are in progress.

**The honest analysis.** The problem the NHI addresses is real and severe: a two-tier system in which the wealthier sixth of the population commands most of the specialist workforce and ten times the per-capita spend. The objections are also real: the Fund's governance and procurement capacity, the state's record on large public financial institutions, whether the tax base can carry the cost, whether specialists will emigrate or move to cash practice, and whether a single purchaser improves quality in facilities whose problems are managerial and infrastructural rather than financial. Both propositions can be true simultaneously, and the outcome depends on implementation detail that the Act largely defers to regulation.

### **[NA]** Namibia's health system

Structure: the **Ministry of Health and Social Services** administers the system through **14 regions and 34 districts**, organised in four tiers — outreach points, health centres, district hospitals, and referral hospitals (Windhoek Central and Katutura Intermediate as the national referral level).

Financing and coverage: about **83%** of the population relies on publicly financed services; about **17%** have private cover, with roughly a fifth of the population covered by medical aid or other voluntary insurance (PSEMAS, the public service scheme, is a major component).

Workforce: Namibia has roughly **3 health workers per 1,000 population**, above WHO minimum thresholds — but the distribution is the problem. Only **38% of health professionals serve the 80% of the population dependent on public care**, with many physicians in private practice concentrated in Windhoek, Swakopmund and Walvis Bay.

Health status (2023 unless noted): under-five mortality **41 per 1,000** live births; maternal mortality in the range **140–210 per 100,000** live births; TB **450–460 per 100,000**; adult HIV prevalence **9.7%**. Life expectancy rose from roughly 41 years in 1950 to the early-to-mid 60s by 1990, fell during the HIV epidemic, and recovered through the 2010s–2020s.

The structural challenges, in order of severity: **geography** — a population of about 2.5 million spread across 824,000 km², making the cost per capita of reaching rural populations extraordinarily high and referral times long; **workforce** — dependence on foreign-trained and South African-trained specialists, with UNAM's medical school (first graduates 2016) only beginning to change the pipeline and almost no domestic specialist training; **the double burden** — HIV and TB alongside rapidly rising non-communicable disease, hypertension, diabetes and injury; **inequality** — among the highest Gini coefficients in the world, with health access tracking it closely; and **financing** — as an upper-middle-income country by GDP per capita, Namibia is increasingly ineligible for concessional donor financing despite an internal distribution that resembles a much poorer country.

## 6. Primary care and universal health coverage

The **Declaration of Alma-Ata (1978)** established primary health care as the route to "Health for All", defining it to include not merely first-contact clinical care but health education, nutrition, safe water and sanitation, maternal and child health, immunisation, control of endemic disease, treatment of common conditions, and provision of essential drugs. The **Astana Declaration (2018)** renewed the commitment forty years on.

The evidence base for primary care is one of the more robust findings in health services research: Barbara Starfield's work established that health systems with stronger primary care orientation achieve better population health outcomes at lower cost, with greater equity. The mechanisms are first-contact accessibility, continuity, comprehensiveness and coordination.

**Universal health coverage** means all people can access the health services they need — promotive, preventive, curative, rehabilitative and palliative — of sufficient quality, **without financial hardship**. It is defined by three dimensions: **who** is covered, **what** services are covered, and **what proportion of costs** is covered — the three axes of the WHO's "UHC cube". **SDG target 3.8** commits to achieving UHC by 2030.

Progress has **stalled since 2015** according to the 2023 WHO/World Bank global monitoring report, with more than **1 billion** people facing catastrophic out-of-pocket spending and **2 billion** experiencing financial difficulty from health costs. The historical timeline of universal systems: Germany **1883**, the UK NHS **1948**, Japan **1961**, Australia **1975**, China's declared universal coverage **2009**, and **[ZA]** South Africa's NHI Act **2023/2024**.

## 7. Antimicrobial resistance

**Burden.** Bacterial AMR was associated with an estimated **4.7 million deaths in 2021**. As of **2023**, approximately **one in six** laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections worldwide showed resistance to antibiotic treatment.

**Drivers.** Misuse and overuse of antibiotics in humans and agriculture — in **2022** only **53%** of global antibiotic use was of "Access" antibiotics (the first-choice, lower-resistance-risk group under the WHO **AWaRe** classification), with roughly **45%** in the "Watch" category; poor infection prevention and control, sanitation and hygiene in healthcare settings and on farms; inadequate diagnostics, which force empirical broad-spectrum prescribing; insufficient vaccine coverage; and a research pipeline too thin to replace what is being lost.

**Priority pathogens.** *Escherichia coli* and *Klebsiella pneumoniae* lead drug-resistant Gram-negative bloodstream infections. *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* shows **75%** ciprofloxacin resistance. Multidrug-resistant TB affects **3.2%** of new TB cases. Among fungi, *Candidozyma auris* (formerly *Candida auris*) and azole-resistant *Aspergillus* are growing threats to immunocompromised patients. Antiviral resistance is an emerging concern in HIV and influenza.

**The economic problem.** Antibiotics are the one drug class whose commercial value falls the better it works and the more carefully it is used — a new last-line antibiotic should be stewarded, which means low volumes, which means no revenue. Several small antibiotic developers have gone bankrupt after successful approvals. Proposed remedies decouple revenue from volume: **market entry rewards**, the **[UK]** NHS subscription model (a fixed annual payment for access regardless of quantity used), the **PASTEUR Act** proposal in the US, and push funding through CARB-X and the AMR Action Fund.

**WHO response.** The updated **Global Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance (2026–2036)** targets a **10% reduction in AMR-associated deaths by 2030**, organised around a **One Health** approach spanning human, animal and environmental sectors. **[ZA]/[NA]** Both countries have national AMR strategy frameworks; the binding constraints regionally are laboratory capacity for surveillance, access to second-line agents, and unregulated antibiotic sale.

## 8. Pandemic preparedness

**What COVID-19 demonstrated.** That the scientific response can be extraordinarily fast (vaccine from sequence to authorisation in under a year); that manufacturing and distribution are the binding constraints, not discovery; that **vaccine nationalism** is the default behaviour of states under pressure, with COVAX badly under-delivering against its equity goals while high-income countries secured multiples of their populations' needs; that health systems fail through workforce exhaustion before they fail through beds; that surveillance and genomic sequencing capacity are decisive, and that South Africa's sequencing network — which identified and reported the Beta and Omicron variants — was punished for its transparency with travel bans; and that public trust, once lost, is not recoverable within the emergency.

**The institutional response.** The **International Health Regulations (2005)** were amended in 2024 to add a "pandemic emergency" tier and strengthen equity provisions, with entry into force staged from 2025. The **WHO Pandemic Agreement** was adopted by the World Health Assembly in **May 2025** after more than three years of negotiation, covering prevention, surveillance, technology transfer, and a pathogen access and benefit-sharing system; its practical force depends on ratification and on an annex still being negotiated. The **Pandemic Fund** at the World Bank finances preparedness capacity. **Africa CDC**'s mandate was strengthened, and continental vaccine manufacturing initiatives — including the **WHO mRNA technology transfer hub hosted by Afrigen in Cape Town**, established 2021 — aim to remove the dependency that COVID-19 exposed.

**What remains unfixed:** financing is pledged rather than assured; surveillance in the places where a spillover is most likely is thinnest; there is no enforceable mechanism to compel sample or data sharing, or to compel equitable allocation; antiviral and vaccine manufacturing capacity remains geographically concentrated; and the political appetite for preparedness declines predictably as the last emergency recedes.

## 9. Health workforce migration

The WHO estimates a global health worker shortfall in the millions, concentrated in Africa and South-East Asia — the regions with the highest disease burden.

**The flow** runs from lower- to higher-income countries: from sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia to the UK, US, Canada, Australia and the Gulf; and within Africa, from Zimbabwe, Zambia, Malawi and Namibia to South Africa, and from South Africa onward to the UK, Canada, Australia and New Zealand. **[NA]** Namibia both loses staff to South Africa and depends on foreign-trained doctors, particularly Cuban, Congolese, Zimbabwean and Nigerian clinicians, to staff district hospitals — a double dependency.

**Why people leave:** pay differentials of five- to twenty-fold; working conditions and equipment; personal safety; children's education; career and specialist training opportunities that do not exist domestically; and in some cases political conditions.

**The equity argument.** Training a doctor costs the source country a substantial public investment — in South Africa, a full MBChB is heavily state-subsidised — which is then transferred, unreimbursed, to a wealthy destination country. This has been described as a perverse subsidy from poor to rich health systems.

**Instruments.** The **WHO Global Code of Practice on the International Recruitment of Health Personnel (2010)** is voluntary and discourages active recruitment from countries on a **health workforce support and safeguards list** of the most vulnerable systems (which includes several southern African countries; the list is periodically updated). **[ZA]** South Africa restricts recruitment of foreign doctors into the public sector through a certificate-of-need process and has bilateral agreements with the UK. Return-of-service and community-service obligations (see `01`) are the domestic retention instrument in both South Africa and Namibia. Bonded scholarships, rural allowances and rural training pathways have modest but real evidence behind them; the strongest single predictor of rural practice is having been recruited from and trained in a rural area.

**The counter-argument**, which deserves fair statement: individuals have a right to move; remittances are economically significant; diaspora clinicians train and support colleagues at home; and blaming migration deflects from the domestic under-investment and management failures that make leaving rational.
