# Becoming a doctor — the training pathway country by country

**Summary.** Every country that trains doctors solves the same four problems — select, teach, license, specialise — and each solves them differently. The United States runs a graduate-entry model with a national examination sequence and a centralised matching algorithm. The United Kingdom and the Commonwealth systems it seeded (South Africa, Namibia, Australia, Ireland, India) run school-leaver entry into a combined bachelor's degree, followed by a supervised pre-registration period and then college-run specialty examinations. This file lays out each route with realistic durations, examination structures, competitiveness and cost, and flags where a figure is a moving target.

> ⚠️ Nothing here is career advice for a specific person, and nothing here is medical advice. Regulations, fees and quotas change annually. Always confirm against the primary regulator — USMLE/NRMP/ACGME, GMC, HPCSA, HPCNA, AHPRA — before making a decision.

## Key facts

| Route | Entry | Degree length | Pre-registration | Specialty training | Total to independent specialist |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **[US]** MD/DO | Graduate (bachelor's + MCAT) | 4 years | Intern year is PGY-1 of residency | 3–7 years residency + 0–3 fellowship | 11–17 years post-school |
| **[UK]** MBBS/MBChB | School-leaver (A-levels + UCAT) | 5 years (6 with intercalation/gateway) | 2-year Foundation Programme | 3 years GP; 5–8 years hospital specialty | 10–16 years post-school |
| **[ZA]** MBChB | School-leaver (NSC + NBT) | 6 years (5 at some schools) | 2-year internship + 1 year community service | 4–5 years registrar + CMSA/MMed | 13–15 years post-school |
| **[NA]** MBChB (UNAM) | School-leaver (NSSCAS/NSSCH) | 6 years | Internship + community service (HPCNA) | Usually abroad (ZA/other) | ~13–15 years post-school |
| **[EU]** national | School-leaver, mostly | 6 years | Varies; some countries none | 3–6 years, EU-recognised titles | 9–12 years post-school |
| **[AU]** MD/MBBS | Both school-leaver and graduate | 4 (graduate) or 5–6 (school-leaver) | 1 intern year + often RMO years | 3–6 years college training | 11–16 years post-school |

## [US] The United States

### Premedical

There is no premedical degree. There is a set of prerequisite courses — general biology, general chemistry, organic chemistry, physics, each with laboratory work, and increasingly biochemistry, statistics, psychology and sociology because the MCAT tests them. A bachelor's degree in any subject is the practical requirement; roughly 40% of matriculants held a non-biology bachelor's as of 2020. Alongside coursework, applicants accumulate the informal curriculum: clinical exposure (scribing, EMT work, hospital volunteering), research with a named supervisor and ideally an abstract or publication, sustained non-clinical service, and leadership. Applications go through **AMCAS** (MD) or **AACOMAS** (DO), followed by school-specific secondary essays and, for a minority of applicants, interviews — increasingly the **Multiple Mini Interview (MMI)**, a circuit of short scenario stations, rather than a single panel.

### The MCAT

The Medical College Admission Test is a roughly 7.5-hour computer-based examination in four sections: Chemical and Physical Foundations of Biological Systems; Critical Analysis and Reasoning Skills (CARS); Biological and Biochemical Foundations of Living Systems; and Psychological, Social, and Biological Foundations of Behavior. Each section is scored 118–132, total 472–528, centred near 500. Mean MCAT for entering MD students was **511.7 in 2023** (about the 82nd percentile); DO matriculants averaged around **504** in 2018. Most applicants prepare for 3–6 months full-time-equivalent.

### Admission competitiveness and cost

In 2023–24 there were **52,577 applicants** and **22,981 matriculants** across 154 MD and 38 DO programmes — a ratio often quoted as a 43.7% applicant success rate, which overstates ease because it excludes the large premedical attrition that occurs before anyone applies. Individual school acceptance rates are commonly 2–6%. Applicants typically apply to 15–30 schools; the AMCAS fee structure means an application cycle alone routinely costs US$3,000–6,000 including secondaries, MCAT registration and interview travel.

Tuition is the dominant number. Median four-year cost of attendance was reported as roughly **US$278,455 (private)** and **US$207,866 (public)** on a 2013 basis, and has risen materially since. Median education debt for graduates was around **US$200,000** for the 2019 cohort. A minority of schools — notably those with very large endowment gifts — have moved to tuition-free or need-blind full-scholarship models, but these remain the exception.

> ⚠️ US tuition, debt and application-cost figures move every year and the ones above are the last that could be verified from secondary sources in this pass. Verify against the AAMC before relying on them.

### The four-year MD/DO curriculum

The traditional "2 + 2" — two preclinical years then two clinical years — has been substantially compressed. The dominant contemporary shape is:

- **Pre-clerkship, 12–18 months.** Organ-system blocks (cardiovascular, renal, pulmonary, gastrointestinal, endocrine/reproductive, neuroscience, musculoskeletal/derm, haematology-oncology) integrating anatomy, physiology, pathology, pharmacology and microbiology for each system, with a longitudinal "doctoring" or clinical-skills thread from week one. Most schools now grade pre-clerkship pass/fail.
- **Core clerkships, 12 months.** Internal medicine, surgery, paediatrics, obstetrics & gynaecology, psychiatry, family medicine, neurology, and usually emergency medicine, in 4–12 week blocks, each ending with an **NBME subject examination** ("shelf exam").
- **Post-clerkship, 12–18 months.** Sub-internships (acting-internships) in the intended specialty, away rotations to be seen by target programmes, electives, research blocks, and the residency application itself.

DO students cover the same material plus **osteopathic manipulative medicine (OMM/OMT)** and sit COMLEX-USA rather than (or in addition to) the USMLE. Since the 2020 single-accreditation merger, DO and MD graduates compete in the same ACGME-accredited residency system.

### USMLE Steps

- **Step 1** — basic sciences, taken after pre-clerkship. **Pass/fail only for examinations taken on or after 26 January 2022** (announced 15 September 2021). Those who fail receive proximity-to-pass information and content feedback; those who pass receive no content feedback. The stated rationale was to reduce the distortion of the pre-clinical years by a single high-stakes score and to "facilitate broader, system-wide changes to improve the transition from undergraduate to graduate medical education." The practical consequence has been displacement of selection pressure onto Step 2 CK, clerkship grades, research output and school reputation.
- **Step 2 CK (Clinical Knowledge)** — a full-day multiple-choice examination on clinical management, taken in the final 18 months. Still numerically scored, and now the dominant quantitative screen in residency selection.
- **Step 2 CS (Clinical Skills)** — a standardised-patient examination, suspended in 2020 and permanently discontinued in January 2021. Schools now certify clinical-skills competence internally; international graduates satisfy the requirement through the ECFMG **Pathways** process including an OET-based English assessment.
- **Step 3** — two days, taken during residency (usually PGY-1 or PGY-2), including computer-based case simulations. Required for full unrestricted state licensure.

### The Match (NRMP)

The **National Resident Matching Program**, founded 1952, runs the Main Residency Match. Applicants apply through **ERAS**, interview (near-universally virtually since 2020), then submit a rank-order list; programmes submit theirs; a deferred-acceptance ("stable marriage") algorithm produces the assignment. The **applicant-proposing** version has been used since March 1998, which makes truthful ranking the dominant strategy — there is no gain from ranking a programme higher than your true preference.

Match Week runs Monday to Friday in mid-March. Applicants learn on Monday whether they matched; unmatched applicants enter **SOAP** (the Supplemental Offer and Acceptance Program, which replaced the pre-2010 "Scramble") with structured offer rounds against unfilled positions; on Friday everyone learns where. Some specialties — ophthalmology, urology, and the military match — run separate earlier matches.

Verified historical scale: in 2021, **48,700 registered applicants** competed for **38,106 positions**, of which 94.9% filled; US MD seniors matched at 92.8% and US DO seniors at 89.1%. In 2016, of 35,476 applicants, 75.6% obtained a PGY-1 position, with US graduates at 93.8% and international medical graduates in the 50–80% range depending on citizenship. Totals have grown roughly 2–4% a year since. NRMP reports over **6,000 participating PGY-1 and PGY-2 programmes**.

> The specific 2026 Match totals could not be extracted in this pass — see `## Open questions`.

### Residency lengths

| Specialty | Years (PGY) |
|---|---|
| Family medicine, internal medicine (categorical), paediatrics, emergency medicine, pathology | 3 (EM sometimes 4; IM 3) |
| Internal medicine, anaesthesiology, psychiatry, PM&R, neurology | 4 including intern year |
| Obstetrics & gynaecology, radiology (diagnostic), dermatology, ophthalmology, radiation oncology | 4–5 including preliminary year |
| General surgery, orthopaedic surgery, otolaryngology, urology | 5 |
| Plastic surgery (integrated), vascular surgery (integrated), cardiothoracic (integrated) | 6 |
| Neurosurgery | 7 |

Duty hours are capped by the ACGME at **80 hours per week averaged over four weeks**, with review committees able to grant exceptions to 88 hours; the 2003 rules also constrained overnight call frequency. Reported average resident salary was around **US$64,000** in 2021 — a figure that, against 60–80 hour weeks, works out to roughly US$15–20 per hour.

### Fellowship, boards and MOC

Subspecialty fellowship adds 1–3 years (cardiology 3, gastroenterology 3, critical care 2, infectious disease 2, hand surgery 1, interventional cardiology +1 on top of cardiology, and so on). Board certification is by the relevant **ABMS** member board (or AOA board for some DO pathways) after training completion, typically a written examination and, in surgical and some other fields, an oral examination.

**Maintenance of Certification (MOC)** was historically a 10-yearly high-stakes recertification examination. Sustained professional backlash led most boards to move to **longitudinal assessment** — a small number of questions delivered quarterly through an app or web platform, with open-book allowances — plus continuing medical education and practice-improvement requirements. State licence renewal is separate, generally biennial with a CME quota.

### MD-PhD

The dual-degree route interleaves the pre-clerkship years, a full PhD, then the clinical years — commonly **8 or more years**. NIH **Medical Scientist Training Program (MSTP)** grants support around 50 schools; some programmes admit only a handful of students a year. Matriculants have higher mean MCAT and GPA than MD-only entrants. Attrition averaged about 10% for 1998–2007 entrants. Outcomes are strongly academic: roughly 67% in academic centres, 4% in research institutes such as the NIH, 8% in industry; internal medicine (29%) then surgery (11%) are the commonest residencies.

## [UK] The United Kingdom

### Entry

Entry is at school-leaving age into a five-year **MBBS / MBChB / BMBS** (the letters differ by university; the qualification is equivalent). Entry requirements are typically three A-levels at A*AA–AAA including chemistry and usually biology, plus an admissions test. The **UCAT** (University Clinical Aptitude Test) is now effectively universal; the **BMAT** was retired after the 2023 cycle, with the schools that used it (including Oxford, Cambridge and Imperial) migrating to UCAT or their own instruments. Interviews are usually MMI or structured panel.

Variants: **six-year courses** exist where a **gateway/foundation year** is offered for widening-participation applicants, or where an **intercalated BSc/BMedSci** year is compulsory or elected (common at Oxford, Cambridge, UCL, Imperial and Nottingham). **Graduate-entry medicine (GEM)** is four years, requires a prior degree and often the GAMSAT, and is intensely competitive.

Home-fee tuition is capped (£9,250/year in England at last verification, with the cap subject to periodic uplift); the fifth and sixth years are NHS-bursary supported. International fees for clinical years commonly run £35,000–60,000 per year.

### The Foundation Programme

All UK graduates enter the **two-year Foundation Programme**, proposed by Sir Liam Donaldson in 2002 under *Modernising Medical Careers* and replacing the old Pre-Registration House Officer year plus the first Senior House Officer year. It is generalist by design: six four-month rotations across the two years, with mandated exposure to general medicine and general surgery, plus a compulsory community/GP placement in most schemes.

- **F1** — provisional GMC registration; on satisfactory completion the GMC grants **full registration with a licence to practise**.
- **F2** — full registration, increasing independence, and the point at which specialty applications are made.

Allocation to a foundation school was historically by an Educational Performance Measure (decile of medical-school performance) plus the Situational Judgement Test. The SJT was withdrawn and, after an interim period, allocation moved to a **preference-informed allocation** model — effectively a partly randomised national allocation — which has been contentious. Applicants who want research exposure compete separately for the **Specialised Foundation Programme (SFP)**, which protects roughly a four-month block in F2 for research, medical education or leadership.

### Specialty training and the Royal Colleges

After F2, doctors apply nationally for either:

- **Run-through training** — a single competitive entry that carries the trainee to CCT (general practice, paediatrics, obstetrics & gynaecology, clinical radiology, neurosurgery, ophthalmology and others). GP training is **three years**: 12–18 months in hospital posts, 18–24 months in primary care.
- **Uncoupled training** — core training first (Internal Medicine Training, Core Surgical Training, Core Psychiatry, Anaesthetics core), then a second competitive application into higher specialty training (ST3+).

The **Royal Colleges** own the examinations: **MRCP(UK)** (Parts 1, 2 Written, and PACES, the clinical examination) for physicianly specialties; **MRCS** then **FRCS** for surgery; **MRCPsych** for psychiatry; **MRCOG** for obstetrics and gynaecology; **MRCPCH** for paediatrics; **MRCGP** (AKT plus the recorded/simulated consultation assessment) for general practice; **FRCA** for anaesthesia; **FRCR** for radiology and clinical oncology; **FRCEM** for emergency medicine. Progress each year is judged at the **ARCP** (Annual Review of Competence Progression) against the curriculum and a portfolio of workplace-based assessments.

On completion the GMC awards the **CCT** and enters the doctor on the **Specialist Register** or **GP Register**. Consultant appointment is a separate competitive process. Total time from graduation to consultant is commonly **7–10 years and often more**, lengthened by out-of-programme research (many trainees take 3 years for a PhD/MD), less-than-full-time training, and bottlenecks at ST3 entry in popular specialties.

### GMC registration and revalidation

The GMC maintains the register and issues the **licence to practise**. **Revalidation** is the periodic confirmation that a licensed doctor remains up to date and fit to practise — normally on a five-yearly cycle, built from annual appraisal, a portfolio of supporting information (CPD, quality improvement activity, significant events, colleague and patient feedback, complaints and compliments) and a recommendation from the doctor's designated **Responsible Officer**. It applies to every licensed doctor, not only trainees or consultants.

International medical graduates without a recognised qualification route enter through **PLAB** (Parts 1 and 2) or an approved postgraduate qualification, plus English-language evidence (IELTS/OET).

## [ZA] South Africa

### The MBChB

Eleven institutions offer medical degrees, all under public universities — among them the University of Cape Town, the University of the Witwatersrand, Stellenbosch, Pretoria, KwaZulu-Natal (Nelson R. Mandela School of Medicine), the Sefako Makgatho Health Sciences University, Walter Sisulu, the Free State, and the newer programmes. Entry is direct from school on the National Senior Certificate with high mathematics and physical-science results, plus the **National Benchmark Tests (NBT)** at most schools; selection also applies statutory transformation criteria. The degree is **six years at most schools** (five at some), following the British undergraduate model.

Places are severely rationed relative to demand — single-digit-percentage acceptance rates are normal, and a substantial number of South Africans train abroad, historically in Cuba under the Nelson Mandela–Fidel Castro Medical Collaboration Programme, returning for an integration year and local examinations.

Public-university tuition for MBChB is far below US or UK international levels, typically in the low tens of thousands of rand per year, with NSFAS and university bursaries for qualifying students; the binding constraint is places, not fees.

### Internship, community service and HPCSA registration

After graduation, South African graduates must complete:

1. **A two-year internship** at an accredited hospital, rotating through the major disciplines (internal medicine, surgery, obstetrics & gynaecology, paediatrics, anaesthesia, family medicine, psychiatry, orthopaedics and emergency medicine across the two years), allocated through a national placement process.
2. **One year of community service** ("comm serv"), typically in an under-served district facility, introduced to address rural and public-sector shortages.

Only after both is the doctor entitled to **independent practice registration** with the **HPCSA**, the statutory regulator established under the Health Professions Act No. 56 of 1974 (successor to the South African Medical and Dental Council). HPCSA registers across 12 professional-board categories and runs mandatory **CPD** with a points requirement for continued registration.

> ⚠️ **[ZA]** Internship and community-service placement allocation has repeatedly produced shortfalls — cohorts of qualified graduates awaiting funded posts. Treat any statement about guaranteed placement with caution and check the current Department of Health circulars.

### Specialist training and the CMSA

Specialist training is as a **registrar** in a university-affiliated department, typically **four to five years** (up to seven for some surgical fields). Two parallel credentials exist and are usually taken together:

- **A Fellowship of the Colleges of Medicine of South Africa (CMSA)** — e.g. FCP(SA) for physicians, FCS(SA) for surgeons, FCPaed(SA), FCOG(SA), FCA(SA), FCPsych(SA), FCRad(Diag)(SA), FCEM(SA). The CMSA, founded **1954**, is the national examining body across all medical and dental specialties and reports roughly **10,000 specialist members**. Examinations are in two parts: a **Part I** (basic sciences relevant to the discipline, taken early in registrar time) and a **Part II** (written, clinical/oral, taken near the end).
- **An MMed** (Master of Medicine) from the training university, which since regulatory changes requires a **research component** — a dissertation or publishable article — that many registrars find is the rate-limiting step to finishing.

HPCSA then registers the doctor as a **specialist**; subspecialty registration (e.g. cardiology, neonatology, gastroenterology) follows a further 2–3 years of accredited subspecialty training and a **Certificate in the subspecialty** from the CMSA.

## [NA] Namibia

**UNAM School of Medicine**, founded in **2009** at the University of Namibia's **Hage Geingob Campus** in Windhoek North, is the first and only medical school in Namibia. It graduated **its first Namibian-trained doctors in 2016**. Clinical teaching is closely tied to **Windhoek Central Hospital** and to regional hospitals for outreach and referral experience. The degree is an **MBChB** on the southern-African six-year model, with intakes in the low tens to low hundreds per year.

Regulation is by the **Health Professions Councils of Namibia (HPCNA)**, an umbrella body at 36/37/46 Schonlein Street, Windhoek West, comprising five councils:

1. Medical and Dental Board (doctors and dentists)
2. Nursing Board
3. Pharmacy Board
4. Allied Health Professions Board
5. Social Work and Psychology Board

The **Medical and Dental Board** sets minimum qualifications for registration, scope of practice, conditions for practice, and registration procedures and fees. As in South Africa, Namibian graduates complete a period of **supervised internship** followed by a **community-service obligation** in the public system before unrestricted registration — the mechanism by which the state recoups a heavily subsidised training and staffs district hospitals.

Because Namibia does not run a full complement of specialist training programmes, **most Namibian doctors who specialise do so abroad**, predominantly in South Africa (registrar posts with CMSA fellowships), and increasingly in other SADC countries, Europe or Cuba. Return-of-service agreements are the usual instrument for getting them back. This dependency is a structural feature of the Namibian health workforce and is discussed further in `09_health-systems-and-global-health.md`.

> **[NA]** The precise statutory durations of Namibian internship and community service, and the regulation numbers under the Medical and Dental Act, were not retrievable from the HPCNA website in this pass. Verify directly with the Medical and Dental Board before relying on a number.

## [EU] Continental Europe, in brief

Most EU/EEA states run a **six-year school-leaver degree** followed by specialty training of 3–6 years. The **Directive 2005/36/EC** framework on the recognition of professional qualifications means a specialist title obtained in one member state is, for the listed specialties, automatically recognised in the others — the single most important structural fact about European medical mobility.

National distinctives worth knowing:

- **Germany** — six years plus a *Praktisches Jahr*; the *Staatsexamen* sequence; *Approbation* is the licence; *Facharzt* training runs 5–6 years under the state medical chambers (*Landesärztekammern*), historically with weaker time-protection and more variable supervision than the UK/US models.
- **France** — a brutally selective first year (historically PACES, reformed to PASS/L.AS), then the *Épreuves Classantes Nationales* (now *Épreuves Dématérialisées Nationales*), a national ranking examination that determines both specialty and city — a rank-order allocation conceptually similar to the Match but driven by a single examination score.
- **Netherlands** — six years, historically with a *decentrale selectie*/lottery hybrid for places, then *opleiding* to specialist.
- **Ireland** — the closest to the UK model: five- or six-year MB BCh BAO, one intern year, then Royal College of Physicians of Ireland / Royal College of Surgeons in Ireland training schemes; Irish and UK qualifications interoperate closely.
- **Poland, Hungary, Czechia, Romania, Bulgaria** — large English-language programmes for international students, EU-recognised on graduation, and a major supply route into UK, German and Scandinavian training.

## [AU] Australia and New Zealand, in brief

Both school-leaver (5–6 year MBBS/MD) and graduate-entry (4-year MD, usually via **GAMSAT**) routes exist. The **Australian Medical Council (AMC)**, established **1985**, is the independent accreditation and standards body: it accredits programmes, sets standards for assessing overseas-qualified practitioners, evaluates recognition of new specialties, and advises governments. Registration is with the **Medical Board of Australia** under **AHPRA**.

Graduates complete a **one-year internship** for general registration, then usually one or more **resident medical officer (RMO)** years before entering a specialist college training programme — RACP (physicians, 3 years basic + 3 advanced), RACS (surgery), RANZCOG, RANZCP, ACEM, ANZCA, RACGP/ACRRM (general practice). Australia relies heavily on international medical graduates; the AMC route for them is the **AMC MCQ examination** (150 questions, computer-adaptive, 3.5 hours) plus the **AMC Clinical Examination** (16 stations, pass at least 12, with compulsory gynaecology and paediatrics stations; 2 minutes to read and 8 minutes per station), or a specialist-pathway assessment by the relevant college.

## Cross-cutting observations

**Selection is not the same as aptitude.** Every system selects on a proxy — MCAT and GPA, A-levels and UCAT, NSC results and NBT, a single national ranking examination — that predicts early academic performance far better than it predicts eventual clinical quality. All of the systems above have layered on interviews, situational judgement and multiple-mini-interviews precisely because they know this, with mixed evidence of improvement.

**The bottleneck moves.** When one gate is loosened, pressure migrates. USMLE Step 1 going pass/fail did not reduce the competitiveness of dermatology; it moved the screen to Step 2 CK, research output and named-programme pedigree. The UK's removal of the SJT moved anxiety onto allocation randomness. **[ZA]** Expanding medical school intake without expanding funded internship posts moves the bottleneck to placement.

**Cost structures differ by an order of magnitude.** The US route carries roughly US$200,000 of median personal debt into a salaried training period; the UK route carries capped tuition debt repaid as an income-contingent graduate contribution; **[ZA]** and **[NA]** the state carries most of the training cost and recovers it through compulsory service. These are three different social contracts and they produce three different patterns of specialty choice, geographic distribution and emigration.

**Everything takes longer than the nominal number.** Research years, less-than-full-time training, failed examination attempts, parental leave, non-training service posts (the UK "clinical fellow" year, the Australian unaccredited registrar years, the US research year taken to be competitive for a surgical subspecialty) all add time. A realistic median in any of these systems is one to three years beyond the paper minimum.
