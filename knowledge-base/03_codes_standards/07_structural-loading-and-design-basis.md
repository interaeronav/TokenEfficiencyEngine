# Structural loading and design basis — limit states, wind, seismic, soils and the rational design

**Summary.** The design basis a southern African competent person works from is: **SANS 10160** (eight parts) for actions and the basis of design; **SANS 10100 / 10162 / 10163 / 10164** for concrete, steel, timber and masonry resistance; **SANS 10400-B** for the regulatory framing, minimum design life and the definition of who may sign; and, for residential work in South Africa, the **NHBRC Home Building Manual** and the site classification system it inherits from the SAICE/IStructE *Code of Practice for Foundations and Superstructures*. SANS 10160 replaced SABS 0160 in 2010 and is Eurocode-aligned in structure — limit states, partial factors on actions, and explicit combination rules — while retaining South African wind and seismic hazard data.

> ⚠️ Every partial factor, combination coefficient, wind speed, seismic zone factor and site class movement range in this domain lives in a purchased standard. This file gives the framework, the standard numbers and the values that could be verified from public sources. **Design values must come from the purchased current edition.**

## Limit states and the basis of design — SANS 10160-1

SANS 10160-1, *Basis of structural design and actions for buildings and industrial structures — Part 1: Basis of structural design*, sets the framework:

- **Design situations** — persistent, transient, accidental and seismic.
- **Limit states** — ultimate limit states (loss of equilibrium, rupture, excessive deformation, fatigue) and serviceability limit states (deflection, vibration, cracking, damage affecting appearance or durability).
- **Reliability differentiation** — buildings are assigned to reliability classes according to the consequence of failure, which modifies the partial factors.
- **Representative values of actions** — characteristic, combination, frequent and quasi-permanent values, with the ψ-factors that convert between them.
- **Partial factors** on permanent actions (γ_G), variable actions (γ_Q) and materials (γ_M).
- **Combination rules** — the fundamental combination for ultimate limit state, accidental combinations and the seismic combination, and the characteristic/frequent/quasi-permanent combinations for serviceability.

The structure will be immediately familiar to anyone who has used EN 1990, but **the numerical partial factors and ψ-values in SANS 10160-1 are not identical to the Eurocode values**, and neither are the load values in Part 2. Do not substitute Eurocode factors.

**Not verified from a public source:** the specific γ_G, γ_Q and ψ values, the combination expressions as numbered in SANS 10160-1:2019, and the reliability class definitions. Consult the purchased standard.

## Actions — SANS 10160-2 (self-weight and imposed loads)

Covers material densities, imposed floor loads by occupancy category, roof imposed loads, and horizontal line loads on barriers, balustrades and parapets. It is cross-referenced from SANS 10400-N 4.4.4 for the impact and line loads that glass balustrades must resist.

Still at the **2011 edition (Ed. 1.01)** — the oldest part of the series apart from the 2011 group.

## Wind loading for southern Africa — SANS 10160-3

The governing action for most low-rise southern African buildings, and the reason light roofs fail.

**SANS 10160-3:2019 (Ed. 2.01)** provides:

- the **fundamental basic wind speed map** for South Africa (a peak gust reference speed);
- **terrain categories** (from open sea/flat unobstructed to dense urban) and the corresponding exposure/roughness factors;
- **topographic factors** for hills, ridges and escarpments;
- **external and internal pressure coefficients** for building forms, including the high local suction zones at roof edges, ridges, corners and eaves;
- **dynamic/size effect factors**.

Practical points that matter on a real southern African project:

1. **The critical case for a light roof is uplift with internal pressure**, not gravity. An open dominant opening on the windward face (a garage door, a large sliding door left open) can reverse the internal pressure coefficient and roughly double the net uplift on the roof.
2. **Local pressure zones at edges and corners** govern fixings and battens, not the general roof area. Tie-down and fixing schedules must reflect this.
3. SANS 10400-B sets an absolute floor of **0,37 kPa** for dwelling houses and **0,45 kPa** for elements, which is a very low bar — in most of the interior the calculated value is higher.
4. **Namibia is not covered by the SANS 10160-3 wind map.** A Namibian design must either use an appropriate Namibian wind climate source, or adopt a conservative basic wind speed with the reasoning documented on the drawings. This is one of the most common silent assumptions in Namibian structural work.

**Not verified from a public source:** the basic wind speed values, the terrain category multipliers and the pressure coefficient tables in SANS 10160-3:2019.

## Seismic considerations — SANS 10160-4

**SANS 10160-4:2017 (Ed. 2.00)**, *Seismic actions and general requirements for buildings*, sets out where seismic design is required, based on the South African seismic hazard map, and carries general robustness requirements.

Context an agent should hold:

- Southern Africa is a stable continental interior with low natural seismicity by global standards. Much of South Africa requires no explicit seismic design.
- The significant exceptions are **mining-induced seismicity** on the Witwatersrand and Klerksdorp/Orkney goldfields, and the **Ceres–Tulbagh** area of the Western Cape which has a genuine natural hazard (the 1969 Tulbagh earthquake, M 6.3).
- Namibia is generally regarded as low seismic hazard, but **no Namibian seismic design standard exists**. A Namibian project requiring seismic consideration must justify its hazard assumption independently.
- The *general requirements* part of SANS 10160-4 — robustness, tying, avoidance of disproportionate collapse — applies more widely than the seismic actions themselves, and is often the more relevant half of the part.

**Not verified from a public source:** the SANS 10160-4 seismic hazard zonation map, the zone factors, the importance factors and the threshold below which seismic design may be omitted.

## Geotechnical basis — SANS 10160-5 and the site investigation

**SANS 10160-5:2021 (Ed. 1.02)**, *Basis for geotechnical design and actions*, establishes geotechnical categories, the extent of ground investigation appropriate to each, earth pressures, and the geotechnical limit states.

**NBR Regulation F3** requires a geotechnical investigation in defined circumstances, and Regulation A19(1)(c) makes that a trigger for the appointment of an approved competent person. This is the regulatory hook: the moment a geotechnical investigation is required, an owner must formally appoint someone under A19 to take responsibility for it.

## Soil classification and NHBRC site classes **[ZA]**

The NHBRC operates under the **Housing Consumers Protection Measures Act 95 of 1998**. Every home in South Africa built by a registered home builder for a housing consumer must be **enrolled** with the NHBRC — the rules require submission **15 days before commencement of construction**, with **soil classification certified by a competent person**.

**The classification system.** The NHBRC residential unit enrolment form (EF003) directs the user to "classify individual sites in accordance with the site class designations as set out in **Table 2.1 of the SAICE/I.Struct.E Code of Practice for Foundations and Superstructures**". The NHBRC does not invent the classification; it adopts the joint SAICE / Institution of Structural Engineers code, and its own **Home Building Manual** carries the corresponding foundation solutions.

**Site classes used on the NHBRC enrolment checklist:**

| Group | Classes | Nature of the ground problem |
|---|---|---|
| Rock | **R** | Rock at or near founding level |
| Heave | **H, H1, H2, H3** | Expansive (heaving) clay soils, in increasing order of expected total heave |
| Collapse | **C, C1, C2** | Collapsible soils — typically aeolian sands and some colluvium that collapse on wetting under load, in increasing order of expected total collapse settlement |
| Settlement / compressible | **S, S1, S2** | Compressible soils giving consolidation settlement, in increasing order |
| Problem | **P** | Other problem soils and conditions — controlled or uncontrolled fill, made ground, mining-affected ground, highly variable profiles |
| Dolomite | **D1, D2, D3, D4** | Dolomite land, in increasing order of sinkhole/subsidence hazard. **Each dolomite class must be paired with a standard soil class.** |

**Documentation the NHBRC requires, by class** (2025 enrolment checklist):

- **Standard soil types C, C1, H, H1, S, S1, R** — a geotechnical report, plus the site soil class designation on **Appendix B3**.
- **Problem soil types H2, H3, C2, S2, P** — a comprehensive geotechnical report; **SANAS-accredited compaction test results** where bulk earthworks are required; a stormwater management report for affected sites; and a **B4 certificate** for dolomitic sites with Council for Geoscience recommendations.
- **Dolomite D1–D3** — a dolomite stability investigation, a Council for Geoscience letter of comments, and a B4 certificate.
- **Dolomite D4** — Phase 1 and Phase 2 geotechnical reports, **two independent peer reviews** (by two Level 4 competent persons), a municipal risk management plan, and professional indemnity insurance.
- All enrolments — **Appendix B1** competent person certification; drawings signed by the registered professional; competent person declarations on all technical submissions.

**Not verified from a public source:** the numeric ranges of total soil movement (in mm) that define each class in Table 2.1, and the specific foundation solutions the NHBRC Home Building Manual prescribes against each class (stiffened raft, split-construction, cellular raft, soil raft, piles, soil replacement, etc.). These are the operative design values and **must be taken from the SAICE/IStructE code and the NHBRC Home Building Manual**.

**Dolomite land** is separately regulated in SANS 10400-B, which defines a *competent person (dolomite land)* as a person registered either as a Professional Engineer, or holding a BSc in geology or engineering geology with Natural Scientific Professions Act registration, **plus dolomite-specific experience**. Dolomite work in Gauteng and North West is the single highest-liability geotechnical exposure in South African residential construction.

**[NA]** Namibia has **no NHBRC equivalent and no mandatory site classification regime**. Namibian sites nonetheless present the same problems — collapsible aeolian sands are widespread in the Kalahari and the northern sandveld, calcrete horizons complicate founding, and shallow perched water tables occur in the Cuvelai floodplain of Ohangwena. Applying the SAICE/IStructE classification voluntarily is the sensible professional default, and should be stated as an assumption rather than presented as compliance.

## The rational design — what it is and who may sign it

A **rational design** is a design based on established engineering principles that satisfies the functional regulation without following the deemed-to-satisfy route. A **rational assessment** is the same exercise applied to an existing building or an existing system.

### When a rational design is required **[ZA]**

Regulation A19(1) lists the triggers: a rational design or rational assessment required in terms of Regulations **AZ4(1)(b)(ii), A1(3), A23(4), G1(3), O4, P2(2), Q3, R3, T1(2) or W4**, or by a part of SANS 10400, or a **geotechnical investigation required under Regulation F3**.

In practice, the everyday triggers on a house are:
- foundations outside the SANS 10400-H deemed-to-satisfy rules (problem soils, deep fill, dolomite, steep slopes);
- masonry wall panels exceeding the SANS 10400-K tables (long walls, tall walls, large openings, gables);
- roof structures outside SANS 10400-L timber roof construction (long spans, unusual geometry, prefabricated trusses — which come with the truss supplier's engineer's design);
- retaining structures and free-standing walls beyond the Part K rules;
- swimming pools, basements, and anything below ground;
- fire engineering solutions under SANS 10400-T Annex B;
- energy compliance by simulation under SANS 10400-XA.

### Who may sign

The competent person must be **registered in an appropriate category** under the Architectural Profession Act 44 of 2000, the Engineering Profession Act 46 of 2000, the Natural Scientific Professions Act 27 of 2003, or another relevant Act, **and** be generally recognised as having the necessary experience and training in the specific field.

SANS 10400-B defines:

- **Competent person (structures)** — registered under the Engineering Profession Act 46 of 2000 as a Professional Engineer or Professional Engineering Technologist, and generally recognised as having the necessary experience and training to undertake rational assessments or rational designs in the field of structural systems.
- **Competent person (civil engineering)** — Engineering Profession Act registration, a tertiary qualification in civil engineering, and recognised experience and training.
- **Competent person (dolomite land)** — as above.

> ⚠️ Registration alone is not competence. The standard requires the person to be "generally recognised as having the necessary experience and training" in the specific field. A Professional Engineer whose experience is in mechanical plant is not a competent person (structures) for a building.

### What the appointment carries

- The **owner** makes the appointment and retains the person (A19(1)).
- The terms must state that the person undertakes **all** the duties required by the Regulations; the person declares acceptance on **Form 2** (A19(4)).
- The person takes responsibility for satisfying the relevant functional regulation **in its entirety**, not just their element (A19(7)).
- Duties include **inspection** — defined in SANS 10400-A as general inspection at intervals necessary in accordance with accepted professional practice to be satisfied that the design assumptions are valid, the design is being correctly interpreted and the work is generally in accordance with the designs, **but excluding detailed supervision and day-to-day inspection**.
- On completion the person certifies the structural, fire protection or fire installation system on **Form 4** under s 14(2A) of the Act — which the local authority requires before issuing the occupancy certificate.
- Design documentation must be retained and producible for **10 years** after completion.
- If the person cannot continue, the owner must appoint a replacement who takes over responsibility for **work already designed or built as well as the balance** (A19(2)) — which is why competent persons are reluctant to take over another's structure without a full re-assessment.

**[NA]** In Namibia there is no Regulation A19 and no Form 2 or Form 4. The equivalent mechanism is: the local authority's regulations require structural computations with the plan submission (Windhoek Reg 6 does so expressly), and a plan submitted by a professional must carry that professional's **registration number** with the Engineering Council of Namibia or NCAQS. The chain of responsibility is contractual and professional rather than statutory.
