# Namibia — how building control actually works

**Summary.** **[NA]** Namibia has no national building act and no national building code. Building control is a *local authority* power, conferred by section 94(1)(ag) of the Local Authorities Act 23 of 1992 and exercised through each council's own gazetted building regulations. Section 94B of the same Act allows those regulations to incorporate SABS/SANS standards by reference without publishing their text — this is the only mechanism by which SANS acquires legal force in Namibia. Outside proclaimed local authority areas the picture changes completely: in declared settlement areas a regional council administers a much thinner regime, and on communal land — the majority of Namibia's land, including most of Ohangwena Region — there is no building plan approval regime at all, and land is held under the Communal Land Reform Act 5 of 2002 through Traditional Authority allocation ratified by a Communal Land Board.

> ⚠️ Do not tell a Namibian client that "SANS 10400 applies". It applies only where their local authority's regulations incorporate it, or where a contract, lender, insurer or engineer requires it. Establish the jurisdiction first.

## The Local Authorities Act 23 of 1992 as the source of building control

### Section 30 — what a council may do

Section 30(1) lists the powers of a local authority council. The ones that matter to a building project:

- (a) supply water to residents for household, business or industrial purposes (subject to Part VI);
- (b) provide and carry on a system of **sewerage and drainage** (Part VII);
- (c) refuse removal and disposal;
- (e) construct and maintain **streets and public places** (Part IX);
- (f) supply electricity or gas (Part X, read with the Electricity Act — now Act 4 of 2007);
- (g) operate sand, clay, stone or gravel quarries and brick and tile works, and dispose of the products;
- (i) establish a **housing scheme** with the Minister's prior approval;
- (o) establish and maintain any building or structure for a community requirement;
- (u) determine by notice in the Gazette the charges, fees and other moneys payable for any service, amenity or facility.

Section 30 does **not** itself confer building plan approval powers. That comes from the regulation-making power.

### Section 94 — the regulation-making power

Section 94(1) permits a local authority council, **after consultation with the Minister**, to make regulations by notice in the Gazette on a long list of subjects. The building-relevant ones:

- **(ag)** the prohibition, restriction, regulation and control of the construction, erection and use, and the regulation and control of maintenance and demolition of **buildings, including informal housing structures or buildings in reception areas** contemplated in section 91A (paragraph amended by Act 24 of 2000);
- **(i)** under the streets head: the regulation and control of **street lines and building lines**;
- **(e)** the regulation and control of **scaffolding** and decorations in streets, and the erection and removal of temporary platforms and structures;
- **(ap)** the regulation and control of the use of immovable property;
- **(aq)** the enclosure of residential buildings with **solid boundary walls**;
- **(as)** the registration, and cancellation of registration, of **contractors undertaking electrical, plumbing or drain-laying work**;
- (a)–(d) water supply, water fittings and fixtures, their size, capacity, materials, strength and workmanship, and connection/disconnection.

Section 94A gives the Minister a parallel regulation-making power.

### Section 94B — how SANS becomes law in Namibia

This is the pivotal provision. Inserted by Act 24 of 2000:

- **94B(1)** — the s 94 power includes the power to **incorporate the provisions of any standard publication, or any part of it, by reference**, without publishing the text in the Gazette.
- **94B(2)** — "standard publication" means "any code of practice, standard specification or standard regulations published or issued by any body, organisation or authority established with the object of promoting standardisation, **whether in Namibia or elsewhere**, and which Namibia is authorised to apply in terms of an agreement entered into with that body, organisation or authority."
- **94B(3)** — any amendment or substitution of a provision of an incorporated standard publication must be **specifically incorporated** by the local authority. In other words, a council that incorporated SANS 10400-K:2011 does *not* automatically get SANS 10400-K:2025 — it must re-incorporate.
- **94B(4)** — while such regulations are in force the council must keep copies of the standard publication, any supplementary standard incorporated by reference in it, and any amendments, **open to inspection free of charge at the council's offices during office hours.**

> ⚠️ 94B(4) is practically important and widely unknown: where a Namibian council has incorporated a SANS standard, **you are entitled to read that standard free of charge at the council's offices.** You may not copy it, but you can verify a clause without buying it. This is the cheapest lawful route to a verified Namibian value.

The 94B(2) requirement that "Namibia is authorised to apply" the standard "in terms of an agreement entered into with that body" is the legal reason the NSI's relationship with SABS and ISO matters. It also means an agent should not assume a council can simply cite any foreign standard; there must be an agreement.

### Section 78 — the building compliance certificate as the enforcement chokepoint

Section 78 prohibits the Registrar of Deeds from registering a transfer of immovable property in a local authority area unless a conveyancer's certificate confirms both that all rates, fees and charges have been paid **and** that "a building compliance certificate by the relevant local authority has been issued, unless the immovable property is unimproved".

Practical consequence: in a Namibian proclaimed town, **unapproved building work eventually surfaces at the point of sale**. This is the functional equivalent of the South African occupancy certificate as a compliance lever, but it bites later — at transfer rather than at first occupation. It is the single most common reason Namibian owners retrospectively submit "as-built" plans.

## The City of Windhoek Building Regulations — the reference example

Windhoek's regulations are the most accessible published Namibian municipal building regulations and are widely used as a template. They were **promulgated by Government Notice 57 of 1969 in Official Gazette 2992 of 28 April 1969** (i.e. under the pre-independence South West Africa administration) and have been amended many times — the published consolidation lists GN 92/69, 62/70, 16/72, 148/80, 128/85, 225/87, 15/88, 108/97, 243/95, 329/95, 274/97, 207/98 and 88/2002.

**Structure:**

| Chapter | Subject |
|---|---|
| I | General — definitions, delegation, responsibility |
| II | Repeal of existing regulations |
| III | Administration — plans, approvals, inspections, offences |
| IV | Structural timber — definitions, quality, preservation, design stresses, connectors, joints, piling |
| V | *Repealed by GN 15 of 1988* |

> ⚠️ The regulations are of 1969 vintage and are partly pre-metric. They reference **superseded SABS specifications**: S.A.B.S. 5 (graded South African softwood timber), S.A.B.S. 61 (black bolts and nuts), S.A.B.S. 538 (high temperature wood preserving creosotes), S.A.B.S. 563 (structural timber), S.A.B.S. 629 (softwood flooring boards), plus C.K.S. co-ordinating specifications for wood preservatives and BS 1579 (connectors for timber). None of those is a current SANS number. In practice the Building Control division applies contemporary professional standards and the plan checklist rather than the letter of Chapter IV.

**Selected numeric requirements from the published text** (note the imperial units — these are quoted as published):

- Underfloor clearance (Reg 38): not less than **18 inches** between floor joists and the ground; **12 inches** between bearers and the ground.
- Underfloor ventilation (Reg 37): at least **½ square inch of unobstructed air passage per square foot** of ground floor area; openings not more than **8 ft** apart; at least one opening within **2 ft 6 in** of each corner.
- Erf coverage (Reg 29B): maximum **50 %** of total area, with discretionary increase to **75 %**; open space at rear or lateral side not less than **30 m²**; open passage not less than **3 ft** wide.
- Cellar/basement (Reg 29B): ceiling not more than **1,33 m** above ground level.
- Vehicular access (Reg 30): minimum width **3,00 m**, minimum height **3 m**; required only for properties exceeding **1 500 m²**.
- Structural timber (Regs 62, 63): floor beams and joists not less than **1½ inches** nominal thickness; roof timber minimum **1½ inches** with exceptions.
- Bolt spacing (Reg 65): minimum centre-to-centre **4,5 × bolt diameter**.

**Administration:**

- **Reg 6** — written application on the prescribed form with **plans in duplicate**. Plan content: block plan at not less than **1:1 000** showing the building's relation to structures and streets; plans, sections and elevations at not less than **1:100** showing foundation and wall heights and thicknesses, ground floor level, damp-course positions, room and passage dimensions and positions, position/form/dimensions of all windows, doors, chimneys and ventilating openings, sanitary conveniences and the intended mode of drainage; a schedule of specifications describing materials and methods; **structural computations** proving sufficient strength; and the owner's or builder's signature on all plans.
- **Reg 8** — the Council must signify approval or disapproval **within 28 days** of receipt, extended to **35 days** where public holidays intervene.
- **Reg 9** — approval lapses if construction has not commenced within **12 months** of sanction.
- **Reg 10** — buildings must be completed within **12 months** of commencement of building operations.
- **Reg 2** — the Council may delegate discretionary powers to an employee who is, in the Council's opinion, skilled in the matter.
- **Reg 3** — Council approval and inspection does **not relieve the owner of full responsibility**.
- **Reg 18** — power of entry for inspection and examination of utilities.

**Fees (as published in the consolidated regulations; verify current gazetted tariff with the Council):**

| Work | Fee |
|---|---|
| Dwelling not exceeding 30 m² | N$150,00 |
| 30–70 m² | N$200,00 |
| 70–130 m² | N$600,00 |
| 130–400 m² | N$800,00 |
| Exceeding 400 m² | N$1 400,00 |
| Boundary wall / swimming pool | N$200,00 |
| Re-inspection (per subsequent attendance) | N$200,00 |

> ⚠️ These fee figures are from the published consolidation and are almost certainly out of date. Local authority fees are set by notice in the Gazette under LAA s 30(1)(u) and are revised annually. Always confirm the current tariff with the council before quoting.

**Penalties (Reg 31(b)):** fine not exceeding **N$1 000** on first conviction; **N$2 000** on a second or subsequent conviction for the same offence; or, in default of payment, imprisonment not exceeding **six months**; plus, for a continuing offence, an additional fine not exceeding **N$100 for each day** the offence continues. Chapter IV (structural timber) carries its own, much lower, penalty under Reg 80(c): R100 or three months, plus R2 per day continuing.

## The Windhoek building permit process in practice

Per the City's *Guidelines for Building Permits* (Office of the CEO):

1. **Application** — completed by the owner, or by an agent under a formal power of attorney.
2. **Submission** — the completed form with the required drawings and other documents, plus the applicable **gazetted fee**, lodged at a Customer Care Centre.
3. **Permit issuance** — after regulatory review against **Building, Fire, Health and Planning Scheme** standards.
4. **Inspections** — the permit requires mandatory inspections by a Council-authorised agent, to ensure the health, safety and welfare of the building's future occupants.
5. **Certificate of Occupation** — a formal certificate from an authorised Council agent is required before legal occupancy.

The Building Control division also issues **plan copies** (two working days) and **building compliance certificates** on completion. Plans may be submitted by the property owner, or by a **registered architect, surveyor or professional engineer quoting their registration number**.

The submission form itself requires: erf number and township; description of work; street from which entrance is obtained; construction materials (foundation, walls, damp course, roof cover); **ventilation percentage and window area per room**; swimming pool volume; building value in N$; total built-up area, individual dwelling areas, outbuilding areas and total new additional area; owner's details and signature; and the designer's details, professional title, **registration number** and signature. Plans are not accepted unless the form is complete and the fees are paid.

## Other Namibian local authorities

Each municipal, town and village council makes its own regulations under LAA s 94. There is no published national consolidation. Practical guidance for an agent:

- **Assume nothing about a council's regulations without obtaining them.** Ask the council for the Government Notice number and date of its current building regulations, and for the list of standard publications incorporated under s 94B.
- Smaller town and village councils frequently operate on a template derived from a larger council's regulations, or on regulations inherited from the pre-independence administration, with an informal practice of accepting plans that satisfy SANS 10400 and a professional's signature.
- The **plan checklist** used by a council's building control office is often more operationally important than the regulations themselves. Windhoek publishes a Building Plan Submission Check List; request the equivalent from the council in question.

## The Namibian Standards Institution and the Standards Act 18 of 2005

- **s 2** establishes the **Namibian Standards Institution (NSI)** as a juristic person and the national standards body.
- **s 6** establishes the **Namibian Standards Council** (four government members, four private sector members, plus the CEO as a non-voting participant) to manage and control the NSI.
- **s 20** empowers the NSI to set, establish and issue **Namibian standards**, publishing a notice in the Gazette identifying each standard by title, number and date (s 20(4)).
- **s 20(2)** permits a Namibian standard to incorporate provisions of international standards issued by bodies such as ISO and IEC; **s 20(3)** provides for automatic incorporation of amendments to referenced international standards.
- **s 20(6)**: "compliance with a Namibian standard **is not compulsory**, but compliance with such a standard may be declared compulsory by the Minister by regulation."

The NSI also administers, among others, the **Cement Regulations under the Standards Act 2005 (GN 259)** — a compulsory specification for cement placed on the Namibian market — and the **Health and Safety Regulations GN 156 of 1997** made under the labour legislation.

Namibia has **no national accreditation body**; the South African National Accreditation System (SANAS) provides Namibia's accreditation needs. The NSI is a Correspondent Member of ISO.

**The national building code project.** The NSI has been working since 2012 on new Namibian building regulations, consulting approximately 89 institutions, with a proposal to base the framework on the structural **Eurocodes** implemented in two-year phases, adopting complementing ISO standards step by step and establishing Nationally Defined Parameters. As at the last public reporting located, this had not been promulgated. **Treat "Namibia is adopting the Eurocodes" as a stated intention, not a current legal position.**

## Why SANS/SABS is nonetheless the reference in Namibian practice

Five independent forces converge:

1. **Materials.** Most cement, steel, timber, brick, roofing, sanitaryware and electrical product sold in Namibia is manufactured in or imported through South Africa and carries a SANS conformity mark. Specifying anything else means specifying an unavailable product.
2. **Professionals.** Namibian architects and engineers are trained in South Africa or to South African curricula, and many hold dual registration. Their professional indemnity and standard of care are framed by SANS design codes.
3. **Legal hook.** LAA s 94B expressly contemplates incorporating standards issued by a standardisation body "in Namibia **or elsewhere**".
4. **Lenders and insurers.** Namibian banks financing a build, and insurers underwriting it, work from South African templates that reference SANS.
5. **Absence of an alternative.** Namibia has no published national building code. In its absence SANS 10400 is the only coherent deemed-to-satisfy body of rules available in the region.

What this means for an AI agent: **cite SANS as the technical reference and as the professional standard of care, but never as Namibian law** unless you have seen the specific council regulation that incorporates it.

## Proclaimed towns vs settlements vs communal land

Namibia's land is administered in three distinct regimes. Establishing which one applies is the first question in any Namibian building enquiry.

### 1. Proclaimed local authority areas (municipal, town, village councils)

- Land is normally surveyed, registered freehold (or sectional title), with a title deed and an erf number.
- A **zoning scheme** under the Urban and Regional Planning Act 5 of 2018 controls land use, coverage, height, building lines and parking. Zoning schemes have "the force of law" once published (s 49) and must be reviewed after five years (s 55).
- The council's **building regulations under LAA s 94(1)(ag)** apply: plans must be submitted and approved before building, inspections are mandatory, and a certificate of occupation and/or building compliance certificate closes the file.
- **LAA s 78** blocks transfer without a building compliance certificate.
- Services (water, sewer, electricity, refuse, roads) are the council's responsibility under LAA s 30.

### 2. Declared settlement areas (Regional Councils Act 22 of 1992)

- A settlement area is declared under the Regional Councils Act and is administered by the **regional council**, not a local authority council.
- Regional councils prepare **regional structure plans** under URPA s 25. Where they fail to do so, the Permanent Secretary may prepare the plan and recover the cost.
- For land outside a local authority area, the **Minister** rather than a council decides zoning scheme preparation (URPA s 43), rezoning (s 56) and most other planning applications (Chapter 9, Part 3).
- **Building control in settlements is thin.** A regional council does not have the LAA s 94(1)(ag) power (that is a local authority council power). In practice, plan approval in a settlement may be handled by the regional council administratively, by the responsible ministry, or not at all. **This must be confirmed settlement by settlement.**

### 3. Communal land (Communal Land Reform Act 5 of 2002)

This is the regime that covers most of Ohangwena Region outside the proclaimed towns.

- **s 17** — all communal land **vests in the State in trust for the benefit of the traditional communities** residing in those areas. It cannot be bought or sold and cannot be held in freehold.
- **Allocation (s 22)** — the **Chief or Traditional Authority** receives a written application for a customary land right and may investigate or hold a hearing before granting or refusing it.
- **Ratification (s 24)** — the **Communal Land Board** must confirm the allocation within 30 days. It may ratify, refer back, or refuse where the land overlaps an existing right, exceeds the prescribed size limit, or is required for a public purpose.
- **Registration (s 25)** — a ratified right is registered and a certificate issued.
- **Size limit** — the maximum area for a customary land right is **20 hectares** under the regulations, adjustable by the Minister.
- **Duration and succession (s 26)** — a customary land right endures for the life of the holder and reverts to the Chief on death, for reallocation to the surviving spouse (if consenting), then to children per customary law, or reopened for allocation.
- **Leasehold (ss 30–36)** — for non-traditional uses (a lodge, a business, an institutional project), a **right of leasehold** is granted by the Communal Land Board with the Traditional Authority's consent. This is the appropriate instrument for a commercial or institutional building on communal land, and it is registrable — which matters for financing.
- **Fencing (s 18, s 28)** — new fencing without authorisation is prohibited; pre-existing fences require an application for retention. Homestead, cattle pen, water trough and crop field fences that predate 1 March 2003 are permitted under the regulations.
- **Improvements (s 40)** — compensation for improvements is dealt with when a right terminates.

**[NA]** **What building control applies on communal land? In the ordinary case, none.**

- The Local Authorities Act does not reach outside a proclaimed local authority area, so there are no s 94(1)(ag) building regulations.
- The Urban and Regional Planning Act's township establishment, zoning and subdivision machinery is not extended to communal tenure — the Act does not provide a mechanism for applying those frameworks to customary tenure. Land outside a local authority is dealt with by ministerial decision, not by a council building control office.
- There is no building control officer, no plan approval, no inspection regime and no occupancy certificate.

**What does still apply on communal land:**

| Requirement | Instrument | Applies? |
|---|---|---|
| Right to occupy and build | Communal Land Reform Act 5 of 2002 — customary land right or leasehold, allocated by the Traditional Authority and ratified/registered by the Communal Land Board | **Yes — this is the essential permission** |
| Environmental clearance | Environmental Management Act 7 of 2007 and the 2012 EIA Regulations | **Yes** for a listed activity, anywhere in Namibia |
| Electrical certification | Electricity Act 4 of 2007; the regional distributor (NORED in northern Namibia) requires a compliant installation before energising | **Yes**, wherever a grid connection is taken |
| Water | Water Resources Management Act 11 of 2013; borehole drilling and abstraction permits; the Directorate of Rural Water Supply for rural schemes | **Yes** |
| Occupational health and safety | Labour Act 11 of 2007 and the health and safety regulations | **Yes** — employer duties apply to any workplace including a construction site |
| Professional registration | Architects' and Quantity Surveyors' Act 13 of 1979 (NCAQS); Engineering Profession Act 18 of 1986 (Engineering Council of Namibia) | **Yes** — who may call themselves an architect or engineer is regulated regardless of where the building is |
| Building plan approval | — | **No** |
| Zoning / land use scheme | — | **No** |
| Occupancy certificate | — | **No** |
| SANS 10400 as law | — | **No** |

> ⚠️ "No building control" is not the same as "no obligations". A structure on communal land still has to be safe, and the designer, engineer and builder carry the ordinary common-law and professional duties of care. A donor-funded, bank-financed or institutional project on communal land will almost always be *contractually* required to comply with SANS 10400 and SANS 10160 even though no council will ever inspect it. In the absence of a regulator, the specification and the engineer's certification become the only quality control.

## The Ohangwena / Okongo case

Ohangwena Region has three local authorities and two declared settlements:

| Name | Status | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Eenhana** | Town Council | Proclaimed a town 15 April 1999; autonomous from 1 July 2002; population over 24 000 |
| **Helao Nafidi** | Town Council | Proclaimed September 2003; five major townships over 7 221 ha; population near 30 000 |
| **Okongo** | **Village Council** | Declared a **settlement area in 1999**; **proclaimed a Village Council in 2015**; five council seats |
| Ongenga | Settlement | Ongenga constituency; ~107 households, ~500 people |
| Omungwelume | Settlement | Ongenga constituency; ~160 households, ~960 people |

**Okongo specifics.** Okongo is the district capital of Okongo Constituency in eastern Ohangwena. The 2023 census recorded **3 564** residents (2 236 in 2011 — about 3,96 % annual growth). It has electricity, water and sanitation, a post office, supermarkets, banking, the **62-bed Okongo District Hospital**, two pre-primary schools, a primary school, a combined school and a secondary school, a community library with internet, a NaTIS vehicle registration office (2020) and tarred road access plus a landing strip for small aircraft.

**What this means for building control at Okongo:**

1. **[NA]** **Inside the proclaimed Village Council boundary**, Okongo Village Council is a **local authority council** under the Local Authorities Act 23 of 1992. It therefore *has* the s 94(1)(ag) power to make building regulations, the s 30 service powers, and the s 78 building compliance certificate is engaged on transfer. **Whether it has actually gazetted building regulations, and what they contain, must be established directly with the Village Council** — a newly proclaimed village council may still be operating on inherited settlement-era practice, or may have adopted a template from a larger council.
2. **[NA]** **Immediately outside that boundary the land is communal**, held under the Communal Land Reform Act 5 of 2002. To build there you need a **customary land right** (residential/farming, up to the prescribed size limit) allocated by the Chief or Traditional Authority and ratified and registered by the **Ohangwena Communal Land Board**, or — for an institutional or commercial building — a **right of leasehold** from the Board with Traditional Authority consent. There is no plan approval, no building inspector and no occupancy certificate.
3. **The boundary matters enormously and is not obvious on the ground.** A proclaimed village boundary is defined by the Government Notice that proclaimed it. The first question on any Okongo project is: *is the site inside or outside the proclaimed Village Council area?* Get the proclamation notice and the surveyed boundary from the Village Council or the Ministry of Urban and Rural Development before designing anything.
4. **Financing and tenure interact.** A customary land right is not freehold and is generally not mortgageable; a registered leasehold under ss 30–36 is the instrument that supports institutional finance on communal land. This drives project structuring more than any building regulation does.
5. **Services.** Electricity in northern Namibia is distributed by **NORED**; bulk water by **NamWater** with rural schemes through the Directorate of Rural Water Supply; a village council supplies reticulation within its area under LAA s 30(1)(a)–(b). On communal land, expect borehole water and on-site sanitation — which brings **SANS 10400-Q** (VIP toilets) squarely into play as the best available technical reference even though it has no legal force there.
