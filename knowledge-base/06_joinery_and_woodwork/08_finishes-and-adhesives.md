# Finishes and adhesives — schedules, systems and hot-climate practice

**Summary.** Finishing fails for three reasons: inadequate preparation, wrong film build, and wrong conditions. In hot, dry inland southern Africa the third dominates — solvent flashes off before the film levels, glue open times collapse, and substrate temperatures on a sun-facing wall exceed what the coating was designed for. Adhesive selection is governed by **EN 204 durability classes D1–D4**; almost all interior joinery should be D3 as a minimum, and anything near water should be D4 or polyurethane.

## Key facts

- **EN 204 minimum shear strengths (EN 205 test):** all classes ≥ **10 N/mm²** after 7 days at 20 °C / 65 % RH. D2: ≥ 8 N/mm² after 3 h cold water + 7 days conditioning. D3: ≥ 2 N/mm² after 4 days cold water, and ≥ 8 N/mm² after 4 days cold water + 7 days conditioning. D4: ≥ 4 N/mm² after 6 h boiling water + 2 h cold water.
- **D3** = interior with frequent short-term exposure to running or condensed water, or heavy humidity; exterior not weather-exposed. **D4** = interior with frequent long-term water exposure; exterior weather-exposed **with an adequate surface coating**.
- **2K PU wood lacquer (Mipa PU 230-10):** mix **10 : 1** by weight; pot life ~**8 h at 20 °C**; spray viscosity **20–25 s (DIN 4 mm)**; nozzle **1.2–1.3 mm**; **2.0–2.5 bar**; 10–15 % dilution; 2–4 passes; **50–60 µm dry film** per coat; dust-dry 15–20 min, handleable 50–60 min, stackable 2 h, fully cured **2–3 days**; apply **from +10 °C and up to 80 % RH**.
- **Osmo Polyx-Oil Original:** natural oils and waxes in de-aromatised white spirit; coverage **24 m²/L** clear by microfibre roller (30 m²/L coloured, 60 m²/L by disc machine); dry ~**8–10 h** clear, ~24 h coloured at 23 °C / 50 % RH; final sanding **P120–150** for floors, **P120–240** for furniture.
- **Woodoc 10 Polywax Sealer** **[ZA]**: coverage **±10 m²/L per coat**, ±3.3 m²/L for a three-coat system; compatible with mineral turpentine; dilution not recommended; lead- and formaldehyde-free.
- **Edgebanding glue:** EVA **170–200 °C**, 120–200 g/m², heat resistance 60–80 °C. PUR **120–140 °C**, 80–150 g/m², heat resistance 120 °C+, waterproof. Open time ~30–45 s for both.

## 1. Sanding schedules

Sanding is the finish. Every scratch you leave will be magnified by the first coat.

| Work | Schedule |
|---|---|
| Machine-planed solid timber, clear finish | P100 → P120 → P150 → P180 (→ P220 for oil) |
| Sawn or hand-planed solid timber | P80 → P100 → P120 → P150 → P180 |
| MDF for paint | P150 → P180; edges P120 then sealer, then P180 |
| Veneered board | P150 → P180 only. **Never** start below P120 — the veneer is 0.5–0.6 mm |
| Between coats, lacquer | P320–P400 dry, or a fine abrasive pad |
| Between coats, oil | P320 or abrasive pad, or nothing at all on Polyx-Oil |
| Final for hardwax oil | P150 (floors) / P180–P240 (furniture) — **do not go finer**, or the oil cannot key |

**Rules.** Never skip more than one grit. Sand with the grain for the last two grits on visible solid timber. Vacuum and tack-cloth between grits — not compressed air, which drives dust into the pores. Raise the grain with a damp cloth before the final grit on anything getting a water-based coating, let dry, then de-nib.

## 2. Coating systems

**Nitrocellulose (NC) lacquer.** Fast, easy to repair, low film build, poor chemical and heat resistance. Solvent-release only — it never crosslinks, so any later NC coat re-dissolves and melts into the previous one. Still the shop default for furniture where repairability matters more than durability. **Not for kitchen doors or worktops.**

**Pre-catalysed (pre-cat) lacquer.** NC modified with an acid catalyst added at the factory; better hardness, water and chemical resistance than NC, still easy to spray, limited shelf life once opened. The sensible general-purpose shop finish for cabinet interiors and non-wet joinery.

**Acid-catalysed (post-cat / AC) lacquer.** Two-pack, catalyst added at the bench, excellent hardness and chemical resistance, very fast dry. Releases formaldehyde while curing — needs serious extraction and PPE, and is increasingly restricted.

**2K polyurethane.** The specification default for kitchen and scullery joinery. High build, excellent chemical, water and abrasion resistance, and it crosslinks so it does not soften with heat. Typical schedule: 2K PU primer/sealer at 50–60 µm dry, denib at P320, then one or two topcoats at 50–60 µm dry each. Total dry film 100–180 µm. Respect pot life — mixed material that has gelled will still spray but will not cure properly.

**Water-based (waterborne PU / acrylic).** Very low odour and VOC, non-yellowing (a real advantage on white and pale finishes), good hardness in 2K form. More sensitive to substrate temperature and to grain raise; needs a warmer, better-controlled booth. **In a dry climate the fast evaporation is a benefit rather than the humidity problem it is in coastal shops.**

**Oils and hardwax oils.** Penetrating, repairable, matt, tactile, no film to chip. Osmo Polyx-Oil, Rubio Monocoat, Fiddes and (locally) the **Woodoc** polywax sealer range are the common products. They give the lowest possible film build and therefore the lowest protection against water and heat — excellent on furniture and dining tables, marginal on a kitchen worktop near a sink unless maintained.

**Waxes.** Not a finish on their own for anything that gets handled or wet; a maintenance and sheen-adjusting layer over another finish.

**Stains.** Dye stains (soluble, transparent, fade-prone), pigment stains (opaque particles, lodge in pores, more lightfast) and NGR (non-grain-raising) stains. On blotch-prone timber — pine, saligna and some meranti — apply a wash-coat sealer (10–15 % solids) before staining, or use a gel stain. Always make a sample board on the actual timber and sign it off with the client; wood colour cannot be judged from a chart.

## 3. Application: spray vs brush

**Spray** is the only realistic method for a joinery package. Equipment options:

| Method | Transfer efficiency | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Conventional air spray | low (~30 %) | fine atomisation, high overspray |
| **HVLP** | high (~65 %) | the shop standard for lacquer and 2K PU |
| Airless | high | high build, fast; poor for fine furniture |
| Air-assisted airless | high | production doors and panels |
| Electrostatic | very high | production only |

**Booth requirements:** downdraught or cross-draught, filtered extract, explosion-proof fittings for solvent, make-up air, and **positive pressure** so dust is not drawn in. Filter the air, not just the exhaust; a dry, dusty climate makes airborne dust the leading cause of rejected panels.

**Brush and roller** are for site touch-up, for oils, and for paints on architrave and skirting. A microfibre roller plus a tipping-off brush gives a very acceptable finish on painted MDF.

**Film build discipline.** Measure it. Use a wet-film comb on a test panel and calculate: `dry film ≈ wet film × % solids by volume`. Building 120 µm of 2K PU in one coat causes solvent entrapment, sagging and a film that stays soft for weeks; three coats of 40–60 µm cure properly.

## 4. Curing

- 2K PU: handleable in ~1 h, stackable in ~2 h, **fully cured in 2–3 days**. Do not wrap, install or place anything on a "dry" panel for at least 48 h.
- NC/pre-cat: touch-dry in minutes, but full solvent release takes days. Wrapping too early prints the wrapping into the film.
- Hardwax oil: 8–10 h between coats; **7–14 days to full cure**, during which the surface should not be washed or covered.
- Water-based: fast dry, slow cure; full hardness typically at 7 days.
- Stack panels on clean, padded battens with air movement between them — never face-to-face, never in the sun.

## 5. Hot-climate application notes **[NA]**

1. **Substrate temperature, not air temperature, controls the film.** A panel that has been in a 35 °C shop or on a sunny truck will flash the solvent instantly and give dry spray and orange peel. Bring panels to 20–25 °C before spraying, and spray in the early morning.
2. **Use a retarder / slow thinner.** In 30 °C+ conditions substitute 10–25 % of the normal thinner with a slow-evaporating retarder so the film has time to level. This is the single most effective hot-climate intervention.
3. **Watch pot life.** An 8-hour pot life at 20 °C may be 3–4 hours at 35 °C. Mix small batches.
4. **Low humidity accelerates everything.** The blushing that plagues humid climates is not a problem here; dry spray, poor levelling and premature glue skinning are.
5. **PVA open times collapse.** A 10-minute open time at 20 °C can be under 4 minutes at 35 °C with air movement. Pre-fit dry, have every clamp laid out and set, and glue-up in the coolest part of the day. For large glue-ups, use a slow-set or "extended open time" PVA, or urea formaldehyde/PUR.
6. **Never glue or finish in direct sun or wind.** Both cause skinning before assembly and dust inclusion.
7. **Store adhesives and coatings below 30 °C.** PVA that has been frozen or cooked is dead; 2K hardeners react with atmospheric moisture even in a "closed" tin.
8. **Allow for post-installation movement.** A finished solid-timber component installed in September (17 % RH) will swell in February; see `01_wood-science.md`.

## 6. Adhesives

| Adhesive | Class / type | Open time (20 °C) | Clamp time | Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PVA D2 | interior, occasional damp | 8–12 min | 30–60 min | interior carcase, non-wet |
| PVA D3 (cross-linked) | water-resistant | 5–10 min | 45–90 min | **the joinery default**; kitchens, doors |
| PVA D4 | waterproof (with hardener or 1K formulation) | 5–10 min | 60–120 min | wet areas, external joinery |
| Polyurethane (PU) | gap-filling, waterproof, foaming | 20–30 min | 60–120 min | mixed materials, oily timbers, wet areas |
| Epoxy | structural, gap-filling, waterproof | 20 min – 2 h | 4–24 h | oily/dense hardwoods (blackwood, Zambezi teak), repairs, bonding to metal |
| Urea formaldehyde (UF) | rigid, water-resistant | 15–30 min | 4–8 h | veneering, bent lamination (no creep) |
| Contact adhesive | instant grab, no clamping | 10–20 min flash-off | none | HPL to substrate, edging by hand |
| Hot-melt EVA | thermoplastic | 30–45 s | instant | edgebanding, general |
| Hot-melt PUR | reactive, waterproof | 30–45 s | instant + cure | edgebanding for kitchens/wet areas |

**Clamping pressure** (typical practice): softwood ~0.7–1.0 N/mm²; medium hardwood ~1.0–1.4 N/mm²; dense hardwood up to ~1.7–2.0 N/mm². Too little pressure starves the joint; too much squeezes the glue out and starves it just as effectively. Pressure must be **even** — this is what cauls are for.

**Glue-line rules.** Both surfaces freshly machined (glue a surface that has sat for a week and you are gluing oxidised, dusty wood). Both surfaces coated for PVA and epoxy; one surface only for PU (which needs moisture from the other). Wood at 6–12 % MC; PVA does not cure properly below ~8 °C or above ~35 °C. Squeeze-out should be a continuous fine bead — if there is none, the joint is starved; if it runs, there is too much.

**Oily and dense timbers** (African blackwood, Zambezi teak, some kiaat) resist PVA. Wipe with acetone immediately before gluing, or use epoxy or PU.

**Bonding HPL:** contact adhesive, both faces, flashed off until touch-dry (finger does not transfer adhesive), positioned with dowels or slip sheets because there is no second chance, then rolled or pressed over the entire area. **Always balance with a backing laminate.**

**Edgebanding:** EVA for cabinet interiors and general work; **PUR for kitchen, scullery, laundry and bathroom**, where its 120 °C+ heat resistance and waterproof bond justify the extra cost and the machine flushing regime. PUR requires a moisture-protected glue tank and the system must be purged before shutdown.

## Open questions

- Clamping-pressure figures are conventional practice (originally from FPL guidance) and were not confirmed against a primary source in this pass.
- Open and clamp times in the adhesive table are typical manufacturer values, not a single sourced dataset — always use the specific product's TDS.
- The Adkwik summary of EN 204 renders the shear-strength requirements ambiguously ("less than 10 N/mm²"); the values given here are stated as *minimum* requirements, which is the correct sense of EN 204, but should be confirmed against the standard itself.
- No local (SA/Namibian) 2K PU or waterborne system was verified; Woodoc is the only confirmed local product here. Obtain TDS for the specific system used (e.g. Plascon Woodcare, Duram, Chemical Initiatives) before writing a finishing specification.
