# Masters, schools and learning routes in woodwork

**Summary.** There are four distinct traditions worth studying, and they teach different things. The **Anglo-American studio tradition** (Krenov, Maloof, Nakashima, Frid, Charlesworth) teaches design judgement, hand skill and the relationship between maker and material. The **German/Central European trade system** teaches production discipline, machine competence and business capability, formalised in the Geselle → Meister progression. The **Japanese lineages** teach precision, tool preparation and joinery as structure. The **southern African trade routes** — NTA/VTC and COSDEC in Namibia, FP&M SETA and Furntech in South Africa — are the practical local qualification paths. A serious joiner borrows from all four.

## Key facts

- **The Krenov School** (Fort Bragg, California) was founded in **1981** by James Krenov as the College of the Redwoods Fine Woodworking Program; it moved to **Mendocino College in 2016** and took Krenov's name. It runs a **nine-month intensive** programme plus summer workshops, taught by five furniture makers who all studied under Krenov. Krenov himself trained at the **Malmsten School in Sweden** and taught until retiring in **2002**.
- **Japanese carpentry** divides into four professions: **miyadaiku** (shrines and temples), **sukiya-daiku** (teahouses and residences), **sashimono-shi** (furniture makers) and **tateguya** (interior fittings — shōji and ranma).
- **Fine Woodworking** was founded in **1975** by Paul and Jan Roman (Newtown, Connecticut), published by Taunton Press and now by Active Interest Media.
- **[ZA]** Furntech offers the **National Certificate: Furniture Making (Wood)** at **NQF 2 (SAQA 49091)** and **NQF 3 (SAQA 49105)**, plus a Wooden Windows and Doors programme at NQF 3 and an eight-week Supervisor Development Programme.
- **[NA]** Joiner & cabinetmaker is a designated trade; entry is Grade 10 minimum, age 16+, through apprenticeship, cooperative or vocational-trainee routes at the Windhoek, Valombola, Eenhana, Okakarara and Zambezi VTCs, NIMT (Arandis, Keetmanshoop) or COSDEC centres.

## 1. The studio tradition

**James Krenov (1920–2009).** Swedish-trained, Malmsten School. His contribution was a *sensibility*: the cabinet on a stand, the hand-planed surface left unsanded, the wooden bodied plane made by the maker, the insistence that the maker listen to the individual board rather than impose a design on it. Four books — *A Cabinetmaker's Notebook*, *The Fine Art of Cabinetmaking*, *The Impractical Cabinetmaker*, *With Wakened Hands* — remain the core texts of the school. Read him for judgement and for the discipline of fitting; do not read him for production method.

**Sam Maloof.** American, self-taught, best known for the sculpted rocking chair and the shaped joint where arm, leg and seat flow into one another. His contribution is *sculptural joinery*: a lapped, screwed-and-plugged joint that is shaped after assembly, which liberated furniture from the frame-and-panel grid. Study him for how to shape and blend, and for how a small shop makes a living from a repeated design.

**George Nakashima.** Japanese-American, architect by training, who built a practice around slab tops with free edges, butterfly keys across checks, and a deep respect for the individual tree. His book *The Soul of a Tree* is the reference. His practical contribution to a working joiner is the vocabulary of stabilising a defect rather than cutting it out.

**Tage Frid.** Danish-trained cabinetmaker who taught at RIT and then RISD and wrote the three-volume *Tage Frid Teaches Woodworking* (Joinery; Shaping, Veneering, Finishing; Furniture Making). Frid is the bridge between European trade training and American studio practice, and his books remain the clearest systematic teaching of machine-based joinery in English.

**Frank Klausz.** Hungarian trade-trained, later in the USA; the reference on fast, accurate hand dovetails and on the European trade approach to bench work.

**David Charlesworth.** English; obsessive, systematic teaching of hand-tool accuracy — the ruler trick for plane-iron backs, shooting boards, and edge-jointing to a controlled hollow. His *Furniture-Making Techniques* volumes and DVDs are the most rigorous hand-skill material available.

**Paul Sellers.** English; a hand-tool-first pedagogy aimed at people without a workshop or machines, delivered mostly online (Woodworking Masterclasses, YouTube). Excellent for fundamentals: sharpening, the poor-man's workbench, hand-cut joints. He is deliberately anti-machine, which is a pedagogical stance rather than a professional one.

**Christian Becksvoort, Robert Wearing, Ernest Joyce.** Becksvoort for Shaker discipline and for wood movement in practice (*With the Grain*); Wearing's *The Essential Woodworker* for the logic of bench work; Joyce's *The Encyclopedia of Furniture Making* as the single most complete reference on construction.

## 2. The British workshop tradition

**The Barnsley Workshop** (Edward Barnsley Educational Trust, Froxfield, Hampshire) continues the Cotswold Arts-and-Crafts line from Ernest Gimson and the brothers Sidney and Ernest Barnsley: honest construction, exposed joinery, English hardwoods, and a genuine apprenticeship model in a working commercial shop. It is the closest living example of learning by production rather than by exercise.

**Rowden Atelier** (Devon), founded by David Savage, teaches design-led fine furniture with long courses and an emphasis on hand-tool accuracy and on making a living from the craft. Between them the two represent the two British options: apprenticeship inside a workshop's own tradition, or an atelier that trains you to start your own.

## 3. The German Tischler / Meister system

The most complete formal training system in the trade. The path runs:

1. **Ausbildung** — a dual-system apprenticeship (workshop plus vocational school), typically three years, ending in the **Gesellenprüfung** (journeyman examination).
2. **Geselle** — working journeyman. The optional **Walz** (journeyman's travelling years) still exists.
3. **Meisterprüfung** — the master examination, examining practical craft, technical theory, business/commercial competence and vocational pedagogy. Its practical centrepiece is the **Meisterstück**, a self-designed and self-made piece.
4. **Meister** — the certificate is a legal precondition for opening a joinery business, because Tischler is a *licensed* craft. The governing instruments are the Meisterprüfungsverfahrensverordnung (MPVerfV) and the Tischlermeisterverordnung (TischlMstrV).

What is worth importing from this system even outside Germany: the explicit separation of *craft skill*, *technical theory*, *business* and *teaching* as four examined competences, and the idea that a master must be able to train others.

## 4. Japanese traditions

- **Miyadaiku** — temple and shrine carpenters. Enormous timber joinery (tsugite lengthening joints, shiguchi angle joints), no nails, and structures that have stood for over a millennium. Their lineage is the deepest continuous joinery tradition in existence and their apprenticeships are correspondingly long.
- **Sukiya-daiku** — teahouse and residential carpenters, working with natural, irregular materials and scribed joints. This is where **scribing** as a discipline reaches its highest form; anyone fitting joinery to a rough wall is doing a crude version of the same operation.
- **Sashimono-shi** — furniture makers, working with joints alone (Edo sashimono is a recognised traditional craft of Tokyo). Very thin sections, no glue-dependence, joinery cut so accurately it is invisible.
- **Tateguya** — interior fitting makers, producing shōji and ranma, and the home of **kumiko**: lattice patterns built from very small, precisely cut pieces held only by friction and geometry. Kumiko is the best available exercise for developing absolute accuracy in cutting and paring.
- **Tool culture.** Japanese saws cut on the pull stroke (thinner kerf, less effort), chisels and plane irons are laminated hard steel on soft iron, and plane setup is a maintenance craft in itself. Adopting Japanese saws and a Japanese pull-stroke dozuki is the cheapest single accuracy upgrade available to a hand-tool joiner.

## 5. Southern African routes

**[NA] Namibia.** Joiner & cabinetmaker is a designated trade under the Namibia Training Authority. Entry requires Grade 10 or equivalent (age 16+), typically with mathematics, physical science and English. Three routes: apprenticeship with an employer, a cooperative route, or a vocational-trainee route through a VTC. Delivery institutions include the Windhoek, Valombola, Eenhana, Okakarara and Zambezi Vocational Training Centres, NIMT (Arandis and Keetmanshoop, four-year training), and COSDEC community skills development centres (Swakopmund among them, which advertises TVET trainer posts in joinery and cabinetmaking). Practical reality: the shallow local pool of trained joiners is the main constraint on a fitted-furniture package in Namibia, and a project of any size should budget for in-house upskilling.

**[ZA] South Africa.** The Fibre Processing and Manufacturing SETA (FP&M SETA) is the sector authority. **Furntech** is the principal specialist provider, delivering the National Certificate: Furniture Making (Wood) at NQF 2 (49091) and NQF 3 (49105), plus targeted programmes in wooden windows and doors, coffin making (20–25 days) and supervisor development (eight weeks). Trade-test routes to qualified artisan status run through accredited trade test centres.

**The Cape furniture tradition.** The Cape Dutch furniture of the 18th and 19th centuries — armoires, kists, rusbanke and jonkmanskaste in **stinkwood** (*Ocotea bullata*) with **yellowwood** (*Podocarpus/Afrocarpus*) panels and drawer linings — is the region's own high-craft lineage, and the Stellenbosch and Cape Winelands workshops that restore and reproduce it are the best local place to see traditional solid-wood construction executed properly. Both timbers are now protected and scarce; the *construction* is what is worth studying.

## 6. Books worth owning

**Reference:** R. Bruce Hoadley, *Understanding Wood* (the wood-science reference for makers) and *Identifying Wood*. Ernest Joyce, *The Encyclopedia of Furniture Making*. USDA FPL, *Wood Handbook* (free PDF).
**Technique:** Tage Frid, *Tage Frid Teaches Woodworking* (3 vols). Robert Wearing, *The Essential Woodworker*. David Charlesworth, *Furniture-Making Techniques*. Christian Becksvoort, *With the Grain*.
**Sensibility:** James Krenov, *A Cabinetmaker's Notebook*. George Nakashima, *The Soul of a Tree*.
**Fitted work:** manufacturer technical catalogues are the real textbooks here — Blum's technical manual, Hettich's system handbooks, and the PG Bison product guides carry more usable detail than any general book on kitchen making.

## 7. Magazines and online sources worth trusting

- **Fine Woodworking** (Taunton, now Active Interest Media, founded 1975) — the deepest archive in English; the online archive is worth the subscription on its own.
- **Furniture & Cabinetmaking** (GMC Publications, UK) — project- and technique-led, still publishing.
- **Popular Woodworking** and **Woodwork** — lighter, but with useful historical technique content.
- **Lost Art Press** — books and a blog specialising in pre-industrial technique and workbench design; unusually rigorous about sources.
- **YouTube, worth trusting:** Paul Sellers (hand tools, fundamentals), Matthias Wandel / Woodgears (measurement and testing — his joint-strength tests are cited in `04_joints-and-techniques.md`), Rob Cosman (dovetails and sharpening), Blacktail Studio and Foureyes Furniture (contemporary shop practice), Peter Millard / 10 Minute Workshop (British fitted-furniture practice — the closest online match to a kitchen and wardrobe package), and the Festool and Blum official channels for system-specific method.
- **Treat with caution:** most "kitchen build" content skips the drilling patterns and load ratings that actually matter, and social-media joinery clips routinely show unsafe spindle-moulder and table-saw practice.
