# Wood science for joiners — moisture, movement, density and durability

**Summary.** Almost every joinery failure that is not a hardware failure is a moisture failure. Wood exchanges water with the air until it reaches equilibrium; as it does, it changes dimension across the grain by a predictable amount and along the grain by almost nothing. Interior southern Africa is dry — Windhoek's annual mean relative humidity is about 34 %, ranging from roughly 17 % in September to 56 % in February — which puts service equilibrium moisture content in the **4–10 % MC** band, well below the 8–12 % that European and North American texts assume. Timber bought at coastal or imported moisture contents will shrink after installation unless it is conditioned first.

## Key facts

- **Fibre saturation point (FSP):** ~30 % MC. Above FSP wood does not change dimension; all shrinkage and swelling happens below it.
- **EMC at ~25 °C** (FPL Wood Handbook Table 4-2): 20 % RH → 4.5 % MC; 30 % → 6.2 %; 40 % → 7.7 %; 50 % → 9.2 %; 60 % → 11.0 %; 65 % → 12.0 %; 70 % → 13.1 %; 80 % → 16.0 %.
- **Windhoek RH:** annual mean ~34 %; minimum month ~17 % (September); maximum month ~56 % (February).
- **Rule of thumb:** flat-sawn material changes about **1 % in width for every 4 % change in MC**. Quarter-sawn moves about half as much.
- **Target MC for interior joinery, hot dry inland southern Africa: 6–8 %.** **[NA]** For Windhoek and the interior highland, specify **7 % ± 2 %** at delivery and re-check on site before machining.

## 1. Anatomy and why it matters at the bench

Wood is a bundle of hollow cells aligned with the trunk axis, bound in lignin, with rays running radially. Three consequences dominate joinery:

- **Longitudinal movement is negligible** (typically 0.1–0.2 % green to oven-dry). Rails, stiles and mullions do not change length.
- **Tangential movement is roughly 1.5–2× radial movement.** The T/R ratio is the single most useful stability number for a species: 1.6 for kiaat (*Pterocarpus angolensis*), 1.7 for *Eucalyptus grandis*, 1.9 for *Pinus patula* and white oak, 2.1 for imbuia.
- **Cut determines behaviour.** A flat-sawn (tangential-face) board cups away from the heart as it dries. A quarter-sawn board stays flat but changes thickness more. Rift-sawn splits the difference. Specify quarter-sawn for wide table tops, door panels over 300 mm and anything that must stay flat.

**Earlywood/latewood** contrast in fast-grown plantation pine causes uneven planing and uneven stain uptake. **Interlocked grain** in kiaat, saligna and meranti causes tear-out when planing against the rise; reduce cut depth, increase cutter sharpness, or scrape/sand instead.

**Reaction wood** — compression wood in softwoods (dark, dense bands, often at a lean) and tension wood in hardwoods (woolly surface) — moves longitudinally far more than normal wood and will bow a component after machining. Reject it in structural or long components.

## 2. Moisture content and equilibrium moisture content

MC is expressed on a dry basis: `MC% = (wet mass − oven-dry mass) / oven-dry mass × 100`.

EMC is the MC that wood will drift to at a given temperature and RH. Because it depends far more on RH than on temperature, the EMC table can be used directly for southern African interiors.

| Ambient RH | EMC (~25 °C) | Where this occurs in the region |
|---|---|---|
| 20 % | 4.5 % | Windhoek, Keetmanshoop, Upington, Aug–Oct afternoons |
| 30 % | 6.2 % | Interior highveld winter; typical air-conditioned interior |
| 40 % | 7.7 % | Windhoek annual mean-ish; Gauteng summer interior |
| 50 % | 9.2 % | Coastal interior, Swakopmund, Cape Town winter |
| 65 % | 12.0 % | Coastal exterior; the value most northern-hemisphere texts assume |
| 80 % | 16.0 % | Walvis Bay fog belt, Durban summer, unventilated bathrooms |

> ⚠️ Timber and boards trucked from Cape Town or Durban to Windhoek arrive at coastal EMC. A 600 mm wide saligna panel at 12 % MC installed into a 6 % MC interior will lose roughly 600 × (12−6)/4 × 0.01 ≈ **9 mm** of width. Stack and sticker for a minimum of 10–14 days in the space, or in a similarly conditioned store, before machining.

**Measurement.** Use a pin-type resistance meter with species correction, or a pinless capacitance meter calibrated for the specific gravity of the species. Measure at 20–25 mm depth on thick stock (moisture gradients are steep after kilning), and take the average of readings on several boards, not one.

## 3. Shrinkage and movement by species and cut

Green-to-oven-dry shrinkage, from the FPL Wood Handbook and the Wood Database:

| Species | Radial % | Tangential % | Volumetric % | T/R |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Pinus patula* (SA pine) | 4.1 | 7.9 | 12.6 | 1.9 |
| *Eucalyptus grandis* (saligna/rose gum) | 5.9 | 10.1 | 15.5 | 1.7 |
| *Pterocarpus angolensis* (kiaat/mukwa) | 1.7 | 2.8 | 5.4 | 1.6 |
| *Baikiaea plurijuga* (Zambezi/Rhodesian teak) | 2.6 | 4.5 | 6.9 | 1.7 |
| *Shorea* spp. (dark red meranti) | 3.9 | 7.8 | 12.5 | 2.0 |
| *Quercus alba* (white oak) | 5.6 | 10.5 | 16.3 | 1.9 |
| *Ocotea porosa* (imbuia) | 3.0 | 6.4 | 9.5 | 2.1 |
| *Dalbergia melanoxylon* (African blackwood) | 2.9 | 4.8 | 7.7 | 1.7 |

**Converting to in-service movement.** Shrinkage from green to a final MC *x* is `Sx = S0 × (30 − x)/30` where `S0` is the green-to-oven-dry value. For movement between two service moisture contents, take the difference of the two `Sx` values.

*Worked example, kiaat wardrobe door panel, 500 mm wide, flat-sawn, Windhoek.* Service range 5–9 % MC. Tangential `S0` = 2.8 %. `S(5%) = 2.8 × 25/30 = 2.33 %`; `S(9%) = 2.8 × 21/30 = 1.96 %`. Difference = 0.37 % of the green width, i.e. ≈ **1.9 mm** on a 500 mm panel. Allow 2 mm total in the groove depth per edge and never glue the panel in.

*Same panel in saligna:* tangential `S0` = 10.1 %; difference over the same MC range = 1.35 %, ≈ **6.7 mm**. Saligna is roughly 3.5× less stable than kiaat and must be detailed accordingly.

**Rule-of-thumb shortcut** for site work: flat-sawn stock changes ~1 % of its width per 4 % MC change; quarter-sawn ~0.5 %. A 300 mm flat-sawn board across a 6 % MC swing moves ~4.5 mm.

## 4. Seasoning and kilning

**Air drying** in the Namibian interior is fast and aggressive. Stack on level bearers 400–450 mm clear of the ground, sticker every 400–600 mm with dry stickers of uniform 20–25 mm thickness aligned vertically over the bearers, weight or strap the top, and **shade the stack** — direct sun and 15 % RH will case-harden and check the outer boards within days. End-seal every board with wax emulsion or PVA within hours of conversion. Air drying alone will reach roughly 10–14 % MC in the interior; it will not reach furniture MC.

**Kiln drying** finishes the job. Typical hardwood schedules run a wet-bulb depression that starts small and widens as MC falls, finishing with an **equalising** stage (bringing all boards to within ~1 % MC of each other) and a **conditioning** stage (a short high-humidity steam period that relieves case-hardening and drying stresses). Ask the supplier for the schedule and for a case-hardening (prong) test result. Un-conditioned kiln stock will cup and pinch the saw as soon as it is resawn.

**Defects arising from drying:** surface checking (too fast an initial schedule), honeycomb (internal checking from case-hardening), collapse (common in eucalypts, sometimes recoverable by steaming), warp in its four forms — bow, crook (spring), cup, twist — and end splits. Twist is the least recoverable; reject it.

**Other defects to grade for:** knots (sound, loose, dead), knot clusters, wane, bark pockets, resin pockets (severe in plantation pine — they will bleed through lacquer in a hot car-port or a north-facing scullery), blue stain (a sapstain fungus, cosmetic but a sign of slow drying), pinhole borer, powder-post beetle in sapwood (very common in kiaat and Zambezi teak sapwood), and brittleheart in fast-grown tropical hardwood.

## 5. Density, hardness and working properties

Density at 12 % MC drives nearly everything else — hardness, machining behaviour, screw-holding, glue-line pressure and tool wear.

| Species | Density kg/m³ @12 % | Janka | Working note |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Pinus patula* | 575 | 2 450 N | Soft, resinous; blunt tools tear earlywood |
| *Eucalyptus grandis* | 640 | 5 600 N | Machines well, dries badly, moves a lot |
| *Pterocarpus angolensis* | 605 | 6 050 N | Excellent all-round furniture timber |
| *Ocotea porosa* (imbuia) | 660 | 4 300 N | Fine, spicy scent, can irritate |
| *Shorea* spp. (dark red meranti) | 675 | ~3 560 N | Interlocked grain, tears easily |
| *Quercus alba* | 755 | 5 990 N | Coarse ring-porous, tannin stains with steel |
| *Baikiaea plurijuga* | 890 | 13 300 N | Silica blunts cutters severely |
| *Dalbergia melanoxylon* | 1 270 | 16 320 N | Worked more like metal than wood |

**Screw-holding and joint pressure** scale with density: use pilot holes in everything over ~700 kg/m³, and raise clamping pressure for dense species (see `08_finishes-and-adhesives.md`).

## 6. Durability and its limits

Natural durability of *heartwood* is normally expressed on a five-class scale (very durable → perishable). Sapwood of essentially every species is non-durable and borer-susceptible; it is a grading defect, not a durability question.

- **Very durable:** *Baikiaea plurijuga*, *Dalbergia melanoxylon*.
- **Durable / moderately durable:** *Pterocarpus angolensis*, *Ocotea porosa*, *Quercus alba* (very durable in cooperage service).
- **Moderately durable to non-durable:** *Shorea* spp.
- **Non-durable to perishable:** *Pinus patula*. Untreated SA pine outdoors in Namibia will be borer-infested within a season; specify CCA or equivalent preservative treatment for anything not fully protected.
- *Eucalyptus grandis* is rated durable to very durable **above ground, interior** but has poor termite resistance and is unsuitable in ground contact untreated.

For interior fitted furniture, durability class is largely irrelevant — but **insect susceptibility is not**. Kiaat and Zambezi teak boards with included sapwood will bring powder-post beetle into a house. Specify "heartwood only, no sapwood" in the timber schedule.

## 7. Movement allowance rules for solid-wood construction

1. **Never glue a panel into a frame.** Groove depth minimum 8 mm for panels up to 400 mm wide, 10–12 mm over that; leave the calculated seasonal movement plus 1 mm as free play at each edge. Use space balls or a bead of silicone to centre the panel.
2. **Frames move only in width.** Stiles and rails are dimensionally stable along their length; all frame movement is in the width of the stiles and rails themselves — usually under 1 mm and ignorable.
3. **Cross-grain construction is the enemy.** Any joint that glues long grain across end/side grain over more than ~75 mm will split. Breadboard ends: glue the central 50–75 mm only, elongate the outer peg holes, draw-bore with a loose fit.
4. **Fix wide tops with movement.** Buttons, figure-8 fasteners, or slotted screw holes running across the grain. Never screw a solid top rigidly to a carcass at more than one point across its width.
5. **Solid doors in melamine carcasses.** Size the door for the *dry* extreme and let the reveal close up in the wet season. In Windhoek, a 600 mm wide flat-sawn solid door needs 3 mm reveals minimum; quarter-sawn or veneered board can run at 2 mm.
6. **Match the finish on both faces.** An unfinished back on a finished panel is the classic recipe for cupping; the exchange rate differs and the panel dishes toward the sealed face.
7. **Condition before machining, not after.** Acclimatise stock in the finished, ventilated space for 10–14 days. Cut oversize, sticker overnight, then machine to final size.
