# Material selection principles and decision framework

**Summary.** A material is selected against nine performance criteria simultaneously, and it must remain compatible with everything it touches for the life of the building. This file sets out those criteria, the compatibility rules that most often get broken in southern African practice (galvanic pairing on roofs, chemical attack on cementitious systems, movement mismatch at joints), a life-cycle way of thinking about cost, and a step-by-step framework an agent can execute to produce a defensible material choice.

## Key facts

| Criterion | Ask | Typical governing document / metric |
|---|---|---|
| Structural | Can it carry the load, for the span, with the required stiffness? | Characteristic strength, grade designation, span tables |
| Thermal | What is its R-value or thermal mass contribution? | R (m²K/W), U (W/m²K), thermal capacity (J/m²·°C) |
| Moisture | Does it get wet, stay wet, or transmit water? | Water absorption, vapour permeability, DPC/DPM class |
| Durability | What is the exposure category and expected service life? | C1–C5M atmospheric corrosivity; clay brick Zones 1–4; H2–H6 timber |
| Fire | Reaction to fire and resistance rating | SANS 53501-1 Euroclass; wall fire ratings in minutes |
| Buildability | Can the available trades install it correctly, with the available tools and water? | Trade skill, plant, tolerance |
| Maintenance | What does it need, how often, and can it be done here? | Recoat interval, access, spare availability |
| Embodied carbon | What did it cost to make and move? | kgCO₂e/kg or /m²; transport distance |
| Cost | Installed cost, not material cost | Rate build-up including waste, labour, freight |

## 1. The nine performance criteria in practice

**Structural.** Specify by grade and standard, not by common name. "Y12 rebar" means SANS 920 Grade 450 deformed bar of 12 mm nominal diameter, not "12 mm steel". "S5 pine" means SANS 1783-2 grade S5. Getting the designation right is what makes a quote comparable and a substitution auditable.

**Thermal.** Two distinct mechanisms compete and are routinely confused. *Resistance* (R-value) slows steady heat flow — this is bulk insulation. *Capacity* (thermal mass) delays and flattens the daily cycle — this is masonry and concrete. In a hot semi-arid climate with a large diurnal swing you want both: mass in the envelope walls, resistance in the roof, and a reflective barrier to intercept radiant gain before it reaches either. Reflective foil only works with an air gap; a foil pressed against the underside of a sheet is worth almost nothing.

**Moisture.** In a dry climate the moisture cases are episodic and severe rather than continuous: intense summer downpours, rising damp from a high seasonal water table, and construction moisture that cannot escape. Specify DPC and DPM properly, ventilate roof spaces, and remember that clay masonry undergoes **irreversible moisture expansion** — generally not more than 0.20 % — which must be accommodated by movement joints.

**Durability.** Durability is an *exposure* question before it is a material question. Establish the category first:

| Exposure | Inland Namibia (e.g. Ohangwena) | Coastal Namibia (Walvis Bay, Swakopmund) |
|---|---|---|
| Atmospheric corrosivity | **C1 desert** — mild steel < 5 µm/yr, zinc < 0.5 µm/yr | **C5 / C5M marine** — mild steel 200–300+ µm/yr, zinc 15–30+ µm/yr |
| Clay masonry exposure zone | Zone 1 "Protected" (>30 km from coast) | Zone 3–4 "Severe"/"Very Severe" |
| Sheet steel coating | Galvanised Z275 or AZ150 both viable | AZ150 minimum, AZ200 preferred |
| Fasteners | Class 3 adequate | Class 4 / stainless with compatibility care |
| Practical consequence | Do not over-specify | Do not under-specify |

**Fire.** For domestic and small commercial work in southern Africa, the fire performance of ordinary masonry is usually more than adequate — a 222 mm plastered clay brick wall is quoted by the industry at indicative 120 minutes structural / 240 minutes non-structural, and even a 106 mm brick wall at 30/60 minutes. The fire risks come from what is added: combustible insulation, cavity linings, and thatch. Where insulation is used in or near a roof, check the Euroclass reaction-to-fire rating (e.g. a foil radiant barrier rated B-s1,d1 or A2-s1,d0) rather than accepting a bare "fire retardant" claim.

**Buildability.** Ask three questions before specifying: does this need water we do not have; does it need a skill the local labour pool lacks; and does it need a tool or plant that is not on site. A high-performance product installed badly performs worse than a modest product installed well. This is the argument for pierce-fixed IBR over standing seam on a remote site, for pre-mixed adhesives over site-gauged mortars where consistency matters, and for standard sizes over cut-to-fit.

**Maintenance.** On a remote site, the maintenance question is really an access-and-supply question. A coating that needs recoating every five years is only sensible if someone will actually be there with the coating. Prefer materials whose failure mode is slow and visible over ones that fail suddenly and invisibly.

**Embodied carbon.** Ranked roughly by embodied carbon intensity: cement and steel are the dominant contributors in a typical low-rise building; clay brick is significant because of firing; timber, earth blocks and locally won aggregate are low. The three highest-leverage moves are (a) replace part of the Portland cement with fly ash or slag, (b) reduce structural steel and cement quantity through better design rather than substituting materials, and (c) source aggregate and blockwork locally, because transport is a large fraction of the footprint of heavy, low-value materials.

**Cost.** Compare *installed* cost. A rate build-up must include material, waste allowance, labour, plant, freight to site, offloading, storage/protection, and the cost of any consequential works (thicker slab, extra purlins, different fixings). Material price alone routinely misleads by 30–50 %.

## 2. Compatibility and interaction

### Galvanic (bimetallic) corrosion

Two dissimilar metals in electrical contact with an electrolyte between them form a cell; the less noble metal corrodes preferentially. On a roof the electrolyte is condensation or rain, and it arrives every night. The rules that matter on a southern African metal roof:

| Upper / draining material | Safe to drain onto or contact | Do **not** pair with |
|---|---|---|
| Aluminium-zinc alloy coated steel (Zincalume / Zincal, AZ150/AZ200) | Aluminium, pre-painted steel, aluminium-zinc, lead | **Zinc-coated steel and zinc**, copper, copper alloys |
| Pre-painted metal | Aluminium, pre-painted, aluminium-zinc, lead | Zinc-coated steel and zinc, copper |
| Aluminium and aluminium alloys | Aluminium, pre-painted, aluminium-zinc, lead | Zinc-coated steel and zinc, copper |
| Copper and copper alloys | Aluminium alloys, pre-painted, lead | Aluminium, zinc-coated steel, aluminium-zinc |
| Zinc-coated steel and zinc | Aluminium, pre-painted, zinc, aluminium-zinc, lead | Copper |

Source: Safintra material compatibility table. Two supplementary rules from the same source:

- Fasteners containing **stainless steel, lead, copper or copper alloys (e.g. Monel) must not be used with aluminium-zinc coated or pre-painted steel.**
- Washers containing conductive **carbon-black fillers must not be used** with aluminium-zinc coated or pre-painted aluminium-zinc products. Specify carbon-black-free washers.
- Where aluminium sheet is laid on galvanised purlins, an **isolation tape** is required at every contact point; direct contact will produce bimetallic corrosion and compromise the aluminium's service life.
- Copper is the classic silent killer: a copper geyser overflow, copper flashing or a copper earth strap discharging onto a Zincalume roof will perforate it. Never let copper drain onto zinc, aluminium or aluminium-zinc.

> ⚠️ Direct contact should be avoided even between pairs marked "safe to drain onto". A drainage relationship is not the same as a contact relationship.

### Chemical interactions in cementitious systems

- **Chloride-based accelerators promote corrosion of reinforcement** and must not be used in reinforced concrete, water-retaining structures, prestressed concrete or steam-cured concrete.
- **Masonry cement (SANS 50413) may not be used in concrete.** It is for bricklaying, block laying, rendering and plastering only. This is stated in the national foreword of the standard and is one of the most common site errors.
- **Do not use lime with masonry cement.** With common cement, lime is permitted up to 10 kg per 50 kg bag for Class I mortar and 25 kg per 50 kg for Class II.
- **Alkali-aggregate reaction:** if the aggregate is potentially reactive, the binder must contain not less than 40 % GGBS, or 20 % fly ash, or 15 % silica fume.
- **Sulfate attack:** rare in South Africa but a real risk in some Namibian soils and borehole waters. CEM I cements with C₃A below about 9–10 % give markedly higher resistance; sulfate-resisting cements are separately designated (CEM I-SR 0/3/5, CEM III/B-SR, CEM IV/A-SR etc.).
- **Gypsum plaster against cement or embedded steel** is an avoidable trouble spot in wet or humid detail: keep gypsum out of external and wet-area applications.
- **Fresh, alkaline concrete attacks aluminium.** Aluminium windows, frames and conduit cast into or bedded against concrete or mortar must be isolated with a bituminous or lacquer coat.

### Movement compatibility

Every interface between materials with different thermal expansion coefficients is a joint waiting to be designed. Note that **aluminium has an expansion coefficient roughly twice that of conventional steel** — a fact that governs long aluminium roof sheets, curtain-wall spandrels and coping. Where movement is expected, the sealant must be selected on movement capability (see `07_insulation-membranes-and-sealants.md`), the joint must be correctly proportioned, and a bond breaker must be used so the sealant works in two-sided adhesion.

### Moisture and vapour compatibility

Build the assembly so that it can dry. A vapour-tight layer on the wrong side of insulation, or two vapour-tight layers with a wettable material between them, produces interstitial condensation. In a dry inland climate the risk is lower but not zero — air-conditioned buildings create an inward vapour drive in summer that reverses the northern-hemisphere textbook rule.

## 3. Life-cycle thinking

Three horizons should be priced, even roughly:

1. **Capital** — installed cost as defined above.
2. **Operating** — the energy consequence of the envelope decision, and the maintenance cycle. On a remote site, add the cost of *getting someone there* to do the maintenance.
3. **End of life** — can it be reused, recycled, or must it be landfilled? Steel and aluminium have high recycled value; treated timber (CCA, creosote) is a disposal liability; composite panels are usually unrecoverable.

The heuristic that works: for a building expected to stand 40+ years in a remote location with weak maintenance capacity, **spend capital on the things that cannot be maintained** (foundations, structure, roof substrate and coating class, embedded services) and economise on the things that can (finishes, fittings, paint).

## 4. Decision framework an agent can apply

Execute in order. Stop and flag if a step cannot be completed with sourced information.

1. **State the element and its function.** ("External loadbearing wall, single storey, plastered both sides.")
2. **State the exposure and service conditions.** Distance from coast; ground contact or not; wet area or not; internal/external; expected service temperature range; termite pressure. Assign a corrosivity category (C1–C5M) and, for masonry, an exposure zone (1–4).
3. **List candidate materials that satisfy the structural requirement**, by grade and standard designation.
4. **Screen against the remaining eight criteria.** Eliminate anything that fails a hard requirement (fire rating, R-value target, ground contact durability).
5. **Check availability by supply tier** — local / regional / cross-border — using `09` and `10`. Record the tier for each surviving candidate.
6. **Run the compatibility check** against every adjacent material: galvanic, chemical, movement, moisture. Record each interface and its detail requirement.
7. **Build installed-cost rates** for the surviving candidates on the same basis, including freight and waste.
8. **Compare on life-cycle terms**, weighting maintainability heavily if the site is remote.
9. **Write the specification** as: performance clause + named reference product + "or approved equivalent, equivalence to be demonstrated against [the stated performance clause]". Never name a product without the performance clause; never write a performance clause without a reference product a merchant can price.
10. **Record the substitution boundary** — what a contractor may swap without asking, and what requires approval.

### Quick-reference red flags

| Symptom in a proposal | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| A material named without a grade or standard | The quote is not comparable and the substitution is uncontrolled |
| Coastal-grade coatings on an inland site | Money spent on a risk that does not exist |
| Inland-grade coatings on a coastal site | Premature failure, usually within 5 years |
| Copper or stainless fasteners on an AZ-coated roof | Galvanic failure at every fixing |
| Masonry cement in a concrete mix | Non-compliant, under-strength concrete |
| Chloride accelerator in reinforced concrete | Reinforcement corrosion |
| Foil insulation with no air gap | Near-zero thermal benefit |
| A long-lead imported item not on the procurement programme | A programme delay already baked in |
