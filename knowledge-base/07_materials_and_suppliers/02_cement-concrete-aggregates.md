# Cement, concrete, aggregates and concrete products

**Summary.** Cement in South Africa and Namibia is specified under SANS 50197-1, which is the SANS adoption of EN 197-1: a type notation (CEM I to CEM VI with letters for the extender), a strength class (32,5 / 42,5 / 52,5) and an early-strength suffix (L / N / R). Extenders — fly ash, ground granulated blast-furnace slag, silica fume, limestone — are either ground into the cement at the factory or blended on site under SANS 50450, SANS 55167 and SANS 53263. Aggregates come under SANS 1083. This file gives the type tables, the strength requirements, extender selection guidance, aggregate grading, admixture dosages, practical mix proportions, and the producer landscape including Ohorongo in Namibia.

## Key facts

- All cement sold in South Africa must comply with **SANS 50197** (common cement) or **SANS 50413** (masonry cement) and with NRCS compulsory specification **VC9085**. Bags must be marked with strength class, composition notation, and an NRCS **Letter of Authority (LoA)** number.
- **Masonry cement (SANS 50413) may not be used in concrete.** It is for bricklaying, block laying, rendering and plastering only.
- Extenders **must never be used without Portland cement**.
- Stone for general concrete should be **19 mm or 26 mm nominal size**.
- A builder's wheelbarrow used for volume batching is taken as **65 litres**.

## 1. Cement types under SANS 50197-1

The notation is `CEM <main type> <A|B|C>-<constituent letters> <strength class><L|N|R>`. `A`, `B`, `C` indicate increasing proportions of the non-clinker main constituent.

### Constituent letters

| Letter | Constituent |
|---|---|
| K | Portland cement clinker |
| S | Blast-furnace slag |
| D | Silica fume |
| P | Natural pozzolana |
| Q | Natural calcined pozzolana |
| V | Siliceous fly ash |
| W | Calcareous fly ash |
| T | Burnt shale |
| L | Limestone (total organic content ≤ 0.50 % by mass) |
| LL | Limestone (total organic content ≤ 0.20 % by mass) |

## 2. Extenders

Standards for site-blended extenders: **SANS 55167** (GGBS), **SANS 50450** (fly ash), **SANS 53263** (silica fume). These apply to site blending with a SANS 50197 cement — not to factory-ground blended cements.

| Property | GGBS | Fly ash (FA) | Silica fume (SF) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bleeding | — | — | Significant reduction |
| Setting time | Slight retardation | Slight retardation | Slight retardation |
| Cohesiveness | — | Ultra-fine FA increases | Increases |
| Workability | Slight improvement with some aggregates | Improves — lower water for given slump | Reduces — higher water for given slump |
| Early strength gain | Reduces, especially at low temperature | Slight reduction at low temperature | Marginal 1-day reduction |
| Strength gain after 28 d | Increases | Increases | Increases |
| Heat of hydration rate | Reduces | Reduces | Reduces |
| Impermeability | Improves | Improves | Improves |
| Sulfate resistance | Improves | Improves | — |
| Chloride diffusion | Reduces | Reduces | Reduces |
| Alkali-aggregate reaction | Prevents/retards if content sufficient | Prevents/retards | Prevents/retards |

### Selection guidance (Cement & Concrete SA)

| Application | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Conventional structural concrete, non-aggressive | Any SANS 50197-1 cement; CEM I + 50 % GGBS or + 30 % FA are extensively used in SA |
| Large placements, heat control | Extender content above 50 % GGBS or 30 % FA |
| Structural precast, high early strength without steam curing | 42,5R or higher, low extender content |
| Precast bricks, blocks, pavers | Choose on economy provided early handling strength is achieved |
| High-strength concrete | 42,5N or higher; ~8 % SF common; superplasticiser essential |
| Floors, roads, pavements with sawn joints | Extender ≤ 30 %, class 42,5N or higher |
| Reinforced concrete, marine | 50 % GGBS, or 10 % SF, or 40 % GGBS + 10 % SF, or 30 % FA |
| Alkali-reactive aggregate | ≥ 40 % GGBS, or ≥ 20 % FA, or ≥ 15 % SF |
| Sulfate exposure | CEM I with C₃A below ~9–10 %; consider SR cements; ≥ 30 % FA should improve resistance |

**Hot, dry site note.** In a BSh climate the extender choice is doing double duty: it reduces the rate of heat generation (fewer thermal cracks in slabs poured under a 35 °C sun) and it improves the pore structure and impermeability of concrete that will spend its life dry with episodic severe wetting. The trade-off is slower early strength — plan formwork striking and traffic times accordingly, and do not compensate by adding water.

## 3. Aggregates — SANS 1083

Classification by size: **fine aggregate** is material of which more than 90 % by mass passes the 4.75 mm sieve and is retained on the 75 µm sieve; **coarse aggregate** is retained on the 4.75 mm sieve.

| Class | Description |
|---|---|
| Natural | Natural origin, mechanical processing only; natural gravels, crushed rock |
| Manufactured | Result of an industrial process involving thermal or other modification |

### What matters on a Namibian site

- **Sand is the variable.** Northern Namibian sands are often fine, single-sized and wind-rounded, which gives poor grading, poor cohesion and high water demand. Test the fineness modulus and grading before designing a mix. Where the sand lacks fines, lime in the mortar (within the limits in `03`) or a plasticiser will recover workability without adding water.
- **Fineness modulus** is a single-number summary of grading (sum of cumulative percentages retained on the standard sieve series ÷ 100). It is useful for *consistency control* between deliveries but does not uniquely describe a grading — two very different sands can share an FM.
- **Deleterious content** — clay lumps, organic matter, salt — is the usual reason a pit sand fails. Salt-contaminated sand near pans is a reinforcement-corrosion risk.
- **Thermal expansion of the aggregate** governs the thermal movement of the concrete: quartzite and chert are high (roughly 9.5–13 ×10⁻⁶/°C), granite and dolerite lower (roughly 6.8–9.5 ×10⁻⁶/°C). In a high-diurnal-range climate this matters for long unjointed slabs.

## 4. Admixtures

| Type | Typical dosage per 100 kg cementitious | Principal effect | Cautions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plasticiser (water reducer) | 200–450 mℓ | ~10 % water reduction at equal workability, or increased slump | Many contain a retarder; overdosing causes problems; increases shrinkage and creep when used to raise workability |
| Superplasticiser (high-range) | 750–2 500 mℓ | 15–25 % water reduction; flowing concrete | Effect lasts 30 min to 6 h; add just before placing; needs a designed mix; high unit cost |
| Air entrainer | 50–150 mℓ | Freeze-thaw resistance; workability in harsh/lean mixes; reduces bleeding | ~1 % air ≈ 5 % strength loss; needs dispensers and monitoring; dose response varies with cement, sand and grading |
| Accelerator (chloride) | 500–2 000 mℓ | Rapid set and early strength | **Never** in reinforced, prestressed, steam-cured or water-retaining concrete |
| Accelerator (non-chloride) | 500–2 000 mℓ | Rapid set and early strength | Overdosing can cause instant set and equipment damage; works better at low temperature |
| Retarder | 150–500 mℓ | Longer setting time, slower early strength | Useful for hot-weather placing and long haul times |

Admixtures are never a substitute for mix design, workmanship or good materials.

## 5. Practical mix proportions

From Cement & Concrete SA, using common cement complying with SANS 50197. Stone 19 mm or 26 mm.

### Concrete (large batches, per 100 kg cement = 2 × 50 kg bags)

| Grade description | Typical use | Sand | Stone | Per m³ of concrete |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low strength | House foundations | 3½ barrows | 3½ barrows | 290 kg cement + 0,65 m³ sand + 0,65 m³ stone |
| Medium strength | House floors, footpaths, driveways | 2½ barrows | 2½ barrows | 385 kg cement + 0,62 m³ sand + 0,62 m³ stone |
| High strength | Precast, heavy-duty floors | 2 barrows | 2 barrows | 460 kg cement + 0,60 m³ sand + 0,60 m³ stone |

Small-batch volumetric ratios (cement : sand : stone) are 1 : 3½ : 3½, 1 : 2½ : 2½ and 1 : 2 : 2 respectively.

### External plaster (SANS 2001-EM1)

| Binder | Sand per 100 kg | Per 100 m² at 15 mm |
|---|---|---|
| Common cement SANS 50197 | 5 barrows | 700 kg cement + 2,25 m³ sand |
| Masonry cement SANS 50413 class MC 22,5X or MC 12,5 (**not** MC 12,5X) | 4 barrows | 850 kg cement + 2,25 m³ sand |

> ⚠️ These volumetric batches do **not** guarantee any particular strength. Where a strength is specified, do trial mixes and test cubes at an accredited laboratory.

## 6. Ready-mix versus site batching

| | Ready-mix | Site-batched |
|---|---|---|
| Quality control | Batched by mass, documented, plant QC | Volumetric, operator-dependent |
| Strength consistency | High | Variable; cube results scatter |
| Practicality in northern Namibia | Available around Windhoek, Walvis Bay, Swakopmund and the larger northern towns; **haul time is the constraint** — concrete must be discharged within the specified time, which in hot weather is short | The default on remote sites |
| Cost | Higher unit rate, lower waste and labour | Lower unit rate, higher waste, plant hire, labour |
| When to insist on ready-mix | Structural elements with specified strength and durability, large continuous pours, anything an engineer must certify | |
| When site batching is acceptable | Blinding, mass fill, non-structural slabs on grade, small foundation pours | |

If site batching structural concrete is unavoidable: batch cement by the bag (never by volume), use a weigh-batched or at minimum a consistently calibrated volumetric method for aggregate, control water by slump not by eye, and cube-test.

**Hot weather placing rules that matter here:** shade the aggregate stockpiles, keep the mixing water cool, place early morning or at night, use a retarder, and cure without interruption for the specified period. Plastic shrinkage cracking in a dry wind is the single most common concrete defect on a Namibian site.

## 7. Concrete products

| Product | Typical southern African form | Governing standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Masonry units (bricks, solid and hollow blocks) | 390×190×190, 390×140×190, 290×90×90, 190×90×90 | SANS 1215 | See `03_masonry-units.md` |
| Paving blocks | 200×100×60/80 rectangular; interlocking shapes | SANS 1058 | 60 mm for pedestrian/light vehicle, 80 mm for heavy |
| Kerbs and channels | Various | SANS 927 | |
| Precast lintels | Prestressed, typically 110/140/190 mm wide, lengths in 300 mm increments | Manufacturer design | Composite action with the masonry above is usually assumed — check propping and bearing requirements |
| Concrete roof tiles | Interlocking (Double Roman, Modern) and plain | SANS 542 | See `06_roofing-and-cladding.md` |
| Precast slabs / rib-and-block | Proprietary systems | Manufacturer/engineer | Reduces water and formwork on remote sites |

Concrete products for a remote site have a hidden advantage: they are made at a plant with quality control, they arrive at a known strength, and they need only mortar and labour on site. The disadvantage is freight — they are heavy and low-value per tonne, so the economics collapse beyond a few hundred kilometres. This is exactly the trade-off that makes on-site block making viable in northern Namibia when volumes are large enough.
