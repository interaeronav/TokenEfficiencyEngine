# South African suppliers and cross-border purchasing

**Summary.** South Africa is the source of almost everything a Namibian project cannot buy locally: boards, joinery hardware, specialist chemicals, technical membranes, glass, sanitaryware, and any imported fitting. Because both countries are in the **Southern African Customs Union**, there is **no customs duty** on goods moving between them — but **import VAT is still payable in Namibia**, and the documentation and freight are real work. This file maps the retail and trade supplier landscape in South Africa and sets out how cross-border purchasing actually works.

## Key facts

- **Namibia is a member of SACU** (with Botswana, Lesotho, South Africa and Eswatini) and **customs duties are not levied on intra-SACU trade**.
- **Import VAT is payable on all imports for home consumption in Namibia**, subject to certain exemptions, at **15 %**.
- Import VAT is calculated on the **greater of FOB value plus 10 %, or market value**.
- Payment may be **deferred to the 20th day of the month following importation** via a registered import VAT account with NamRA.
- Import VAT is deductible as input tax **only if** the import was in furtherance of a taxable activity **and** the importer holds the required documentation (stamped customs entries).
- Namibian VAT registration is compulsory above **N$1 million** taxable supplies in any 12-month period; voluntary registration is possible above **N$200 000**.
- Penalties on outstanding VAT or import VAT: **10 % per month or part month**, plus interest at the prime lending rate.
- South African VAT is **15 %**. (The proposed 2025 increase was reversed; 15 % remains the standard rate.)

**How to use them.** For a Namibian project, the SA retail chains are useful for three things: (1) **price discovery** — Builders and Leroy Merlin publish prices online, which gives you a benchmark before you ask a Namibian merchant to quote; (2) **specification research** — product ranges, datasheets and dimensions; (3) **actual purchase** of items with no Namibian equivalent. For bulk materials, buying in South Africa and freighting is almost never economic against a Namibian merchant.

**These are the classic Namibian long-lead items.** Joinery hardware is low-mass, high-value and highly specific — exactly the profile that suits air freight or courier and rewards ordering early. Two rules: order the hardware **before** the joinery is manufactured, not after (the hardware determines the cutting list); and order **spares** — a single failed Blum hinge in Okongo is a six-week problem if you have no spare.

## 5. How cross-border purchasing works, NA ← ZA

### The tax position

| Element | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Customs duty** | **Nil** on goods originating in SACU. Namibia and South Africa are both SACU members and customs duties are not levied on intra-SACU trade |
| **Third-country goods** | Goods originating outside SACU pay the **SACU Common External Tariff**, whether they enter via South Africa or directly |
| **South African VAT on export** | A properly documented export from South Africa is zero-rated for SA VAT. In practice a supplier will only zero-rate if the goods are exported by an approved route with correct documentation; otherwise they will charge 15 % SA VAT and you will be paying tax twice |
| **Namibian import VAT** | **15 %**, on the greater of FOB + 10 % or market value. Payable on all imports for home consumption, subject to certain exemptions (technical assistance agreements, donations to the state, goods whose local supply is zero-rated) |
| **Deferment** | Import VAT can be deferred to the **20th day of the month following importation** via an import VAT account registered with **NamRA** |
| **Input tax recovery** | Import VAT is recoverable as input tax **only if** the import furthers a taxable activity and the importer holds **stamped customs entries** |
| **Temporary imports** | Surety — a provisional payment, bank or insurance guarantee — is required by Namibian Customs on most temporary imports, to cover import VAT and any customs duty |
| **AEO status** | Namibia Customs operates an **Authorised Economic Operator** scheme under the Customs and Excise Act 1998, giving reduced security requirements, bonded warehouse and temporary import benefits, and faster clearing including pre-clearance on AsycudaWorld |
| **Environmental levies** | Namibia levies environmental charges on certain imported or locally manufactured products including vehicles' CO₂, certain light bulbs, certain lubricants, pneumatic tyres, disposable batteries and certain plastic bags |

### The practical route

1. **Decide who imports.** Either your Namibian entity is the importer of record (you handle the clearance, you recover the import VAT) or the supplier delivers DDP (they handle it, you pay a margin and you may not be able to recover the VAT). For anything of consequence, be the importer of record and register an import VAT account.
2. **Get a clearing agent** at the border post you will use. This is not optional for anything beyond a courier parcel. The agent prepares the customs entry, and it is the **stamped entry** that lets you recover the import VAT later.
3. **Assemble the documentation.** In general practice this means: commercial invoice showing goods, quantities, unit prices and total value; packing list; transport document (bill of lading / air waybill / road consignment note); proof of SACU origin where preference is claimed; import/export declarations; and any product-specific certification. *(This documentation list reflects general trade practice and was not verified against a NamRA or SARS primary source in this research — confirm with your clearing agent.)*
4. **Get the HS classification right.** Even where duty is nil, misclassification causes delays and can attract penalties, and it determines whether an excise or environmental levy applies.
5. **Choose the transport mode by value density.** Bulk and heavy: road freight, consolidated, groupage or full load. Low-mass, high-value, urgent: courier or air. Nothing about southern African cross-border road freight is fast — build in the border time.
6. **Insure in transit.** Materials are damaged and pilfered on this route. The insurance costs less than one replacement roof sheet consignment.
7. **Plan the last leg.** Getting goods to Windhoek is the easy part. Windhoek to Okongo is a separate arrangement and a separate cost — see `11_procurement-and-pricing.md`.

### Where cross-border buying makes sense — and where it does not

| Buy in South Africa | Buy in Namibia |
|---|---|
| Joinery and architectural hardware | Cement, aggregate, blocks |
| Specialist chemicals, membranes, two-pack coatings | Standard timber and roof sheeting |
| Board decors not stocked by Megaboards | Common rebar and mesh |
| Specific sanitaryware, taps, electrical fittings | General hardware and consumables |
| Anything imported that a Namibian merchant would import anyway (buy it one link up the chain) | Paint (tinting is available locally) |
| Tools and plant not carried by Cymot | Anything you might need again in a hurry |

The governing heuristic: **cross-border buying converts money into time risk.** It saves money on high-margin specialist items and costs you weeks. Use it for what cannot be bought locally and for what you can order far in advance — and never for something on the critical path that you have not ordered yet.
