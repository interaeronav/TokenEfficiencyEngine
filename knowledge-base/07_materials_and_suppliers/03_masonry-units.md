# Masonry units — clay, concrete, calcium silicate and earth

**Summary.** Southern African masonry is dominated by two standards: **SANS 227** for burnt clay masonry units and **SANS 1215** for concrete masonry units, with **SANS 285** covering calcium silicate bricks. The clay classification (FBX, FBS, FBA, NFP, NFX, and the E engineering designation) describes *appearance and application*, not strength; strength is separately specified. Concrete units are classified by nominal compressive strength from 3,5 to 21,0 MPa. This file gives the classes, sizes, strengths, tolerances, absorption behaviour, mortar requirements, and the producer landscape — plus an honest assessment of stabilised earth blocks in northern Namibia.

## Key facts

- A **brick** is a burnt clay masonry unit with length ≤ 300 mm, width ≤ 130 mm and height ≤ 120 mm. Anything outside that is a **block** (SANS 227:2007 definition). CMA gives the concrete-unit equivalent: a block has length 300–650 mm, or width 130–300 mm, or height 120–300 mm.
- The **imperial brick** — 222 × 106 × 73 mm, mass 2,4–3,3 kg — is still the most commonly manufactured size in South Africa.
- Modular wall thicknesses under SANS 10400 are **90, 140 and 190 mm**; permissible wall thicknesses are 90, 110, 140, 190 and 230 mm.
- Clay brick compressive strength ranges from about **7 MPa (NFP)** to **over 50 MPa** (Face Brick Extra and engineering units). Standard testing is on a sample of 12.
- Clay bricks should be **wetted before laying**; concrete bricks and blocks **should not be**.
- Burnt clay units undergo **irreversible moisture expansion**, generally not more than **0,20 %**.
- Structural clay units with an initial rate of absorption above **1,8 kg/m²·min** should be moistened before laying to bring the rate to between **0,7 and 1,8 kg/m²·min** (SANS 10164-1).

## 1. Burnt clay masonry units — SANS 227

### Classes

| Class | Name | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| **FBX** | Face Brick Extra | Selected or produced for durability and a **high degree of uniformity** of size, shape and colour |
| **FBS** | Face Brick Standard | Selected or produced for durability and **uniformity of size and shape** |
| **FBA** | Face Brick Aesthetic | Selected or produced for durability and **aesthetic effect deriving from non-uniformity** of size, shape or colour |
| **NFP** | Non-Facing Plastered | Suitable for general building work **that is to be plastered** |
| **NFX** | Non-Facing Extra | Suitable plastered or unplastered for general building work **below DPC, under damp conditions, or below ground level**, where durability rather than aesthetics governs |
| **E** | Engineering unit | Any class produced for structural/loadbearing purposes to an **agreed compressive strength**; designated by `E` plus the nominal strength in MPa, e.g. **FBSE21** |
| **PA / PB** | Clay pavers (SANS 1575) | PA: high uniformity, work-size length:width ≈ 1:1, 2:1 or 3:1. PB: durable and uniform in size and shape |

> ⚠️ The class letters describe **appearance and application**, not strength. A specification that says "FBS brick" without a strength has not specified a structural material. For loadbearing work use the E designation or state a required compressive strength.

|---|---|---|---|
| *Individual units* | | | |
| FBX | ±5 | ±3 | ±3 |
| FBS | ±7 | ±4 | ±4 |
| FBA, NFP, NFX | no requirement | — | — |

| FBS | ±3,5 | ±2 | ±2 |
| FBA | no requirement | — | — |

**Warpage** (across the length or across diagonal corners): FBX individual units not to exceed 5 mm, with warpage exceeding 3 mm in not more than three units. FBS and engineering: individual not to exceed 5 mm. FBA and NFP: no requirement.

### Efflorescence limits (number of units in a sample exhibiting each degree)

| Grade | Class | Slight | Moderate | Heavy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Special | FBS, FBX, FBA | 20 | — | — |
| Special | NFP, NFX | 10 | 10 | — |
| Normal | FBS, FBX, FBA | 10 | 10 | — |
| Normal | NFP, NFX | — | 10 | 10 |

Degrees: **Nil** (no perceptible deposit) → **Slight** (very thin deposit on edges only) → **Moderate** (heavier, no powdering or flaking) → **Heavy** (thick, large area, no powdering or flaking) → **Serious** (deposit that has caused powdering or flaking).

### Exposure zones for face brick selection

| Zone | Severity | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Protected | All inland areas more than 30 km from the coastline |
| **2** | Moderate | The 30 km coastal zone, excluding the sea spray zone |
| **3** | Severe | Sea spray zone; exposed coastal headlands; the 15 km coastal zone from Mtunzini northwards to the Mozambique border including Richards Bay; **and the coastal belt of Namibia** |
| **4** | Very severe | Areas such as **Walvis Bay** where sea mist, high ground water tables, soluble soil sulfates and rapid temperature change combine; and industrial areas with high acid or alkaline discharge |

**[NA]** This is the single most important masonry table for Namibian work. An inland site in Ohangwena is **Zone 1**. Walvis Bay is **Zone 4** — the harshest category in the southern African scheme. Certain facing bricks are not suited to external exposure in Zones 3 and 4; the manufacturer must state the recommended zone for each product.

## 2. Concrete masonry units — SANS 1215

### Recommended nominal work sizes (SANS 1215 Appendix F, Table F-1)

| Length (mm) | Width (mm) | Height (mm) |
|---|---|---|
| 190 | 90 | 90 |
| 290 | 90 | 90 |
| 390 | 90 | 190 |
| 390 | 190 | 190 |

Modular planning assumes a nominal 10 mm joint. Non-modular sizes (e.g. 222 × 90 × 73, made to satisfy SANS 10400's 90 mm minimum while retaining the imperial length) **do not bond well** without cutting — English and Flemish bond and square brick piers become impossible, because the bonding principle requires unit length = 2 × width + one joint.

**Solid vs hollow:** a *solid* unit has cavities not exceeding **25 %** of gross volume; a *hollow* unit has cavities exceeding 25 % but not exceeding **60 %**.

### Compressive strength (SANS 1215 Table 2) — based on gross area

| Nominal compressive strength, MPa | Average for 5 units*, MPa min | Individual unit, MPa min |
|---|---|---|
| 3,5 | 4,0 | 3,0 |
| 7,0 | 8,0 | 5,5 |
| 10,5 | 11,5 | 8,5 |
| 14,0 | 15,5 | 11,0 |
| 21,0 | 23,5 | 17,0 |

\* For units of overall length 290 mm or less, an average of 12 units is taken.

### Tolerances on work sizes (SANS 1215 Table 1)

| Dimension | Tolerance |
|---|---|
| Length | +2 / −4 mm |
| Width | ±3 mm (±10 mm for FUA face unit aesthetic) |
| Height | ±3 mm |

### Other properties

- **Drying shrinkage** should not exceed **0,06 %**. Expansion on re-wetting should not exceed the drying shrinkage value by more than **0,02 %**.
- **Water absorption is not specified in SANS 1215** and is not regarded as a significant characteristic under South African weather conditions; it measures a laboratory quantity, not porosity or permeability.
- **Initial rate of absorption** is generally not a significant property of concrete units (unlike clay).
- Units made from slag, clinker or burnt-clay-brick aggregates should be checked for soundness so that pop-outs do not exceed the specified amount.

## 3. Cement stock bricks and maxi bricks

Cement stock bricks are the everyday non-facing unit of southern African construction: solid concrete units made on a static or mobile block machine, usually to the imperial footprint or a maxi size, and usually plastered.

| Common trade name | Typical size (mm) | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Cement stock brick | 222 × 106 × 73 | General plastered walling, matches imperial clay coursing |
| Maxi brick | 290 × 90 × 90 | Fewer units per m², faster laying, 90 mm modular wall |
| Maxi brick | 290 × 140 × 90 | 140 mm wall |
| Hollow block | 390 × 140 × 190 | Loadbearing and infill walling |
| Hollow block | 390 × 190 × 190 | 190 mm loadbearing walling; reinforcement can be grouted in cores |

Sizes and strengths vary between manufacturers — always ask for the actual work size and the SANS 1215 nominal strength class, not the trade name. Units not bearing the SABS mark certifying compliance with SANS 1215 should be tested before use.

**On-site block making** is a genuine option in northern Namibia for volume projects: a mobile machine, local sand, imported cement and imported stone can produce units at the point of use and cut the freight cost of the heaviest, lowest-value material in the building. The failure modes to control are curing (blocks must be cured, not just stacked in the sun), consistency of mix, and dimensional tolerance. The Cement & Concrete SA leaflet *How to make concrete bricks and blocks* is the working reference.

## 4. Calcium silicate bricks — SANS 285

Calcium silicate (sand-lime) bricks are made from sand, lime and water autoclaved under steam pressure. Standard engineering practice specifies that **calcium silicate bricks shall comply with SANS 285** and that they shall be **laid slightly wetted but not saturated**. They are dimensionally very accurate and available in pale colours, but they have higher drying shrinkage than clay and are less common in southern Africa than clay or concrete. They are not widely produced in Namibia and would be an import item.

## 5. Adobe and stabilised earth blocks

**Relevance in northern Namibia.** The traditional building culture of Owambo and Kavango uses earth, timber and thatch, and the raw material for earth block production is present on almost every site. Cement-stabilised soil blocks (CSSB) and interlocking stabilised soil blocks (ISSB, e.g. the Hydraform system) are an attractive answer to the freight problem: the aggregate is the site, and only the stabiliser (typically 5–10 % cement by mass) is imported.

Honest assessment:

| Factor | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Material cost | Very low — soil is free, cement is the only significant input |
| Freight | Almost eliminated for the walling material |
| Thermal | Excellent thermal mass; well suited to the large diurnal swing |
| Structural | Adequate for single-storey loadbearing with correct stabilisation and detailing; needs engineering input for anything more |
| Durability | **The critical risk.** Earth walls fail at the base from rising damp and splash, and at the top from rain ingress. "Good boots and a good hat" — a raised, waterproofed plinth and a generous roof overhang — is not optional |
| Standards | **No SANS product standard for stabilised earth blocks was located in this research.** Agrément South Africa certification exists for some proprietary systems; verify the certificate for the specific system |
| Regulatory acceptance | Uncertain in Namibia; requires local authority engagement early |
| Labour | Labour-intensive, which may be an advantage where employment is a project objective |
| Soil suitability | Must be tested — needs a specific clay/silt/sand balance. Pure Kalahari sand will not stabilise well without added fines |

> ⚠️ Do not specify stabilised earth blocks without (a) a soil test, (b) a wet/dry strength test on trial blocks, and (c) confirmation that the local authority will accept them. Where the system is proprietary, obtain the current Agrément certificate.

## 6. Mortar for masonry — SANS 2001-CM1

| Class | Use | Min compressive strength at 28 d (preliminary lab / works test), MPa | Sand per 50 kg common cement 32,5 or 42,5 | Sand per 50 kg masonry cement 22,5X |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | Highly stressed masonry — high-strength structural units, multi-storey loadbearing, reinforced masonry | 14,5 / 10 | 130 ℓ | 80 ℓ |
| **II** | Normal loadbearing, parapets, balustrades, retaining structures, freestanding and garden walls, walls exposed to possible severe dampness | 7 / 5 | 200 ℓ | 130 ℓ |

Per m³ of mortar: Class I — 10,0 bags common cement (32,5 or 42,5) + 1,25 m³ sand, or 13,5 bags masonry cement 22,5X + 1,15 m³ sand. Class II — 7 bags + 1,35 m³ sand, or 10 bags masonry cement + 1,25 m³ sand.

- **Lime:** maximum 10 kg per 50 kg cement for Class I; maximum 25 kg per 50 kg for Class II. Mix proportions need no adjustment; yield increases by about 5 %. **Do not use lime with masonry cement.**
- Use lime where the sand lacks fines or is single-sized — exactly the northern Namibian case.
- 1 m³ of mortar lays about **3 700 bricks** of 190 × 90 × 90 mm in a 90–110 mm single-leaf wall, before wastage. Allow 10–20 % wastage.
- One person laying can manage a little over **60 bricks an hour** (a weekend-builder figure; trade output is higher).

## 7. Producers

### South Africa **[ZA]**

| Producer | Type | Notes |

| **Corobrik** | Clay face brick and pavers; also concrete bricks and blocks | The largest SA clay brick producer; national factory network; publishes a face brick classification aligned to SANS 227 |

### Namibia **[NA]**

Namibian masonry supply is dominated by **concrete units made locally** rather than by clay brick, because there is no large-scale clay brick industry comparable to South Africa's and because freighting clay brick from South Africa is uneconomic for anything but face work.

- Concrete blocks and cement stock bricks are made by numerous small and medium producers in and around Windhoek, Okahandja, Otjiwarongo, Tsumeb, Ondangwa, Oshakati and Rundu, and are stocked by all the major merchants (Pupkewitz Megabuild, Build it, Cashbuild, BUCO, Agrimark — see `09_suppliers-namibia.md`).
- Clay face brick used in Namibia is generally imported from South African producers.
- **The producer list for Namibia could not be verified from primary company websites in this research and is therefore not tabulated here.** Establish the actual local block supplier by asking the merchant branch nearest the site which local yards they buy from, and test the units against SANS 1215 before accepting them for loadbearing work.
